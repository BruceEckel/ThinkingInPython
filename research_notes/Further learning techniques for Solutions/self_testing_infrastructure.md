# Self-checking infrastructure for programming exercises in a book-plus-repository setting

Scope note for the report writer.
The question is what lets a reader check an attempt before opening the solution,
when there is no server and no grader:
provided tests, output oracles, koan-style progressions, type-checker feedback, and the practice of the main self-study projects.
Worked examples, completion problems, and the six exercise formats were covered in the prior round and are left out here.
Most of the controlled evidence is on CS1 novices and is flagged as such;
the audience for this book is experienced programmers meeting a new idea, so each finding carries a note on transfer.
Several of the primary papers (Edwards 2004, Buffardi and Edwards 2015, Karavirta 2006) sit behind the ACM paywall,
so for those the notes report what the abstract and secondary sources say and mark the rest as a gap.

## Key question 1: Does providing tests with an exercise help, and what does it do to behavior?

### Takeaway
The best-documented effect is that feedback from a test suite raises completion and assignment scores, and that students lean on it;
the evidence that it raises *learning* (a later test without the feedback) is thin, mixed, and almost all on CS1 novices.
Where the tests are the only oracle, students "learn the grader": they submit until green, write fewer tests of their own, and the behavior falls by half once the grader stops being free.

### Cited Findings
- Edwards' Web-CAT approach (students write their own JUnit tests, the grader scores test validity, coverage against a reference suite, and style) was piloted in a junior-level class of 59 students; the OOPSLA 2003 poster reports "students produced 45% fewer bugs per thousand lines of code using this approach", citing Edwards' own 2003 ICEIS paper, and that students "tested their programs more thoroughly" and preferred the approach. This is a pilot, reported by the tool's author, with no control described in the poster. — [Edwards, Teaching Software Testing: Automatic Grading Meets Test-first Coding, OOPSLA 2003 poster](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/stephen-edwards/teaching-testing.pdf)
- A later comparative review describes the same study as 59 TDD students against 59 non-TDD students on the same assignments and grader, with 45% fewer defects in the TDD group; a separate Edwards and Allowatt paper reports a 28% average reduction in bugs per thousand lines in upper-division courses, so the size varies by setting. — [search summary citing arXiv 1711.05082 and Web-CAT materials](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.05082); [VT news release, 2007](https://news.vt.edu/articles/2007/01/2007-31.html)
- Edwards' SIGCSE 2004 paper argues that "introductory computer science students rely on a trial and error approach to fixing errors and debugging for too long" and that making students demonstrate correctness through their own tests builds the hypothesis-forming habit he calls reflection-in-action; SIGCSE ranked it 7th in its top ten CS-education papers of 50 years. Full results are paywalled. — [ACM DOI 10.1145/971300.971312](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F971300.971312); [ACM top-ten announcement](https://www.acm.org/media-center/2019/march/sigcse-top-10-papers)
- Fridge's 2014 dissertation tested the *other* arrangement, closest to a shipped test file: 144 introductory Java students at the University of West Florida used Web-CAT with researcher-supplied tests (students wrote none) on three projects, grouped by usage (none, low, high). Average project performance rose significantly in the high-usage group, but on Kember's reflective-thinking survey the one significant difference was that high-usage students reported *lower* understanding. CS1 only. — [Fridge dissertation record](https://ircommons.uwf.edu/esploro/outputs/doctoral/An-Investigation-of-the-Impact-of/99380090747406600)
- Baniassad et al. (SIGCSE 2021) measured autograder overreliance in a third-year software engineering course (247 students, 137 pairs in the treated term; 42 instructor unit tests per deliverable). Without penalties, teams called the grader 8.96 times on average, 46% regressed at least once, and 8.5% of submissions caused a regression. With a "regression penalty" (half the size of any grade drop between submissions, charged permanently), calls fell to 4.62 (−48.4%), teams regressing fell to 6.5%, regressions per team fell 89.5%, and the median grade moved from 97% to 96% (mean 92% to 82%). Of 241 survey respondents, 86% said the penalty changed how they worked; 33% reported more testing of their own, 68% more caution, 20% stress. One student: "In previous classes with autograders I would just make changes and test using the autograder rather than write my own exhaustive test suite." The authors still conclude students "tweak and submit as often as they can" and "end up learning the grader, not learning the concepts." Not CS1. — [Baniassad et al., Stop the (Autograder) Insanity, SIGCSE 2021](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rtholmes/papers/sigcse_2021_baniassad.pdf)
- An observational study across five US community colleges (199 students, 15 sections, Python, unlimited submissions) found an average of 66 submissions per student and 64 feedback checks; 28% of feedback pages were never opened, 56% once, 16% more than once. Feedback checks correlate with project score at r = 0.273 (p < 0.001), but with hours spent at r = 0.497, so the authors warn the score link may be time on task. When a submission that fell short was followed by another on the same task, the next scored higher 38.46% of the time after a feedback check versus 33.77% without (Fisher's exact p = 0.0063). CS1 only, correlational. — [Auto-grader Feedback Utilization, arXiv 2507.14235](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.14235v1)
- Gabbay and Cohen (EDM 2022) compared a Python MOOC's learners with automated test feedback and unlimited resubmission against a control without it (N = 4652 active learners): feedback use went with higher engagement and performance, and "no difference was found between groups" on sense of learning or intention fulfilment. No effect sizes on the record page. — [Gabbay and Cohen, EDM 2022](https://zenodo.org/record/6853125)
- A SIGCSE 2023 study at Stony Brook (abstract only) reports that immediate autograder feedback went with better grades and self-efficacy in introductory courses and that students "did not lose the ability to develop independent testing skills". CS1. — [SIGCSE 2023 listing](https://sigcse2023.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2023-papers/6/Studying-The-Impact-Of-Auto-Graders-Giving-Immediate-Feedback-In-Programming-Assignme)
- Marmoset (Maryland) was designed around the overreliance problem: a student who passes the public tests may spend a token (3 tokens, each regenerating 24 hours after use) to run the instructor's "release tests", and sees only the count of failures and the names of the first two failed tests. The stated purpose is "to start early and implement their own test cases". — [Marmoset documentation](https://theory.cpe.ku.ac.th/~jittat/marmoset-docs/marmoset_introduction.html); [Marmoset OOPSLA 2006 paper](https://www.cs.jhu.edu/~daveho/pubs/oopsla2006-marmoset.pdf)
- Karavirta, Korhonen, and Malmi (Computer Science Education 2006) studied resubmissions in automatic assessment and, with Malmi et al. 2005, concluded that the number of resubmissions "should be carefully controlled to avoid aimless trial-and-error problem solving"; later teaching slides from the group recommend "a smallish limit, unless the basic task can be parameterized". Full text paywalled. — [Malmi, Using automatic and semi-automatic feedback (slides)](https://www.4tu.nl/cee/events/archive-2017-and-before/cdio_conference/using-automatic-and-semi-automatic-feedback-malmi.pdf)
- Desai, Janzen, and Savage's 2008 survey of TDD in academia found, per a secondary summary, that "some aspects of internal and external quality saw improvement but the results for productivity were mixed" and that student experience was a moderating factor; Janzen's dissertation notes TDD advocates "rely primarily on anecdotal evidence". — [Janzen publication list](https://users.csc.calpoly.edu/~djanzen/pubs/index.html); [Helsinki thesis summarizing the survey](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/28435ba6-f068-4f28-983a-47d703536365/content)
- Keuning, Jeuring, and Heeren's systematic review (ACM TOCE 2018, 101 tools) found tool feedback concentrates on "knowledge about mistakes" and seldom on how to fix them or what the next step is. — [Keuning et al. record](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/a-systematic-literature-review-of-automated-feedback-generation-f/)
- Wisniewski, Zierer, and Hattie's 2020 meta-analysis of feedback (435 studies, 994 effect sizes, N > 61,000) gives an overall d = 0.48 with large heterogeneity; the effect "depends heavily on the information content the feedback conveys", and is larger for cognitive and motor outcomes than for motivation. This is general education, not programming. — [Wisniewski et al., Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/pdf)

### Inferences
- For a self-study reader with no grade at stake, the "excessive submissions" problem has no penalty lever, so the design choices that remain are (a) what the test reveals on failure and (b) whether the test is the reader's only oracle. The Baniassad survey quotes suggest that a visible full suite becomes the reader's test suite, which is fine when the goal is to use a construct and bad when the goal is to think about correctness.
- The Fridge result (scores up, self-reported understanding down with supplied tests) and the MOOC null on sense of learning are the two data points closest to "ship a test file with the exercise"; both are CS1, both are weak designs, and neither shows a learning gain. The repeated finding is that supplied tests raise completion, not understanding.
- Nothing here is on experienced programmers. The overreliance finding (Baniassad) is the one study on third-year students and it shows the same behavior as CS1, so the behavior transfers; whether it costs learning in the experienced audience is unmeasured.

### Gaps
- No study found that compares exercises shipped with tests against the same exercises without tests on a delayed learning measure for anyone past CS1.
- Edwards 2004's quantitative results, Buffardi and Edwards 2015, and Karavirta 2006 are paywalled; only abstracts and secondary citations were reachable.
- No effect sizes (d or g) for immediate automated feedback in programming were found; the programming studies report significance tests and correlations.

## Key question 2: Feedback granularity (full visible suite, hidden tests, one failing assertion at a time, graduated feedback)

### Takeaway
There is no controlled study of the koan "one failure at a time" progression; its support is practice (Ruby Koans and its ports, Exercism's skipped-test convention, Rustlings' ordered watch mode) plus general findings that less-than-complete feedback (conceptual feedback, Marmoset's two-name limit) pushes students to form their own hypotheses.
Hidden tests reduce hard-coding in practice; no study was found that shows they improve learning over visible tests.

### Cited Findings
- Ruby Koans, the origin of programming koans, is "a list of failing test cases, where tiny pieces of code have to be filled in to make them pass", stepping up in difficulty; Python Koans "is a port of Edgecase's 'Ruby Koans'", run with `python contemplate_koans.py`, and most koans are fixed by replacing a `__` placeholder in an assert such as `self.assertEqual(__, 1+2)`, with some pre-written failing tests that require the learner to write code (the triangle exercise). The README frames the cycle as "red, green, refactor" and suggests recreating a scenario in the REPL when the expected value is unclear; an optional Sniffer watcher reruns on save. — [Python Koans README](https://github.com/gregmalcolm/python_koans); [Code Cop blog on koans](https://dcmpx.remotevs.com/org/code-cop/blog/PL/2015)
- Elixir Koans: `mix meditate` and "You should see the first failure"; "fill in the blanks to make the koans pass one by one"; "The autorunner will give you feedback each time you save." — [elixir-koans README](https://github.com/elixirkoans/elixir-koans)
- Exercism tracks ship a test file with every test but the first skipped, so a learner enables them one at a time: JavaScript uses `xtest` "to enable users to concentrate on one test at a time and enable one by one as they evolve the solution"; Elixir uses `@tag :pending` and `mix test --include pending`; Rust marks them `#[ignore]` and `cargo test -- --ignored` runs them; the online runner "runs all the tests every time". — [Exercism JavaScript track tests doc](https://exercism.org/docs/tracks/javascript/tests); [Exercism forum on skipping](https://forum.exercism.org/t/skipping-tests-in-the-8th-track/7395)
- Rustlings "walks you through the exercises in a predefined order" and "will rerun the current exercise automatically every time you change the exercise's file"; `h` shows the hint, `l` the done/pending list, and the list allows skipping ahead or resetting an exercise. — [Rustlings usage page](https://rustlings.rust-lang.org/usage)
- Marmoset's release tests show "only the number of release tests the submission failed and the names of the first two failed release tests"; course pages advise students to "write a test case that replicates that failure" before spending another token. — [Marmoset introduction](https://theory.cpe.ku.ac.th/~jittat/marmoset-docs/marmoset_introduction.html); [Waterloo course page](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~plragde/245/Marmoset.html)
- Codewars shows sample tests while training and hides the full submission suite until ATTEMPT; "many kata give vague information about failure without any useful details", so users print the inputs to debug. — [Codewars docs](https://docs.codewars.com/training/training-example)
- Gradescope and similar autograders support per-test visibility (hidden, after due date, visible); instructor rationale is that hidden tests "restrict students from getting the expected output through hard-coding" and prevent "overfit" to the test cases; Otter's integration can show "All tests passed" for the public tests while a hidden test fails. No study measuring a learning effect was found. — [Gradescope autograder specs](https://gradescope-autograders.readthedocs.io/en/latest/specs/); [Moodle forum thread](https://moodle.org/mod/forum/discuss.php?d=457640)
- Inquiry-based conceptual feedback (which reinforces a testing concept without naming the missing case) versus detailed coverage feedback, in two sophomore courses through the Testing Tutor platform: the conceptual-feedback students had higher coverage, fewer redundant tests, and higher programming grades. N and statistics are not on the abstract page. — [arXiv 2011.13004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13004)
- Marwan et al. (ICER 2019): next-step code hints with textual explanations "significantly improved immediate programming performance" (Study 2, randomized, 250 Mechanical Turk workers with no programming experience, block-based language), but performance on a later task without hints improved only when self-explanation prompts accompanied the hints; the authors conclude "prompts to self-explain are necessary in order for code hints to lead to learning." Novices only; the PDF extraction stopped before the Study 2 tables, so the test statistics are not reported here. — [Marwan et al., ICER 2019](https://isnap.csc.ncsu.edu/home/public/papers/MarwanICER2019.pdf)
- Keuning et al. note that feedback types have diversified and that newer techniques produce feedback "increasingly helpful for students", but their coding found most tools stop at identifying the mistake. — [Keuning et al. 2018](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/a-systematic-literature-review-of-automated-feedback-generation-f/)

### Inferences
- The one-at-a-time progression is a sequencing device, not a feedback-granularity device: each koan still shows a full assertion failure. Its claimed benefit (focus, a path through a topic) matches what Exercism and Rustlings adopted independently, which is practice consensus rather than evidence.
- The two studies that touch granularity (Testing Tutor, Marwan) point the same way: feedback that makes the reader explain or reason beats feedback that hands over the answer, on both performance and, for Marwan, retention. A test that prints "expected X, got Y" is the detailed end of that spectrum; a test name or a hint that names the concept is the conceptual end.
- For a static repo, hidden tests are impossible (the reader can open any file), so the practical equivalent is a split between a visible "smoke" assertion and a fuller suite kept in a separate file the reader is told to open only after their own check, which is the Codewars sample/attempt split enforced by convention rather than by the platform.

### Gaps
- No controlled study on koans, on one-failure-at-a-time sequencing, or on partial credit versus pass/fail feedback for self-study readers was found.
- No study comparing hidden and visible tests on a learning outcome was found; the sources are platform docs and instructor forum posts.

## Key question 3: Type checkers and linters as a feedback channel

### Takeaway
Type-checker-as-oracle exercises are established practice (Rustlings' compile-to-pass exercises, TypeScript type-challenges, Idris "type, define, refine"), and the research is on usability of the checker's messages and on typed holes as a working surface; no study was found that measures learning from "make it type-check" exercises against another format.

### Cited Findings
- Rustlings: "Most exercises contain an error that keeps them from compiling", and "Some exercises contain tests that need to pass for the exercise to be done"; the first error message in watch mode "is expected, since it describes the problem you're meant to solve." The project is "recommended in parallel to reading the official Rust book." — [Rustlings usage](https://rustlings.rust-lang.org/usage); [Rustlings README](https://github.com/rust-lang/rustlings)
- type-challenges (Anthony Fu) is "a collection of TypeScript type challenges with online judge"; solutions are written purely as types, each challenge carries test types, and a solution counts when the compiler accepts it in strict mode; the CLI generates a local `./playground` so the same check runs offline. — [type-challenges fork README](https://github.com/EdoTrotta/type-challenges); [The New Stack article](https://thenewstack.io/improve-your-typescript-skills-with-type-challenges/)
- Brady's *Type-Driven Development with Idris* teaches a "type, define, refine" workflow where the type is written first and "the compiler and type checker guide you to a working program." — [Manning liveBook chapter 1](https://livebook.manning.com/book/type-driven-development-with-idris/chapter-1)
- Crichton's "The Usability of Ownership" argues the borrow checker is "a notorious learning barrier" because it is sound and incomplete, so a diagnostic may mean a real bug or an analyzer limit, and learners must tell the two apart; the Rust Book teaches the rules without that distinction. — [Crichton, arXiv 2011.06171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06171)
- Ferrous Systems' practitioner piece says the Rust compiler "can work as your personal mentor when learning a core concept", because messages name the violated rule, the location, and a suggested fix. Opinion, not data. — [The compiler is your friend](https://ferrous-systems.com/blog/the-compiler-is-your-friend/)
- Hazel Tutor (SPLASH-E 2020, work in progress) is an editor service that, with the cursor on an empty hole, proposes next steps organized around a type-driven development strategy for novices. — [Hazel Tutor](https://2020.splashcon.org/details/hatra-2020-papers/10/Hazel-Tutor-Guiding-Novices-Through-Type-Driven-Development-Strategies)
- In a seven-student study of Hazel Deriver, students named typed holes a major strength because they could leave sub-goals unfinished and refine gradually; the domain was logic derivations, not programs. — [arXiv 2506.10781](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10781)
- Among novice Haskell programmers, about 18% of syntactically correct expressions contained type errors, concentrated in equality tests and if/then/else. — [Functional Baby Talk, arXiv 1805.05126](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1805.05126)

### Inferences
- The oracle form that transfers to a Python repo with `ty` is the Rustlings one: a file that fails `ty check` with a diagnostic the exercise names, which the reader edits until the check passes, with a second form where the exercise asks the reader to *cause* a specific diagnostic (an annotation that should reject a call). The book already quotes `ty` diagnostics and gates them (`check_quoted_diagnostics.py`), so the quoted diagnostic can double as the expected "output" of an exercise.
- Crichton's point carries over: a `ty` false positive (the book records several in `pyright_baseline.txt` and the `ty-gaps` memory) would break a "make it type-check" exercise, so each such exercise needs the checker version pinned and the expected diagnostic re-probed on upgrade, which the repo already does for quoted diagnostics.

### Gaps
- No study measuring learning from type-checker-oracle exercises was found, in any language.
- No documentation from type-challenges or Rustlings maintainers on which exercise shapes worked or failed was found.

## Key question 4: Expected-output oracles

### Takeaway
Seeing the program's output helps novices learn more than correctness feedback alone (one RCT), and asking learners to *predict* output before running beats tell-and-practice (one RCT, N = 121, beginners); printing the expected output in the book is the standard self-study oracle (Learn Python the Hard Way, Effective Python, Fluent Python's doctests), with no study found that compares a printed output oracle against a behavioral test.

### Cited Findings
- Leinonen, Hellas, and Edwards (ITiCSE 2023), a randomized controlled trial with novices on code-mimicking exercises: one group saw the program output plus correctness feedback, the other correctness feedback alone, measured by a code-reading pre/post test. For novices, seeing output gave larger learning gains; for more experienced students both groups gained and the difference was not strong. The authors recommend environments "encourage - or even require - running the code before allowing submitting the program for assessment." Full text is self-archived at Aalto (fetch returned 403); numbers are from the abstract. — [research.fi record](https://research.fi/en/results/publication/0675408423); [Aalto record](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/98f20d4c-24e4-4071-bb42-4cc55cc54b34)
- "Prediction versus production for teaching computer programming" (Learning & Instruction vol. 91, 2024): 121 college students with no coding experience, randomized to a predict condition (given code, predict output before being told how it works) or tell-and-practice; the predict group "showed greater learning and better non-cognitive outcomes", more positive emotional responses to R and its error messages, and rated the activity as less costly. Beginners only; authors and effect sizes not on the index record. — [EDS record](https://ubmr.hds.hebis.de/EdsRecord/ehh%252C176332717)
- PRIMM (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make) and Runestone's "Predict Before You Run" sections institutionalize the same step: write what you think it prints, run, mark every mismatch. — [Runestone FOPP 1.8](https://runestone.academy/ns/books/published/fopp/GeneralIntro/WPPredictBeforeYouRun.html); [PRIMM summary](https://faq.computersciencewiki.org/index.php/intro/article/primm)
- Learn Python the Hard Way follows every exercise with a "What You Should See" section, then "Study Drills" and "Common Student Questions" (52 exercises in the 3rd and 4th editions, 60 in the 5th); the method is "read them, type in the code, run it, fix your mistakes, and repeat", with copy-paste forbidden. — [InformIT listing, 4th edition TOC](https://www.informit.com/store/learn-python-3-the-hard-way-9780138270667)
- Effective Python's repository: "Each file is annotated with which example snippet it came from within each chapter" and "To run all the code for an item, just type `./item_01.py` into your shell and see what it prints out"; no tests and no expected output in the repo. — [bslatkin/effectivepython README](https://github.com/bslatkin/effectivepython)
- Fluent Python 2e's repository has one directory per chapter, a root `pytest.ini` and `ruff.toml`, and listings whose module docstrings carry doctests in interpreter format (`>>> v1 + v2` / `Vector(4, 5)`); the file has no `__main__` runner, so the doctest runs through `python -m doctest` or pytest. — [example-code-2e README](https://github.com/fluentpython/example-code-2e); [vector2d.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fluentpython/example-code-2e/master/01-data-model/vector2d.py)
- The Rust Book gives three exercises at the end of chapter 8 (median and mode, Pig Latin, an employee-directory interface) with no tests, expected output, or solutions, only a pointer to the standard-library docs; the checked companion is Rustlings. — [Rust Book ch. 8.3](https://doc.rust-lang.org/book/ch08-03-hash-maps.html)
- Doctest in teaching is anecdotal: instructors on edu-sig report that doctests "look just like what students will enter in an interactive session", that explanatory doctests are what get students to write tests at all, and that the downside is docstring clutter and no fixtures. No study found. — [edu-sig 2011 thread](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/edu-sig/2011-March/010215.html)

### Inferences
- The two RCTs reward the reader for *producing or predicting* output, not for reading it. A printed `#:` marker is a comparison target after the run; it becomes a prediction task only if the exercise asks for the prediction first, which a collapsed `<details>` around the output can enforce by convention.
- For experienced programmers the Leinonen result suggests the output oracle is neither help nor hindrance to learning; its value is checking. A behavioral test and a printed output differ in what they catch: output matching catches formatting and ordering, a test catches a return value or a raised exception. Neither study compares them.
- A doctest is the one oracle form that is simultaneously rendered Markdown, an expected-output marker, and a runnable test, which fits a book whose solutions are Markdown first.

### Gaps
- No study compares a printed expected output against a behavioral test or against no oracle.
- No evidence on "predict then run" where the output is printed on the same page as the code (the book's situation); the studies hide the output until the learner commits.
- The Learning & Instruction 2024 authors, method details, and effect sizes were not retrievable from the index record.

## Key question 5: Practice survey of self-study projects and books

### Takeaway
Three delivery shapes recur: a test file per exercise in the repo with a CLI runner (Exercism, Rustlings, koans, type-challenges), a server-side checker with a sample-versus-hidden split (Codewars, Advent of Code), and printed output with no checks (Learn Python the Hard Way, Effective Python, the Rust Book).
Maintainers' written lessons are about exercise *design* (Exercism's v3 rationale) and feedback latency, not about whether readers run the tests.

### Cited Findings
- Exercism, local workflow: `exercism download` gives `README.md` (the instructions), `HELP.md` ("how to run the tests and how to get help"), optional `HINTS.md`, the test file(s), a stub, and track tooling; the learner runs the tests locally and `exercism submit` uploads the solution. — [Exercism working locally](https://exercism.org/docs/using/solving-exercises/working-locally)
- Exercism, test philosophy: "The exercises are not production software, nor are they designed to imitate real-world use-cases. They are designed to be toy-problems to help you gain fluency", so edge-case test additions are usually declined. — [Suggesting Exercise Improvements](https://exercism.org/docs/community/suggesting-exercise-improvements)
- Exercism, v3 rationale: v2's core paths "looked good on paper" but "a year later ... no tracks have core pathways that do a great job of comprehensively teaching the key concepts", because maintainers reordered existing exercises and "the original exercises were never designed to teach language concepts"; v3 adds concept exercises that "introduce a specific concept in the instructions and then challenge the student to use that concept", with stubs to remove "unnecessary code". The author is "not convinced that at the end of an exercise a student really understands what they've learnt." — [rationale-for-v3.md](https://github.com/exercism/v3/blob/master/docs/rationale-for-v3.md)
- Exercism, other maintainer notes: an early Rust-track thread held that successful tracks gave feedback "within the first 24 hours" and favored nitpicks that prompt reflection over dictated fixes; a C# maintainer regrets "a wildly over engineered test generator"; a Clojure maintainer added CI that runs every exercise's example solution against its tests. — [exercism/rust issue 3](https://github.com/exercism/rust/issues/3); [Erik Schierboom blog](https://exercism.org/blog/thank-you-by-erik); [Clojurians log](https://clojurians-log.clojureverse.org/exercism/2021-10-12)
- Rustlings: ordered exercises, watch mode, `h` for a hint, a done/pending list, `exercises/` and `solutions/` directories in the repo, paired with the Rust Book; v6.5.0 as of 2025-08-21. — [Rustlings usage](https://rustlings.rust-lang.org/usage); [docs.rs/rustlings](https://docs.rs/rustlings)
- Python Koans and Elixir Koans: a single runner command, first failure shown, a watcher on save, `__` or blank placeholders, red-green-refactor framing. — [python_koans](https://github.com/gregmalcolm/python_koans); [elixir-koans](https://github.com/elixirkoans/elixir-koans)
- Advent of Code: each puzzle gives example input with expected answers ("If your program doesn't produce those answers, re-read the description"), a full personal input, and the advice to "build small test cases whose answers you can verify by hand" when the examples pass but the answer is wrong; every puzzle solves within 15 seconds on ten-year-old hardware; the author says puzzles are "designed to be interesting for humans to solve." The answer check itself is server-side. — [Advent of Code about](https://adventofcode.com/2025/about)
- Codewars: sample tests visible and editable in the trainer, submission tests hidden until ATTEMPT, sparse failure messages, honor on pass. — [Codewars docs](https://docs.codewars.com/training/training-example)
- type-challenges: tests expressed as types, checked by the compiler, online judge plus a generated local playground. — [type-challenges README](https://github.com/EdoTrotta/type-challenges)
- Learn Python the Hard Way: printed "What You Should See" after every exercise, no repo tests. Effective Python: runnable item scripts, "see what it prints out", no tests. Fluent Python: doctests in the listings, pytest config in the repo. Rust Book: prose exercises, no checks. — sources as in question 4.
- Keuning et al. found "teachers have difficulty adapting these tools to their own needs", which is one reason book authors default to printed output. — [Keuning et al. 2018](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/a-systematic-literature-review-of-automated-feedback-generation-f/)

### Inferences
- Every project that ships tests also ships a single-command runner with a watch mode and a hint command; the test file alone is never the whole delivery. The runner is what turns "tests exist" into "the reader runs them".
- The projects whose maintainers wrote retrospectives (Exercism) found their failures in exercise design, not in the test mechanism, which suggests the mechanism is cheap and the exercise-to-concept fit is the expensive part.
- Books aimed at experienced programmers (Effective Python, Fluent Python) chose output or doctest over test files, and none of them documents a reason.

### Gaps
- No maintainer write-up was found from Rustlings, Python Koans, Codewars, or Advent of Code on what worked pedagogically.
- No usage data (what fraction of readers run shipped tests) was found for any book repository.

## Key question 6: Minimum viable form for a Markdown-plus-pytest repo, and whether readers use shipped tests

### Takeaway
No study measures whether self-study readers run tests a book ships; the nearest evidence is that students run provided tests when a runner makes it one command, use them as a pass/fail gate rather than for probing, and test less thoroughly than required.
The forms that fit a static repo are a doctest in the exercise statement (rendered and runnable), a `test_exercise_N.py` beside the solution that imports the reader's file by a documented name, and a one-command `tip` task with a watch option; the practice survey says the runner matters more than the file.

### Cited Findings
- Fangohr et al. (arXiv 1509.03556) describe a system where the teacher writes unit tests, student code runs against them, feedback arrives by email within minutes, and resubmission is allowed, used since 2009 with a class of 425; its stated benefit is staff time. — [arXiv 1509.03556](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.03556)
- A study that provided unit tests and logged editing and test runs in Eclipse used the time between successive test runs to predict success, reporting "a clear difference in the strategies employed by students within different success levels" (abstract-level; full results not reached). — [NTNU record, Sharma et al.](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2593619?show=full)
- Students "only test to the level that they are required", and a common pattern is to run the code to get an output and then paste that output in as the expected value, which yields coverage without checking correctness and "false confidence" while hidden reference tests keep failing. — [CSEET 2017 paper](https://conferences.computer.org/cseet/2017/papers/2536a170.pdf)
- One comparison found students who write their own tests "perform better in programming assignments" than those given instructor tests. — [ICIT 2011 paper](https://icit.zuj.edu.jo/icit11/PaperList/Papers/E-Education/535_Ivan.pdf)
- In the community-college study, students checked feedback almost every time they submitted (64 checks per 66 submissions), so when the feedback is a click away it is read; 28% of pages still went unread. — [arXiv 2507.14235](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.14235v1)
- Edwards' pilot and the Baniassad survey both report that students prefer and value the immediate check (Edwards: "Students preferred this approach over that used in prior classes"; Baniassad: 31% expressed positive regard for the penalties, 10% naming the production-like discipline). — [Edwards 2003](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/stephen-edwards/teaching-testing.pdf); [Baniassad 2021](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rtholmes/papers/sigcse_2021_baniassad.pdf)
- Exercism's per-exercise package (instructions, HELP with the test command, hints, test file, stub) and Rustlings' single `rustlings` command with watch, hint, and list are the two established minimal shapes for a cloned repo. — [Exercism working locally](https://exercism.org/docs/using/solving-exercises/working-locally); [Rustlings usage](https://rustlings.rust-lang.org/usage)
- Fluent Python's doctests show that a listing can carry its expected output in a form pytest runs (`pytest --doctest-modules`) and GitHub renders as code. — [vector2d.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/fluentpython/example-code-2e/master/01-data-model/vector2d.py)
- Wrenn, Nelson, and Krishnamurthi (Brown) report that "relational" problems, where one input admits several valid outputs (graph algorithms, sorts with ties), "easily motivate the use of PBT", and that PBT "is not widespread in collegiate curricula" partly because good motivating examples are hard to find; a 2021 follow-up uses Hypothesis to evaluate student-written properties. Mariño et al. report three classroom experiences with QuviQ QuickCheck. No learning-outcome comparison between example-based and property-based tests was found. — [arXiv 2010.16305](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.16305); [arXiv 2111.10414](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.10414)

### Inferences
- For this repo the three forms map onto existing machinery. (1) A doctest-style `>>>` block inside the exercise statement is the cheapest: it renders in the Markdown, pytest runs it against the Solutions `.py` in the gate, and the reader runs `python -m doctest` on their own file. (2) A `test_exercise_N.py` beside `Solutions/<ch>/README.md` is the Exercism shape; it needs a documented import name (`exercise_N` or a module the reader names on the command line) so the same test runs against the reader's attempt and the committed solution. (3) A `tip exercise CH=30 N=3` task that extracts the test, runs it against a path the reader passes, and reruns on save is the Rustlings shape; `tip hint` already prints the ladder's steps one per run, so the test runner slots in as a fourth rung between "The shape" and "Solution".
- The behavioral evidence (tests used as a gate, expected values pasted from a run) argues for tests that assert a *property* or a hand-verified value rather than a captured output, since a reader can regenerate a captured output from a wrong program. Relational problems are the natural place for a Hypothesis property; most of the book's exercises are not relational, so example-based tests with hand-chosen values are the default.
- Because the reader can open the solution at any time, the only enforcement available is sequencing in the rendered page (the ladder) and friction (the test lives in a separate file the hint names). Marmoset-style limits and hidden tests do not exist here, so the design should assume the reader sees everything and make the test worth running on its own terms: it should tell the reader *which* behavior is wrong in the words the chapter uses, which is the Testing Tutor "conceptual feedback" finding applied to a test's failure message.

### Gaps
- No evidence on what fraction of readers of any book run shipped tests, doctests, or runners.
- No study of doctest-as-exercise-oracle, or of property-based tests shipped with self-study exercises, on a learning outcome.
- The Sharma et al. Eclipse-log study and the CSEET 2017 paper were reached only at abstract level.
