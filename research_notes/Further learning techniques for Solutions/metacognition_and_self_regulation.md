# Metacognitive and self-regulation techniques for a reader working an exercise before opening its solution

Scope note.
The book's ladder is three rungs: "Where to look" (a hint back into the chapter), "The shape" (the solution's skeleton with bodies elided), then the full solution with a short design explanation.
The reader is an experienced programmer working alone in a static Markdown file.
These notes cover six techniques that change what the reader does with their own attempt; worked examples, completion problems, subgoal labels, post-solution self-explanation prompts, and compare-two-implementations exercises were covered in an earlier round and are mentioned only where a finding here depends on them.
Population flags: most laboratory evidence is on undergraduates (adults, but not experienced in the domain); the tutoring-system evidence is on high-school students; two sources are on adult professionals or graduate students and are marked as such.

## Key question 1: Does committing to an answer before seeing the solution improve retention or transfer compared with reading the solution directly?

### Takeaway
Generating an answer before seeing the correct one reliably improves later memory for that answer when corrective feedback follows promptly, with effects of roughly 10 to 20 percentage points on recall in laboratory studies with undergraduates and a d of about 0.3 against retrieval practice on text passages (n = 1,573).
The benefit needs an informed attempt (not a blind guess), prompt feedback, and material the learner can relate to; for experienced learners the expertise-reversal literature says problem solving before any example is already the better order, so the ladder's "attempt first" default is well supported, but no study tests the effect on experienced programmers specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), six experiments with undergraduates: trying to answer (fictional general-knowledge questions; cue-to-weak-associate pairs) and failing, then seeing the answer, produced better final recall than reading question and answer together for the same total time; correct guesses were excluded, so the effect is from errors — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/)
- Metcalfe's 2017 review reports that in the related-pair version of this paradigm the benefit was about 20% (Huelser & Metcalfe 2012), that participants nevertheless judged the error-free condition to have worked better, and that the effect vanishes when the pairs are unrelated (Huelser & Metcalfe 2012; Grimaldi & Karpicke 2012; Knight et al. 2012) or when learners have no idea at all and are forced to guess (Kang et al. 2011): "to be beneficial, the guess needs to be somewhat informed rather than a shot in the dark" — [Metcalfe 2017, Learning from Errors, Annual Review of Psychology](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Hays, Kornell & Bjork (2012): failed tests followed by immediate feedback beat presentation-only; delayed feedback produced no comparable benefit except in one reversed-direction test condition — [Hays, Kornell & Bjork, in-press PDF](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Hays_Kornell_RBjork_inpress.pdf)
- Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009), five experiments, undergraduates reading a two-page passage on vision/colorblindness: answering open-ended pretest questions before reading (all answers wrong) improved final-test performance on those questions versus extra reading time, even when the tested concepts were highlighted for the control group; a secondary account puts the advantage near 10 percentage points — [Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009 PDF](https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf); [UChicago Learning Lab summary](https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing.html)
- Potts & Shanks (2014), four experiments, adults learning unfamiliar word definitions and foreign translations: guess-then-feedback beat both reading and choosing among options, so generation helps even when nearly every guess is wrong and unrelated to the target; learners again judged errorful generation the worse method — [Westminster repository record](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/8y95x/the-benefit-of-generating-errors-during-learning)
- Follow-ups: Seabrooke et al. (2019, Basque nouns) found guessing improved recognition but impaired cued recall, so the test format matters; Potts, Davies & Shanks (2018) found generating a translation after seeing the answer did not help, pointing to anticipation or curiosity as the mechanism — [Plymouth record (Seabrooke et al.)](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/handle/10026.1/12450); [UCL record (Potts, Davies & Shanks)](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10052994)
- Pan & Sana (2021), five experiments, combined n = 1,573, expository text passages: a pretest before reading was as good as or better than a posttest after reading, across test formats, with and without feedback, and at different retention intervals — [Athabasca record of Pan & Sana 2021](https://pure.athabascau.ca/en/publications/pretesting-versus-posttesting-comparing-the-pedagogical-benefits-/)
- A later paper citing Pan & Sana reports a pretesting advantage of Cohen's d = 0.30 over retrieval practice across four of those experiments (this figure comes from a citing paper surfaced in search; verify against the original) — [Memory & Cognition 2022 citing paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-022-01392-1)
- Pan & Carpenter (2023) is the current review of prequestioning and pretesting (Educational Psychology Review 35(4), DOI 10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5); one practitioner summary of it says pretesting benefits learning "if there is an opportunity to study the correct answers afterward" — [Ollie Lovell summary of Pan & Carpenter](https://www.ollielovell.com/how-you-can-use-prequestioning-to-boost-student-learning/)
- Brod (2021) conceptual review of predicting: every cited study found a benefit of predicting before seeing the outcome; the proposed mechanism beyond retrieval is surprise at a wrong prediction, which raises attention to the correct answer; predicting is defined by having at least some confidence in the response, and guessing (no confidence) is a different, weaker case; the review covers simple declarative material only and does not address whether a prediction must be written versus thought — [Brod 2021, Predicting as a learning strategy, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8642250/)
- Brod (2021) also notes that guessing helps only when the correct answer follows shortly after the guess (Vaughn & Rawson 2012) and that correct predictions do not generate the surprise response — [Brod 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8642250/)
- Crouch, Fagen, Callan & Mazur (2004), introductory physics undergraduates: students who merely watched a demonstration understood the concept about as well as students who never saw it; students who predicted the outcome before watching did significantly better; a secondary summary gives learning gains of 0.09 (watch), 0.36 (predict and watch), 0.45 (predict, watch, discuss), with the prediction step costing about two minutes (figures not verified against the paper) — [ComPADRE record of Crouch et al. 2004](https://www.compadre.org/IntroPhys/items/detail.cfm?ID=2329); [Glessmer summary with gain figures](https://mirjamglessmer.com/2014/06/01/how-to-make-demos-useful-in-teaching/)
- Kalyuga, Chandler, Tuovinen & Sweller (2001), mechanical trade apprentices: inexperienced trainees learned more from worked examples; as experience grew, worked examples became redundant and problem solving came out ahead (the expertise reversal effect), so for experienced learners attempting first is the recommended order independent of any pretesting mechanism — [academia.edu copy of Kalyuga et al. 2001](https://www.academia.edu/1405548/When_problem_solving_is); [Kalyuga 2007 review PDF](https://faculty.engineering.asu.edu/mre/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2020/02/Exp_Rev_LI06.pdf)
- Sinha & Kapur (2021), meta-analysis of 53 studies and 166 comparisons, mostly school and university students in STEM and medicine (45% in grades 6 to 10): problem solving before instruction beats instruction first, with larger effects at high fidelity to productive-failure design principles; press coverage reports effects "up to three times" a good teacher's annual effect, but I could not retrieve the pooled Hedges' g (DOI 10.3102/00346543211019105) — [World Economic Forum coverage of Sinha & Kapur](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/students-who-productively-fail-learn-more/)
- Predicting output before running code is the first stage of PRIMM (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make); learners record the prediction in writing; a mixed-methods study with 13 teachers and 493 secondary-school students found the PRIMM group outperformed controls, but this is schoolchildren and the whole package, not the prediction step in isolation — [Raspberry Pi Foundation on PRIMM research](https://www.raspberrypi.org/blog/primm-talk-in-programming-lessons-research-seminar/); [Teach Computing Quick Read on PRIMM](https://static.teachcomputing.org/pedagogy/QR11-PRIMM.pdf)
- Keith & Frese (2008), meta-analysis of 24 error-management-training studies, mostly adult software-skills training: letting trainees make errors and learn from them beat proceduralized step-by-step training, with the largest benefit on post-training transfer; the benefit was larger when trainees were told to expect errors and treat them as informative — reported in [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)

### Inferences
- The ladder already enforces the pretesting order (attempt, then hint, then shape, then solution).
  What the evidence adds is that the attempt must be committed and informed: the reader should write code or a written design before opening any rung, not just think about it, because the surprise mechanism needs a response the reader holds with some confidence.
  Writing is the cheapest way to make the commitment real in a static file; no study tests written versus mental commitment directly, so this is practice backed by the mechanism, not a measured difference.
- Prediction tasks fit the book's `#:` output markers: a one-line prompt before a listing's output ("Before you run it, write what this prints") turns the marker reveal into a predict-observe step.
  The physics-demo result (Crouch et al.) is the closest analogue and it is on undergraduates in a lecture, so treat it as supportive, not proven for code.
- The error-management-training meta-analysis is the one adult, software-domain result, and it favors attempting and erring over following a procedure.
  It supports a one-line framing at the top of each solutions file that tells the reader to expect the first attempt to be wrong in places and to treat the diff as information.
- For an experienced reader, expertise reversal says the full solution should be the last rung, not an alternative entry point; the ladder's order is right and should not offer a "skip to solution" shortcut.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests pretesting, prediction, or errorful generation on experienced programmers or on multi-line code design tasks; the material in every cited experiment is facts, word pairs, or short passages.
- No study found that compares a written attempt against a mental attempt before reveal.
- The pooled Hedges' g for Sinha & Kapur (2021) was not retrievable; only the press framing is cited.
- Hausman & Rhodes (2018), "When pretesting fails to enhance learning," appeared in search results as a boundary-condition paper but was not read — [PDF](https://hausman.sites.ucsc.edu/files/2023/02/Hausman.Rhodes.2018b-When-Pretesting-Fails-to-Enhance-Learning.pdf)

## Key question 2: Does asking "how sure are you?" before the reveal improve learning or just measure it, and does hypercorrection argue for a "state your confidence" step?

### Takeaway
A confidence rating is mostly a measurement, with a reactivity effect that is negligible overall (meta-analytic g = 0.05) and that can help or hurt depending on material and on the learner's self-confidence.
Hypercorrection is robust and real (high-confidence errors are corrected more, including a week later), but it is a property of the error, not of the act of rating; the rating's value in a static ladder is to make the reader notice a high-confidence error, which is also the one most likely to return if the correction is forgotten.

### Cited Findings
- Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001): undergraduates answered general-knowledge questions, rated confidence, saw the correct answer, and were retested after 5 minutes; high-confidence errors were corrected more often than low-confidence errors; the authors proposed that the discrepancy between confidence and feedback captures attention — [PMC review describing the paradigm](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4084803)
- Metcalfe (2017) lists the effect as replicated across many labs, with immediate and delayed retests; the two explanations are surprise (a tone-detection task showed attention captured at high-confidence-error feedback, Butterfield & Metcalfe 2006; a confidence-graded P3a in ERP, Butterfield & Mangels 2003) and greater semantic familiarity with the domain of a high-confidence error (errors are "closer" to the right answer by latent semantic analysis; learners often say they "knew it all along") — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Butler, Fazio & Marsh (2011): the effect held both immediately and after one week, but overall correction fell on the delayed test, and high-confidence errors that were not corrected were more likely than low-confidence errors to be reproduced; a secondary summary gives immediate correction rising from 79% (lowest confidence) to 92% (highest), and delayed from 43% to 64% (figures not verified against the paper) — [WUSTL record of Butler, Fazio & Marsh 2011](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-hypercorrection-effect-persists-over-a-week-but-high-confiden/); [memory-key summary with percentages](https://archive.memory-key.com/research/news/problem-correcting-false-knowledge)
- Older adults are the exception: they correct high-confidence errors only slightly more than low-confidence errors, because they also correct low-confidence errors very well (Eich et al. 2013; Sitzman et al. 2015; Metcalfe et al. 2015) — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Van Loon et al. (2015): hypercorrection of science misconceptions occurred only with refutation texts that stated the misconception and refuted it, not with standard texts that presented the correct fact — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Metcalfe (2017) on overconfidence: people decline further study when confident they have learned, and one method shown to offset overconfidence is to have people consider how they could be wrong (Koriat, Lichtenstein & Fischhoff 1980) — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Reactivity of judgments of learning: Double, Birney & Walker (2018) meta-analysis found an overall effect of g = 0.054 (95% CI −0.027 to 0.135), with positive reactivity for related word pairs (g = 0.32) and word lists (g = 0.38) and none for unrelated pairs — [Durham repository record](https://durham-repository.worktribe.com/output/2253677/a-meta-analysis-and-systematic-review-of-reactivity-to-judgements-of-learning)
- A 17-experiment series (Undorf, Schäfer & Halamish) found JOLs reliably improved cued recall of related pairs while the negative effect on unrelated pairs was about as large and robust; Halamish, Meer & Undorf (2026) found reactivity arises from the requirement to assess learning, not from reporting a number — [Haifa record (Halamish et al.)](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/making-judgments-of-learning-either-enhances-or-impairs-memory-ev/); [Shanks, Zhao et al. manuscript on JOL reactivity](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10200684/1/Shanks_Zhao_et_al_JML_manuscript_final.pdf)
- Confidence ratings on reasoning tasks: Birney et al. (2017), 252 industry managers (adults), found that giving confidence ratings impaired Raven's matrices performance, most on difficult items; Double & Birney (2017) found ratings helped participants with high initial self-confidence and hurt those with low self-confidence — [Durham record (Birney et al. 2017)](https://dro.dur.ac.uk/20799); [Sydney thesis record (Double)](https://ses.library.usyd.edu.au/handle/2123/18588); [Double & Birney 2019, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6557865/)
- Zhang, Chen & Liu (2019): the benefit of retrieval practice emerged only for answers held with confidence above about 56%, so confidence tags the items that practice actually consolidated — [Stony Brook record](https://researchconnect.stonybrook.edu/en/publications/confidence-in-accuracy-moderates-the-benefits-of-retrieval-practi/)
- Calibration training in a classroom: Callender, Franco-Watkins & Roberts (2016), upper-level undergraduates (Study 1 n = 127, Study 2 n = 90), taught about overconfidence, gave performance judgments on exams, and got feedback with incentives; judgments and performance both improved from exam 1 to exam 2, and in Study 2 improvement appeared only in the semester with feedback; quasi-experimental — [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11409-015-9142-6)

### Inferences
- A "state your confidence" line costs little and its direct learning effect is near zero on average, so it should be justified by what it enables, not by reactivity.
  What it enables in a static file is a self-administered hypercorrection check: a reader who wrote "sure" and then finds the shape or solution differs has the surprise that the literature says drives the correction, and knows this is the error most likely to recur, so the prompt can say "if you were sure and wrong, write down why the wrong design seemed right."
- The reasoning-task findings (managers, matrices) are a warning: a confidence prompt on a hard design task can depress performance for a reader with low self-confidence.
  Phrase it as a prediction about a specific output or behavior ("will this raise on an empty list?") rather than a global "how good is your solution," and place it after the attempt, not before.
- Van Loon's refutation-text result suggests the solution's design explanation should name the plausible wrong design and say why it fails, not only present the right one; that is the only form in which hypercorrection of a conceptual error showed up.
- Callender et al. show calibration improves with repeated judgment plus feedback; the ladder can give the feedback for free by pairing a yes/no prediction with the `#:` marker, so the reader gets a calibration data point every exercise without an instructor.

### Gaps
- No study tests whether a confidence prompt before revealing a programming solution changes later performance.
- The hypercorrection literature is about short factual answers; no evidence found on whether a confident wrong design (as opposed to a confident wrong fact) is corrected better.
- Percentages for Butler, Fazio & Marsh (2011) come from a secondary summary and were not verified against the paper.

## Key question 3: Stop rules: when should a learner open a hint or solution, and is there evidence for a minimum effort first?

### Takeaway
The tutoring-system literature (high-school students) finds that clicking through to the answer without engaging ("help abuse") predicts poor learning at every skill level, that failing attempts on skills the learner barely knows predicted better learning than asking for help, and that teaching students to seek help well changed their behavior but not their learning.
No study prescribes a minimum time or attempt count; the one measurable rule is behavioral (attempt, then reflect on the error, then ask), and platforms that gate solutions behind attempts do so as practice, not from measured outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- Roll, Baker, Aleven & Koedinger (JLS), 38 high-school students using the Geometry Cognitive Tutor for two months, 25,337 actions on 6,434 problem steps, within-student logistic regression on success at the next step needing the same skill: desired help seeking raised the odds of success (odds ratio 1.45, p = .01; from 50% to 59%); help abuse (clicking through hints to the bottom-out answer, or asking instead of trying when the skill is known) cut a 50% chance of learning to 14%, 7%, and 19% on high-, medium-, and low-skill steps; and on low-skill steps, "inappropriate attempts" (trying and failing instead of asking) raised a 50% chance to 68%, so avoiding help and failing repeatedly beat asking for help on skills the student had barely begun — [Roll et al. JLS manuscript PDF](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/Roll%20HT%20JLS%20clean.pdf)
- The same paper's Help Seeking Model prescribes: attempt only if you think you know how; if you understand the problem but not the method, look it up (glossary); if you do not understand the problem, ask for a hint; after an error, reflect on the error, then either retry or ask depending on whether you understood the error; students on high-skill steps are expected to retry after one error, those on low-skill steps to ask — [Roll et al. JLS manuscript PDF](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/Roll%20HT%20JLS%20clean.pdf)
- The paper notes a competing long-term finding: Baker, Gowda & Corbett (2011) found students who avoid help and keep failing do worse on later transfer tests when all skill levels are pooled, and a pooled analysis in this paper found the same trend; the benefit of failing appeared only on low-skill steps — [Roll et al. JLS manuscript PDF](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/Roll%20HT%20JLS%20clean.pdf)
- Shih, Koedinger & Scheines (2008): drilling to the bottom-out hint is not always abuse; a response-time model separates students who read and reflect on the given answer (treating it as a worked example, with learning) from those who copy it; longer reflection time after a detailed hint was associated with greater learning — [Shih, Koedinger & Scheines 2008 PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf)
- LearnLab's help-abuse entry: in one Geometry Cognitive Tutor unit, students abused hints on 36% of actions, or 14% when rapid successive requests count as one — [LearnLab wiki, Help abuse](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse)
- Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Wagner (2004), middle-school Cognitive Tutor classrooms: gaming the system (exploiting feedback and help to advance) was the off-task behavior most strongly tied to lower learning, correlating with post-test score about as strongly as prior knowledge; correlational — [CHI 2004 paper PDF](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/materials/conferences/CHI2004/1p383.pdf)
- Aleven, Roll, McLaren & Koedinger (2016), review: the Help Tutor's real-time feedback on help seeking made students use on-demand help more deliberately, and the change persisted after feedback stopped, but did not improve domain learning; the authors now see on-demand principle-based help as less important than they once did, though still worth including — [Help Helps, but only so Much (record)](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/help-helps-but-only-so-much-research-on-help-seeking-with-intelli/); [Roll et al. 2011 Help Tutor study (n = 58)](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/improving-students-help-seeking-skills-using-metacognitive-feedba/)
- Koedinger & Aleven (2007) frame this as the assistance dilemma: assistance makes learning efficient but misused help produces shallow learning, while withholding forces construction at the cost of time and confusion; no general parameters for when to give or withhold are established — [Koedinger & Aleven 2007 PDF](https://pslcdatashop.web.cmu.edu/KDDCup/FAQ/Koedinger-Aleven-EPR-07.pdf)
- Metcalfe & Kornell's region-of-proximal-learning model: learners should (and when free to choose, do) stop studying an item when their judged rate of learning approaches zero, rather than at a mastery criterion; under free choice undergraduates spent most time on medium-difficulty items, which matched where learning curves kept rising — [Metcalfe & Kornell 2005 PDF](https://psychology.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/2016-11/Metcalfe.Kornell.2005_0.pdf); [Kornell & Bjork 2007 PDF](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_RBjork_2007.pdf)
- The labor-in-vain effect (Nelson & Leonesio 1988): under accuracy instructions, undergraduates spent up to 10 s more per item, mostly on the hardest items, with very little gain in performance; correlational — [PMC article reviewing labor-in-vain](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11273422/)
- Practice, not measured: Open edX lets authors require a minimum number of submitted attempts before "Show Answer" appears and can impose a wait between attempts; Grok Learning reveals the solution after a correct submission or, for some courses, five days after the first attempt; Berkeley CS61A releases solutions only after submission and logs both submission and solution-view times; none of these report learning outcomes — [Open edX problem settings](https://docs.openedx.org/en/ulmo/_sources/educators/references/course_development/problem_settings.rst); [Grok Learning help](https://groklearning.helpscoutdocs.com/article/149-how-do-i-view-solutions-for-a-problem); [CS61A solutions policy](https://edge.edx.org/c4x/UCBerkeley/CS61A/asset/solutions_and_autograders.pdf)

### Inferences
- The strongest, most consistent result is negative: opening rungs rapidly in sequence without engaging each one is the pattern tied to poor learning at every skill level.
  A static ladder cannot enforce a delay, but it can make each rung demand an action before the next: "Where to look" ends with "reread that section and change your attempt before opening the shape"; "The shape" ends with "fill in the bodies before opening the solution."
- The low-skill result (failing beats asking) argues against telling an experienced reader to open the hint quickly on a topic new to them; the Roll et al. explanation is that a learner without grounding cannot make sense of the hint yet.
  A reasonable rule to state: open "Where to look" after a failed attempt you can describe, not before an attempt.
- The region-of-proximal-learning stop rule gives a wording that is evidence-based rather than a timer: stop and open the next rung when you have made no progress in your last few minutes, not when a clock expires.
  "Attempt for N minutes" has no empirical support that I found; a progress-based rule has the Metcalfe & Kornell model behind it and avoids the labor-in-vain pattern.
- Shih et al.'s response-time result transfers directly to the full-solution rung: reading the solution slowly and reflecting is what distinguished learning from copying, so the solution rung should open with a prompt that takes time (the diff and classification in question 4), not with the code alone.
- The Help Tutor result (behavior changed, learning did not) is a caution against expecting a written help-seeking policy to raise learning on its own; its value is in preventing the abuse pattern, which does predict poor learning.

### Gaps
- All tutoring-system evidence is on secondary-school students in geometry; nothing comparable on adults or on programming.
- No study found that measures a minimum-time or minimum-attempts gate against learning outcomes, in programming or elsewhere; the gating platforms are practice.
- No study found on "show solution" analytics in programming autograders with a learning outcome.

## Key question 4: Does diffing one's attempt against the reference and classifying the difference help?

### Takeaway
Self-diagnosis against a reference helps, most for lower-prior-knowledge learners, but two studies on students show the failure mode directly: given a detailed written solution to compare against, many students match surface features, treat the sample as a template, and miss conceptual conflicts; the group that had to diagnose with only notes and textbooks (no written solution) transferred best.
Reflecting on the rationale behind one's own error beat reflecting on the correct solution in a randomized field experiment (grades 7 and 8).
No programming study of a diff-and-classify prompt was found.

### Cited Findings
- Mason, Yerushalmi, Cohen & Singh (2016; data from Yerushalmi et al. 2012), about 200 college students in algebra-based physics, recitation sections assigned to control or three self-diagnosis conditions: students diagnosed their own graded quiz solutions with (I) an oral outline plus a rubric, (W) a detailed written solution, or (S) only the final answer plus notes and textbook; all then received the full solution; the transfer measure was a paired midterm problem; in the control group quiz and transfer scores correlated (r = 0.41, p = .03) and in every self-diagnosis group they did not, which the authors read as the gap between low and high prior knowledge closing; self-diagnosis quality predicted transfer only in group S on the typical problem (r = 0.54, p = .01); on the challenging problem group S averaged 51% on transfer versus 35% for group W, a 46% relative advantage — [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.07253); [Yerushalmi et al. 2012 PRST-PER record](https://doaj.org/article/71c1b7f6cc1c49d181b8fb1a4c769cf1)
- The authors' interpretation: students given a detailed solution "may have simply compared the surface features of their solution with the solution provided," producing an acceptable diagnosis without self-repair, while students who struggled with notes only engaged more with the solution handed out afterward — [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.07253)
- Safadi & Yerushalmi (2009), 180 high-school students (Arab sector, Israel): students comparing their solution with a sample solution found the significant differences but many gave no sign of recognizing a conflict between their model and the scientific one, and they treated the sample as an "ultimate template," labeling non-significant surface deviations as flaws — [PER-Central record](https://www.per-central.org/items/detail.cfm?ID=9497)
- Weizmann project summary of the same line: students have difficulty self-diagnosing; with external support, low-prior-knowledge students benefit most; pairs discussing criteria for scientific versus alternative interpretations helped; a year of repeated computerized troubleshooting improved self-repair more than a traditional self-diagnosis intervention — [Weizmann project page](https://weizmann.ac.il/ScienceTeaching/research-and-development/physics/projects/1874)
- Heemsoth & Heinze (2016), randomized field experiment, 174 seventh- and eighth-graders learning fractions, pre/post/follow-up: reflecting on the rationale behind one's own errors produced greater procedural knowledge at post and follow-up, and greater conceptual knowledge at follow-up, than reflecting on the correct solution to the same errors — [Leibniz-IPN record](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/secondary-school-students-learning-from-reflections-on-the-rationale-behind-self-made-errors)
- Metcalfe (2017) reports Siegler (1995), children: after feedback, "How do you think I knew that?" (explain the correct reasoning) beat "Why do you think that?" (explain own reasoning) and feedback alone; and describes the army's after-action review (every misstep analyzed, what should have been done and why) as a practice with wide consensus but little experimental research — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Metcalfe (2017): externally presented wrong answers harm memory where self-generated errors help (Grimaldi & Karpicke 2012), and whether watching someone else's error plus feedback helps is untested — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Comparison format in programming (covered in the earlier round, cited here only for the mechanism): Patitsas, Craig & Easterbrook, n = 241 second-level CS students, side-by-side compare-and-contrast of different algorithms beat sequential presentation on code reading and writing and on flexibility, replicating Rittle-Johnson & Star — [compare_contrast.pdf](https://cs.mcgill.ca/~patitsas/publications/compare_contrast.pdf)
- Exercism practice (not measured): the "Dig Deeper" tab presents curated approaches with tradeoffs after the reader's tests pass; the team's motivation was that in 500 passing TwoFer solutions over 350 were unique and over half remained distinct after normalizing names and whitespace; a mentor reported 8 to 10 students overlooked the tab in four weeks — [Exercism blog, Dig Deeper](https://exercism.org/blog/dig-deeper); [Exercism forum thread](https://forum.exercism.org/t/emphasize-the-dig-deeper-tab/4583)
- Henderson & Harper (2009) quiz corrections in physics are the practice antecedent of self-diagnosis tasks, cited by Mason et al. as "similar in spirit" — [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.07253)

### Inferences
- The template failure mode is the main design constraint.
  A bare "compare your solution to the reference" prompt invites surface matching (variable names, ordering, idiom) and lets the reader declare every difference a flaw.
  The classification the task brief proposes (wrong design, wrong detail, equivalent alternative) is the right counter, because "equivalent alternative" is the category that Safadi's students lacked; the solution's design explanation should name which choices are load-free alternatives and which are the point.
- Heemsoth & Heinze and Siegler together suggest the prompt wording: first "why did your version seem right?" (rationale of the own error, which won at follow-up), then "why does the reference make this choice?" (explain the correct reasoning, which won in Siegler).
  Both are on children; the direction is consistent across the two and across Metcalfe's review, so it is a reasonable default, flagged as untested on adults.
- The group-S result (notes only beats written solution during diagnosis) maps onto the ladder's order: "Where to look" is the notes-only condition, and the full solution comes after the reader has tried to repair with the chapter alone.
  It argues against putting the full solution's design explanation inside "The shape."
- Since externally presented wrong answers can harm memory, a solution's "common wrong design" note should be short and framed as the reader's own possible attempt ("if you reached for X, the test that fails is Y"), not as a catalog of bugs to read.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures a diff-and-classify prompt in programming, with or without categories.
- No evidence found on peer-comparison or community-solution browsing (Exercism, Codecademy) with a learning outcome.
- Yerushalmi's 2012 companion papers (parts I and II) were not read; sample sizes per group and the atypical-problem results are in them.

## Key question 5: What wording of a post-solve reflection prompt has evidence, and what has none?

### Takeaway
Prompts that make the learner generate something not already on the page (explain the correct reasoning, state the rationale of their own error, plan before a hint) have the evidence; prompts that ask for a restatement or a free-form "what did you learn" produce text but no measured learning gain.
One adult result exists: in an online data-science master's course, hints plus reflection prompts raised delayed transfer performance over hints alone (no effect sizes available); the "what would break this" and "where else does this apply" wordings have no direct evidence either way.

### Cited Findings
- Choi, Jovanovic, Poquet, Brooks, Joksimović & Williams (2023), field experiment in a four-week online master's data-science course (adults; n not given on the record): hints with reflection prompts increased performance on delayed knowledge-transfer tasks and perceived learning versus hints alone, without reducing enjoyment; prompt wording and statistics not on the record — [TUM record of Choi et al. 2023](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/the-benefit-of-reflection-prompts-for-encouraging-learning-with-h/)
- Choi et al. (2025 preprint), online introductory programming course: reflection prompts before an AI-generated hint, targeting planning, and directed (structured) rather than open produced higher-quality reflections but lower hint satisfaction; immediate performance did not differ across conditions; no delayed measure reported — [arXiv 2512.04630](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04630)
- Davis (2003), middle-school science, Journal of the Learning Sciences 12(1): generic prompts (learner picks the focus) led to more coherent understanding on complex projects, while directed prompts more often elicited unproductive reflection; later summaries note directed prompts suit learners with less reflective experience, and that the open-versus-directed question remains debated — [FU Berlin thesis page summarizing Davis](https://www.mi.fu-berlin.de/en/inf/groups/hcc/theses/finished/2023-Theses/effect-of-general-and-specific-reflection-prompts/index.html); [THE Campus piece on generic prompts](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/campus/generic-prompts-give-students-autonomy-and-flexibility-expand-their-ideas)
- Siegler (1995), children: "How do you think I knew that?" after feedback beat "Why do you think that?" and feedback alone; Siegler's own gloss is that explaining another's correct reasoning combines discovery (the child generates the analysis) with didactic focus on the correct reasoning — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Heemsoth & Heinze (2016), grades 7 and 8, randomized: "explain the rationale behind your own error" beat "reflect on the correct solution" at post-test and follow-up — [Leibniz-IPN record](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/secondary-school-students-learning-from-reflections-on-the-rationale-behind-self-made-errors)
- Metcalfe (2017): having people consider how they could be wrong offsets overconfidence (Koriat, Lichtenstein & Fischhoff 1980), and a medical-school reflective method has students imagine their diagnosis has already failed and reason from there (Mamede & Schmidt 2004) — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- A data-structures course study (219 students, ASEE) used "What did you learn?", "What was the hardest part?", and "What did you change from your initial design?" after two assignments; 87% of responses were coded at the two highest reflection levels; this is a coding of response depth, with no learning outcome — [ASEE paper record](https://peer.asee.org/37651)
- Wellesley CS111 asks students to compare their work against posted solutions and answer reflection questions about the solutions and their process ("to help you take an active role in monitoring your learning"); practice, not measured — [CS111 reflection assignment](https://cs111.wellesley.edu/archive/cs111_spring17/public_html//psets/ps06/reflection)
- Loksa & Ko's problem-solving framework (reinterpret the prompt, search for analogous problems, search for solutions, evaluate a potential solution, implement, evaluate the implementation) with five self-regulation types; Loksa's 2020 dissertation reports that explicit training helped learners become more productive and independent and raised self-efficacy; an in-situ study found some students self-regulated in many ways and others in almost none, with reported difficulty integrating reflection into their work; novices — [PCDIT paper citing Loksa & Ko](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.08896); [Loksa 2020 dissertation record](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/2c8e1804-c9fe-4dda-834d-23a1b07f2789/full)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate practice testing and distributed practice highest, summarization, highlighting, and rereading lowest; a reflection prompt that asks the reader to restate the solution is closest to summarization — [Kent State summary of Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.kent.edu/node/230016)

### Inferences
- Wordings with evidence behind them, each mapped to a source: "Why does the reference make this choice?" (Siegler: explain the correct reasoning); "Why did your version seem right?" (Heemsoth & Heinze: rationale of the own error); "How could this solution be wrong?" (Koriat et al. 1980 via Metcalfe: considering how you could be wrong offsets overconfidence).
  The first two are on children and the third is a confidence-calibration result, so for an adult programmer they are the best-supported defaults rather than proven prompts.
- "What would break this?" has no direct study, but it is the generative form of "how could this be wrong," which the overconfidence literature supports, and it sits inside the book's testing idiom (write the failing case).
  "Where else does this apply?" has no direct study found; it resembles elaborative interrogation and analogical comparison, which the earlier round covered, so it should be labeled as plausible rather than evidenced.
- Wordings without evidence of learning gain: "What did you learn?", "What was the hardest part?", and any prompt answerable by rereading the solution.
  The ASEE study shows students write deep-sounding responses to these; it did not show they learn more.
- Choi et al. (2025) found directed, before-hint, planning prompts produce the best reflections and the least satisfaction; in a book the reader can skip anything, so a directed prompt should be one line and sit where the reader is already stopped (just above the next rung), not as a separate exercise.
- Since the earlier round found appended self-explanation prompts weak or harmful, the distinction to preserve is generation versus restatement: a prompt must require something the page does not already contain (a failing input, a reason the wrong design looked right, a second place the pattern fits).

### Gaps
- No study found that compares specific post-solve prompt wordings on adults learning programming with a delayed outcome; Choi et al. (2023) is the only adult result and its prompt text is not on the record.
- "What would break this?" and "Where else does this apply?" have no direct evidence found.
- Davis (2003) itself was not read; findings come from secondary summaries that partly cite a 2000 paper.

## Key question 6: Does reading a fluent solution inflate confidence, and what counters it?

### Takeaway
Yes, in several forms with measured sizes: incomplete worked examples led university students to overestimate their performance while completion and conventional problems did not; a physics explainer video alone produced an illusion of understanding that demanding follow-up tasks cut by d = 0.69; learners in errorful-generation studies judged the better method worse.
The counter in every case is a task that requires producing the answer with the solution out of sight, and for the illusion from a visible answer (foresight bias), debiasing transferred to new items only when learners adopted a theory about why the illusion happens.

### Cited Findings
- Koriat & Bjork (2005), undergraduates, paired associates: judgments of learning made while the answer is in view are inflated because the learner cannot discount the answer's presence; they named this foresight bias and showed it with pairs whose backward association is weaker than their forward association — [Koriat & Bjork 2005 PDF](https://newiipdm.haifa.ac.il/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/2005-Koriat-Bjork-JEPLMC-KBA.pdf)
- Koriat & Bjork (2006): both raising sensitivity to retrieval-ease cues and inducing theory-based judgments repaired the illusion, but only theory-based debiasing transferred to new items — [Koriat & Bjork 2006 PDF](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Koriat_Bjork_2006_MC.pdf)
- Mihalca, Mengelkamp, Schnotz & Paas (2015), 67 university students learning genetics in a computer environment: incomplete worked-out examples led to overestimated performance (an illusion of understanding); completion problems and conventional problems showed neither over- nor underestimation; lower-prior-knowledge students performed better with completion problems and higher-prior-knowledge students with conventional problems — [Erasmus repository record](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/91167)
- Hörnlein & Kulgemeyer (2025 preprint), two studies (n = 244 and n = 175; level not stated): high-level learning tasks after a physics explainer video reduced the illusion of understanding immediately versus video alone (t(88) = 6.50, p < .001, d = 0.69); in the long term high- and low-level tasks were similar; learners with lower prior knowledge were more susceptible — [arXiv 2512.02824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02824)
- Potts & Shanks (2014): learners judged errorful generation a less effective encoding method than reading, when it was better — [Westminster record](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/8y95x/the-benefit-of-generating-errors-during-learning)
- Huelser & Metcalfe (2012), via Metcalfe (2017): after experiencing a 20% benefit from error generation, participants still believed the error-free condition had worked better — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Metcalfe (2017): overconfidence is the overarching finding of the confidence literature (Dunlosky & Metcalfe 2009); confident learners decline further study; considering how one could be wrong offsets it — [Metcalfe 2017](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED574569.pdf)
- Retrieval practice improves the accuracy of judgments of learning: the JOL-to-accuracy correlation was higher for items practiced by testing than by restudy, mediated partly by confidence and partly by retrieval reaction time (Frontiers in Psychology 2019) — [Frontiers 2019 PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00987/pdf)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate rereading low utility and practice testing high; rereading a solution is the fluent, low-utility form — [APS summary of Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://psychologicalscience.org/?p=82014)
- Roll et al. report that high-school students attempted too often on low-skill steps (28% of actions were erroneous "inappropriate attempts"), which they attribute to overestimating their ability (Kruger & Dunning 1999) — [Roll et al. JLS manuscript PDF](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/Roll%20HT%20JLS%20clean.pdf)

### Inferences
- "The shape" rung is a completion problem in Mihalca's terms (skeleton with bodies elided), and completion problems were the condition with no over- or underestimation; that is a measured reason to keep the shape rung as a required stop rather than a preview of the solution.
  The same study says higher-prior-knowledge learners performed better with conventional problems, which for an experienced reader means the attempt-from-scratch rung matters more than the shape rung.
- Reading the full solution is the fluent, in-view condition that both foresight bias and the explainer-video study describe.
  The counter is a generation task after the reveal with the solution hidden: close the solution and rewrite the function body, or write the failing input, before moving on.
  In a `<details>` ladder the reader can collapse the solution, so the prompt can say so.
- Koriat & Bjork's transfer result argues for one short paragraph, once in the book (or once per Solutions file header), that tells the reader why a read solution feels understood: the explanation is the theory-based debiasing that transferred; a per-exercise nudge is the cue-based form that did not.
- Learners misjudge which method works (Potts & Shanks; Huelser & Metcalfe), so a sentence that says attempting and erring feels worse and works better is not decoration; it addresses a measured misbelief that otherwise steers the reader toward reading first.

### Gaps
- No programming-specific study found on overconfidence after reading a model solution; the nearest are genetics worked examples and physics videos.
- Hörnlein & Kulgemeyer (2025) is a preprint and the learners' level is not stated on the abstract page.
- No study found on whether collapsing a solution and regenerating it (the `<details>` affordance) changes calibration or retention.
