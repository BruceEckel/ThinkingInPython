# Sequencing and spacing exercises across a 47-chapter technical book

Scope: exercise-set architecture across chapters for a static Markdown book with collapsible three-rung solutions (hint, skeleton, full solution), read by experienced programmers over weeks.
The earlier round covered worked examples, completion problems, and six exercise formats, so this round goes past the single "cumulative exercises that interleave confusable patterns" line into the evidence for spacing, interleaving, variation sequences, attempt-before-instruction, pre-questions, chaining, and within-set ordering.

Reading guide for the report writer.
Most of the cognitive-psychology evidence comes from university undergraduates learning lab materials (word pairs, paintings, short math procedures, expository passages), and the strongest classroom trials are in grade 7 mathematics.
Evidence from computing education is thin and small-sample.
No study was found on experienced programmers learning a new idiom from a book.
Every "author practice" item is labelled as such.

## Spacing and retrieval: what the meta-analyses say, and what gap fits a book read over weeks

### Takeaway
Spaced retrieval is the best-supported technique in the whole literature (practice testing g about 0.50, spacing rated "high utility"), and the usable rule for a book is that the optimal gap grows with how long the reader must keep the skill while shrinking as a fraction of it: roughly 20 to 40 percent of a one-week horizon, 5 to 10 percent of a one-year horizon, with Dunlosky's "about 15 percent" as a serviceable midpoint.
Textbooks that return to earlier material are rare, the one controlled test of a "spiral" sequence found spaced review (and not repetition or the spiral shape) carried the gain, and the three concrete instructional recommendations from the spacing researchers all translate into exercise placement.

### Cited Findings
- Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132, reviewed 839 assessments of distributed practice across 317 experiments in 184 articles; the interstudy interval (ISI) and the retention interval act jointly, and the ISI producing maximal retention increased as the retention interval increased. Most included studies used brief gaps and brief retention intervals (seconds to minutes). — [Cepeda et al. 2006 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf); [York publication page](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html)
- Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), *Psychological Science*: more than 1,350 adults learned facts, reviewed them after gaps up to 3.5 months, and were tested up to a year later. At any given test delay, lengthening the gap first raised and then gradually lowered final-test performance (a "ridgeline"). The optimal gap in absolute terms grew with test delay, but as a proportion of the delay it fell from about 20 to 40 percent of a one-week delay to about 5 to 10 percent of a one-year delay. The authors wrote that this interaction "implies that many educational practices are highly inefficient." — [Cepeda et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf); [USF abstract](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1766/)
- The York press release on that study quotes Cepeda suggesting a gap of about 20 percent of the interval (several days for an exam three weeks away) and nearer 5 percent for recall several years out. — [York press release](https://www.yorku.ca/yfile/?p=9454)
- Cepeda et al. (2009), "Optimizing distributed practice," used test delays up to six months and found that an optimal gap improved final recall by up to 150 percent over a poor gap. — [Cepeda et al. 2009 PDF](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/Cepedaetal2009.pdf)
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), *PSPI* 14(1), rated practice testing and distributed practice "high utility" because they help learners of different ages and abilities across many criterion tasks, including classroom settings; interleaved practice, elaborative interrogation, and self-explanation received "moderate" because the evidence base was still limited; summarization, highlighting, and rereading were rated low. The review's spacing guideline: separate the original learning and the review by about 15 percent of the desired retention interval (to retain something for 150 days, space sessions 10 days apart). It also notes students are often unaware they benefited from spacing. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [Stafforini summary](https://stafforini.com/works/dunlosky-2013-improving-students-learning/)
- A secondary source attributes to Rohrer & Pashler (2007) the guideline that the study-to-restudy gap should be 10 to 30 percent of the time until the material is needed. — [The Emotional Learner summary](https://theemotionallearner.com/2024/11/02/unpacking-the-spacing-effect/) (secondary; the 2007 paper is at [gwern copy](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2007-rohrer.pdf), not opened)
- Rowland (2014), *Psychological Bulletin*, 159 effect sizes from 61 studies: testing beat restudy with an overall effect of about g = 0.50; initial recall tests gave larger benefits than recognition tests; benefits were larger with feedback and at retention intervals of a day or more. Rowland excluded applied classroom research, and Adesope et al. (2017), which included it, found g = 0.61. — [Rowland 2014 PDF](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf); [Frontiers 2018 review citing both](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full). The g = 0.73 with feedback versus 0.39 without figure appears only in a blog and is unverified. — [StudyCards blog](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics)
- Carpenter, Cepeda, Rohrer, Kang & Pashler (2012), *Educational Psychology Review* 24(3), 369-378, reports Carpenter et al. (2009): eighth graders who reviewed US-history questions 16 weeks after the course (after summer) retained more nine months later than those who reviewed one week after it. The review states that "many textbooks present information in a nondistributed fashion": foreign-language textbooks confine each chapter's vocabulary to one topic that "rarely appear[s] in subsequent chapters," and "in most mathematics textbooks, each set of problems is devoted to the most recent lesson," so instructors "may find it necessary to supplement" a lesson with examples from earlier lessons. Its three recommendations: (1) build into each lesson a brief review of concepts learned several weeks earlier; (2) put questions on earlier material into homework; (3) make quizzes and exams cumulative, which also gives students a reason to review on their own. It adds that for long-term retention the review should come "after a time period of at least several weeks," and that forgetting before a review is expected: re-exposed material is relearned much faster than new material. — [Carpenter et al. 2012, USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1756) (quotes extracted from the [gwern-hosted PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/laplab.ucsd.edu/9caf1988b2238f23ddb55be5b756d0cee4e7bd33.pdf))
- Rohrer, Dedrick & Hartwig (2020), *Educational Psychology Review*, "The scarcity of interleaved practice in mathematics textbooks": across six seventh-grade texts evaluated in 2016, 78 percent of practice problems were blocked, 11 percent interleaved, 11 percent unclassifiable; most texts do offer some mixed practice as "chapter reviews, mixed reviews, or spiral reviews," but even those "often consist of several small blocks." — [Rohrer et al. 2019/2020 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-020-09516-2)
- Scientific American's account claims that of US math textbooks "all but one type—the Saxon series—uses blocked practice." The peer-reviewed textbook analyses above do not single out Saxon, so the Saxon exception is popular-press, not measured. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-interleaving-effect-mixing-it-up-boosts-learning/)
- Spiral curriculum (Bruner): no meta-analysis or systematic review testing the spiral was found. One older controlled study compared a conventional course, a linear programmed version, and a spiral programmed version; the spiral offered "few, if any, advantages" over the linear program, and a third experiment separating repetition from spaced review found that spaced review raised learning and retention while repetition did not. — search result attributed to [IIT digital library item](https://digital2.library.iit.edu/item/160276) (attribution from a search snippet; verify the citation before quoting it). Publisher evidence for Everyday Mathematics' spiral is the publisher's own white paper. — [McGraw Hill](https://www.mheducation.com/prek-12/resources/research/library/math/the-research-behind-the-everyday-mathematics-spiral.html). A practitioner summary argues the "backbone of effective spiral review" is interleaved plus spaced practice. — [NWEA blog](https://www.nwea.org/blog/?p=25842)
- Spaced retrieval practice in nine introductory STEM courses (none CS) found significant gains on the criterial test in two courses (Calculus I for Engineers, Chemistry for Health Professionals) and small positive effects in two more; the authors call it "a low-cost method of improving student performance in at least some STEM courses." — [IJ STEM Education 2024](https://doaj.org/article/13125faeb142423695a0e121ef8777ef)
- CS1 (Colorado State, Moraes et al.): quizzes were redesigned as retakeable low-stakes practice so students could test themselves "in a spaced and interleaved manner," with visualizations of their own study habits; outcomes (final exams, course grade) were compared for students who did and did not space, but the retrieved abstract stops before the result. — [Mountain Scholar record](https://mountainscholar.org/items/55f9d5e8-8575-45e5-962b-bb563f71d9b4)
- SIGCSE 2022 quasi-experiment in an introductory programming course: many small ungraded in-class retrieval questions, hypothesized to raise study time and spacing; the title claims improved grades, the retrieved abstract does not show the numbers. — [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10222132)
- Spacing out retrieval practice "increases the difficulty of the exercises, reducing student performance on them" in the moment, which the authors argue pays off later; a vocabulary study found spaced retrieval works only once learners have enough prior repetitions to succeed at retrieving. — [NSF PAR 10544324](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10544324)

### Inferences
- A book read over weeks has a natural horizon: a reader working through Part III (Patterns) wants the Part I and II skills for the rest of the book and beyond, so a retention interval of months applies, and the 5 to 15 percent rule puts the first return to a chapter's skill one to three chapters later, with a second return a part later. A return every chapter is too dense by this rule; a return once at the end of the book is too sparse.
- Carpenter's three recommendations map to a static book as: (1) a "from earlier chapters" subsection in each chapter's exercise set holding one or two retrieval exercises on skills from two to four chapters back; (2) phrasing those as recall-then-apply (write the idiom from memory before using it) rather than recognition, since Rowland found recall tests beat recognition tests; (3) a cumulative exercise at each part boundary that needs several chapters' skills in one program, which is also the one place a part-level "exam" fits a book.
- The collapsible ladder supplies the feedback condition Rowland found matters: a review exercise whose hint names the earlier chapter's section, and whose full solution is one click away, is retrieval with delayed feedback, not restudy.
- Expect readers to have forgotten; Carpenter's point that relearning is fast means a review exercise whose hint rung restates the earlier idiom in one line is a legitimate design, not a concession.
- The in-the-moment cost is real: a spaced review exercise feels harder than a same-chapter exercise and readers may misjudge it as less useful (Dunlosky's unawareness finding, Samani & Pan's physics students below). A one-line note at the head of a review subsection saying why it is there is cheap and addresses that.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measured review exercises placed in a textbook, as opposed to instructor-added review or quizzes; the textbook evidence is a content analysis showing they are rare, plus a programmed-instruction study of uncertain citation.
- No spacing study on adults learning programming idioms; the horizon figures come from fact learning in adults and classroom learning in children.
- The 2008 ridgeline was measured with a single review; a book can return to a skill several times, and the optimal schedule for repeated expanding returns across months is not settled in the sources found.

## Interleaving: when it beats blocking, and whether that holds for design patterns

### Takeaway
Interleaving's pooled effect is moderate (g = 0.42) but it is largest where categories are hard to tell apart (paintings g = 0.67; effect rises with between-category similarity and material complexity) and small or absent for mathematics (g = 0.34), expository text (nonsignificant), and words; the classroom trials in grade 7 math are large (d = 0.83 to 1.05) when the interleaved problems are ones students must first classify. Design patterns that share a class diagram (State and Strategy, Proxy and Decorator) are the textbook case of low discriminability, and teaching reports confirm students confuse them, but no controlled interleaving study on patterns or on programming exists.

### Cited Findings
- Brunmair & Richter (2019), *Psychological Bulletin*, "Similarity matters": 59 studies, 238 effect sizes in 158 samples; overall interleaving effect g = 0.42 (95% CI [0.34, 0.50]); after a trim-and-fill correction g = 0.36. By material: paintings g = 0.67 [0.57, 0.77], naturalistic photographs g = 0.35, mathematical tasks g = 0.34 (p = .005, [0.11, 0.57]), expository texts and tastes nonsignificant. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 77.3% overall; 76.9% for math tasks). — [Brunmair & Richter accepted manuscript PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Same paper, moderators: "the interleaving effect increased with similarity between categories and with the complexity of the material"; within-category similarity reduced it (model coefficients: between-category similarity +0.41, within-category similarity -0.33, complexity +0.06 to +0.15 across models). The theory it supports (attentional bias / discriminative contrast) predicts interleaving wins for "low-discriminability categories in which all exemplars presented during learning are highly similar to each other," and blocking wins "in high-discriminability categories in which all exemplars are very different." The effect was "fragile when categories were highly discriminable, when classification of categories required rule-based learning (Noh, Yan, Bjork & Maddox, 2016), or when items were studied in passive learning conditions." A significant effect appeared for immediate succession of items (g = 0.73) but not for temporally spaced items (g = 0.22, CI crosses zero). — [same PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Same paper, cautions: "We are reluctant to recommend interleaving for learning materials such as mathematical tasks, expository texts, grammar rules, foreign languages or words ... it could even impede learning compared to a blocked presentation." Most studies used university students; "educationally interesting materials, such as mathematical tasks or expository texts, are clearly underrepresented." The authors attribute the mixed math results partly to math tasks being interleavable "by different dimensions of categories." — [same PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); the Frontiers corrigendum describes the math effect as "small, significant" and attributes it to procedural knowledge. — [Frontiers corrigendum](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02296/full)
- A Strathclyde systematic review of interleaving as a concept-learning strategy found interleaving "of greatest use when differences between items are subtle." — [Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/75711)
- Rohrer, Dedrick & Burgess (2014): 140 grade 7 students, nine weeks of blocked or interleaved math practice, unannounced test two weeks later: 72% versus 38%, d = 1.05. — [Rohrer et al. 2014 PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2014-rohrer.pdf)
- Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020), randomized trial, 787 students in 54 classes, 15 teachers, 5 schools: interleaved assignments beat blocked ones, d = 0.83, 95% CI [0.68, 0.97]. A WestEd replication in US middle schools is under way. — [IES award page](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/interleaved-mathematics-practice); [IES replication study](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/systematic-replication-study-interleaved-mathematics-practice?ID=5754); [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2021/10/28-1)
- Rohrer's mechanism claim: interleaving "prevents students from safely assuming that each practice problem relates to the same skill or concept as the previous problem, thus forcing them to choose an appropriate strategy on the basis of the problem itself." — [EdWorkingPaper AI23-876 (quoting Rohrer)](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai23-876)
- Year-long randomized trial in 62 Nigerian middle-school math classrooms: interleaving raised short-term retention scores by 0.29 SD but showed no evidence of improving the year-end cumulative assessment. — [EdWorkingPaper AI23-876](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai23-876)
- Samani & Pan (2021), eight-week study in an introductory undergraduate physics course: interleaved homework beat topic-by-topic homework on surprise tests, and students rated the interleaved homework as harder and wrongly believed they learned less from it. — [npj Science of Learning via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8589969/)
- Yan et al. (2021) note that in the motor-skills literature "interleaved practice is not as effective for complex skills or for novice learners," with initial blocked practice needed first, while reporting no case where blocking beat interleaving in their own review. A chemistry-education article reports the same pattern for numerical problems: "topics may require extensive blocked practice before students can engage with the benefits of interleaved practice." — [Yan 2021 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-yan.pdf); [RSC Education in Chemistry](https://edu.rsc.org/education-research/tweak-your-questions-to-improve-students-problem-solving-skills/4015382.article)
- Programming: the only direct test found is a 2020 UC Berkeley MIMS student project reporting that an "interleaved study pattern works best for the programming task"; it is a brief, unreviewed write-up. — [Berkeley MIMS project](https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/projects/2020/statistical-analysis-procedural-learning-blocked-vs-interleaved-humans). No ICER, SIGCSE, ITiCSE, or Koli Calling paper on interleaved practice in programming surfaced in any search.
- Design-pattern confusability (teaching reports, not controlled studies): a 2025 arXiv teaching study of patterns via the Aronson jigsaw method lists Strategy being "frequently confused with Decorator" because both use composition, and the structural group (Adapter, Decorator, Proxy, Facade) causing confusion through "overlapping intent." — [arXiv 2508.16770](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.16770). An Open University (NL) student thesis is titled "Misconceptions about design patterns." — [OU thesis record](https://research.ou.nl/en/studentTheses/misconceptions-about-design-patterns/). Practitioner sources: Proxy and Decorator "are often confused and mislabeled, mostly because of the similarity in their implementation," separated by intent (control access versus add responsibilities). — [Educative](https://www.educative.io/courses/nodejs-design-patterns/the-line-between-the-proxy-and-decorator-pattern). State and Strategy "have nearly identical class diagrams, and the difference is intent." — [techinterview cheat sheet](https://www.techinterview.org/post/3233476917/design-patterns-cheat-sheet/). Iglberger's C++ talks list Decorator mistaken for Adapter and Bridge mixed up with Strategy. — [Meeting C++ 2024](https://meetingcpp.com/2024/Talks/items/Design_Patterns___The_Most_Common_Misconceptions__2_of_N_.html)

### Inferences
- The conditions under which interleaving wins (similar-looking categories, complex material, active classification by the learner, items in immediate succession) describe design patterns closely: State and Strategy share a diagram, Proxy and Decorator share an implementation, and the learner's task is to pick the pattern from the problem. So the Patterns part is where interleaved exercises have the strongest theoretical warrant, and the "name the pattern this problem needs, then build it" shape is the one Rohrer's trials used (choose the strategy from the problem, then execute it).
- The same meta-analysis warns against interleaving procedural or text material for its own sake, and the novice literature says block first. For a chapter teaching a new idiom (a Part I or II chapter), the chapter's own exercises should stay blocked on that idiom, and interleaving belongs in later chapters' review subsections and in part-boundary cumulative exercises, once the reader has done the blocked practice.
- Brunmair & Richter's finding that the effect holds for immediate succession (g = 0.73) but not temporally spaced items (g = 0.22) means an interleaved exercise should put the confusable cases side by side in one exercise or one adjacent pair ("here are three requirements; which is State, which is Strategy, which needs neither; implement the two that do"), rather than relying on the pattern being from an earlier chapter to supply the contrast.
- The Nigeria result (short-term gain, no cumulative-exam gain) and Dunlosky's "moderate" rating mean interleaving is a supplement to spacing and retrieval, not a replacement.
- Readers will rate interleaved exercises harder and less useful (Samani & Pan); the hint rung can name the discrimination the exercise is testing after the reader has attempted it.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of interleaved practice on design patterns, on software-design categories, or on programming idioms for any population.
- No evidence on interleaving for experienced adult learners outside university samples (Brunmair & Richter flag "adults beyond the college age" as untested).
- How many patterns to interleave in one exercise, and whether interleaving across Parts (a Functional-part exercise that also needs a Pattern) helps or overloads, is unmeasured.

## Variation theory: holding structure and varying one aspect at a time

### Takeaway
Variation theory (Marton) has a documented programming-education lineage (Eckerdal & Thuné, 2005 and 2009, on novices' conceptions of object and class; Lam 2022's four design principles) and a practitioner presence in UK computing teaching, but the evidence is phenomenographic and design-based rather than controlled, and no study combines it with PRIMM or measures variation-sequenced exercise sets in programming.

### Cited Findings
- Variation theory "emphasizes variation as a necessary condition for learners to be able to discern new aspects of an object of learning"; Lam (2022) turned it into four software-design principles: aim for learning rather than testing, focus on one specific object of learning, let learners explore the variation, keep everything else invariant. — [Lam 2022, EdUHK repository](https://repository.eduhk.hk/en/publications/revisiting-variation-affordance-applying-variation-theory-in-the-/)
- Eckerdal & Thuné (ITiCSE 2005), "Novice Java programmers' conceptions of 'object' and 'class', and variation theory"; Thuné & Eckerdal (2009), *European Journal of Engineering Education*, "Variation theory applied to students' conceptions of computer programming." Eckerdal's 2009 doctoral thesis (Uppsala) concludes that "activities at a higher level of proficiency, as well as qualitatively richer understandings of concepts, relate to more dimensions of variation," gives "concrete examples ... on how variation theory and patterns of variation can be applied in teaching programming," and suggests educators help students "discern dimensions of variation." The thesis's method is phenomenographic (interview-based categories of conception), not experimental. — [Eckerdal thesis PDF](https://user.it.uu.se/~annae/FullAvh-Spikenheten.pdf); [Uppsala profile](https://katalog.uu.se/profile/?id=N99-802); [thesis record](https://www.avhandlingar.se/avhandling/40098d8edc/)
- A syntax-correction system for OOP applied the four variation patterns (generation, contrast, separation, fusion); this is a design paper, not a learning-outcome study. — [i-JET article](https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-jet/article/download/14191/7495/53163)
- William Lau (UK teacher) presented variation theory in computing education at Glasgow's CCSE and noted that "most computing teacher training programs do not cover variation theory at all." — [Glasgow CCSE page](https://www.gla.ac.uk/research/az/ccse/activities/headline_1149449_en.html)
- PRIMM (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make) is grounded by Sentance and colleagues (2019, *Computer Science Education*) in Vygotsky's sociocultural theory, not in variation theory; the Raspberry Pi Foundation describes it as building understanding before learners write their own code. — [QMUL repository](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/handle/123456789/57800); [Raspberry Pi course page](https://www.raspberrypi.org/?p=92629)
- The one artefact linking variation theory to try-first exercise design is a practitioner "skill" file suggesting try-first prompts to find which critical aspects learners can already discern, then variation patterns for the rest; it is not peer-reviewed. — [dbosk variation-theory skill](https://skills.cat/skills/dbosk/claude-skills/variation-theory)
- Related evidence from the interleaving literature: Brunmair & Richter attribute mixed math results to tasks being interleavable "by different dimensions of categories" (Rau et al. 2013), which is the same question variation theory asks about which dimension to vary. — [Brunmair & Richter PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)

### Inferences
- The variation patterns (contrast, generalization, separation, fusion) give a vocabulary for ordering a chapter's exercises: contrast (the idiom against its nearest alternative), generalization (the same idiom across different data), separation (vary one parameter of the idiom while the program stays the same), fusion (vary two at once in the stretch exercise). This is a design heuristic with phenomenographic backing, not a measured effect.
- Variation sequences and interleaving address the same thing (discerning the critical aspect) from two sides: a variation sequence varies one dimension within one concept; an interleaved set varies the concept and holds the problem shape. A chapter's own set can run a variation sequence; the review subsection can run the interleaved contrast.
- A three-rung ladder supports "keep everything else invariant": the skeleton rung can be the invariant structure, so that a sequence of exercises shares one skeleton and changes one line's worth of demand.

### Gaps
- No controlled study measuring variation-sequenced programming exercises against unsequenced ones, for any population; the programming evidence is interview-based studies of novices.
- No study links variation theory to PRIMM, to Parsons problems with systematic variation, or to Kölling's work; searches on those names returned nothing relevant.

## Productive failure and invention before instruction: does attempting before reading help, and for whom?

### Takeaway
Problem-solving before instruction (PS-I) shows a moderate pooled advantage over instruction-first (g = 0.36, 95% CI [0.20, 0.51]; g = 0.37 to 0.58 with high fidelity to productive-failure principles) for conceptual knowledge and transfer in STEM, reversed for second-to-fifth graders and for domain-general skills, and instruction-first wins for procedural fluency; CS evidence is a handful of studies with 16 to 20 participants, one favouring PF on a delayed test. The condition that matters for a book is that the attempt comes before the explanation and the explanation then consolidates, so the "attempt first" exercise has to be pointed to from the chapter text before the section and answered by the section.

### Cited Findings
- Sinha & Kapur (2021), *Review of Educational Research*: 53 studies, 166 comparisons; PS-I beat I-PS with Hedges' g = 0.36 [0.20, 0.51]; after correcting for publication bias the estimate was larger (g = 0.87). Grade level, intervention length, and (quasi-)experimental design moderated the effect; high fidelity to productive-failure principles gave g = 0.37 to 0.58; effects reversed (favouring I-PS) for younger learners (grades 2 to 5) and for domain-general skills. Scope was conceptual understanding and application in STEM. — [RER abstract (mirror)](https://https-sage-cnpereading-com-443.webvpn1.xju.edu.cn/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105); Kapur's own framing: "nearly twice the effect size of receiving a year of instruction from a good teacher," up to three times with high fidelity. — [Times Higher Education piece by Kapur](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/campus/using-productive-failure-activate-deeper-learning)
- Sinha & Kapur's comparative meta-analysis of preparatory designs: scaffolded PS-I versus unscaffolded PS-I showed no significant difference (g = -0.08 [-0.20, 0.04]); PS-I versus sensemaking preparation showed a nonsignificant g = 0.22 [-0.06, 0.51]. — [ETH research collection](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/entities/publication/087241d3-ca0e-469e-a7f3-df9c122e2585)
- Loibl, Roll & Rummel (2017), *Educational Psychology Review*, is the standard theoretical review of PS-I; its proposed mechanisms are that during the problem-solving phase students "activate their prior knowledge, become aware of their knowledge gaps, and discover deep features of the target content." A 2025 review in the same journal states there is "very limited empirical evidence directly testing how instructional sequences differentially affect students with different levels of prior knowledge." — [Educ Psychol Rev 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10074-8)
- Loibl, Tillema, Rummel & van Gog (2020): "for procedural fluency instruction prior to problem solving was more beneficial than problem solving prior to instruction," and "merely delaying instruction did not increase conceptual understanding." — [Instructional Science 2020](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11251-020-09528-z)
- Hartmann, van Gog & Rummel found "no conclusive evidence that the beneficial effects of PF are explained by the attempt to generate one's own solutions prior to instruction," testing whether observing others' failed attempts works as well. — [Instructional Science](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11251-020-09504-7)
- Schwartz & Martin (2004), *Cognition and Instruction* 22(2), 129-184, "Inventing to prepare for future learning: the hidden efficiency of encouraging original student production in statistics instruction" (the source paper for invention tasks; full text not retrieved). A 2022 *Instructional Science* study with 45 pre-service teachers found "a worked-out inventing problem seems to prepare future learning more effectively than an open inventing activity," which qualifies the open-invention design. — [Instructional Science 2022](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11251-022-09577-6); Schwartz's group frames the outcome as "how well it prepares students to keep learning." — [Stanford H-STAR](https://hstar.stanford.edu/research/improving-and-assessing-learners-preparation-for-future-learning-in-science)
- CS1, Suriyaarachchi, Denny & Nanayakkara (SIGCSE TS 2025): 20 undergraduates new to programming, within-subjects, one DI session and one PF session two weeks apart, Python lists; in PF students first attempted an open-ended "weather station" task, then a consolidation phase discussed their solutions; "students exposed to the PF approach outperformed their DI counterparts in terms of knowledge retention and task performance on the delayed follow-up"; the authors call for work "at scale across a range of concepts." Earlier CS work (sorting, graph theory in Algot and Snap!) reported gains in constructive reasoning and curiosity with "inconclusive evidence on the impact of PF on learner performance and conceptual understanding." — [arXiv 2411.11227](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11227); [SIGCSE TS 2025 listing](https://sigcse2025.sigcse.org/details/sigcse-ts-2025-Papers/53/Investigating-the-Use-of-Productive-Failure-as-a-Design-Paradigm-for-Learning-Introdu)
- An earlier CS1 PF paper randomized 16 students (DI n = 9, PF n = 7); a reviewer flagged the sample as "quite small." — [Teaching CS substack](https://teachingcs.substack.com/p/productive-failure). Izu, Ng & Weerasinghe (PPIG 2022) compare PF and mastery learning conceptually for CS1. — [PPIG 2022 PDF](https://ppig.org:443/files/2022-PPIG-33rd-izu.pdf)

### Inferences
- The population closest to this book's reader (adults, conceptual STEM content, high fidelity) is where PS-I's effect is largest, and the reversals (young children, domain-general skills, procedural fluency) do not describe it. Prior-knowledge moderation, the thing one would most want to know for experienced programmers, is untested.
- Productive failure's structure is attempt, then consolidating instruction that contrasts the reader's attempt with the canonical solution. In a static book that is: an "attempt first" exercise pointed to at the start of a section, with the section written to name the typical failed attempts and show why the canonical form resolves them. The collapsible solution then serves as a second consolidation, and its explanation rung can open with "a first attempt usually does X."
- Loibl 2020's procedural-fluency result says this placement suits a design idea (why `Protocol` over an ABC, when a Visitor beats a `match`) and does not suit syntax or API mechanics, where instruction-first is better.
- The 2022 worked-out-inventing result and the scaffolding-equivalence result (g = -0.08) mean the attempt exercise need not be fully open; a skeleton with a stated goal and no guidance on the design decision keeps the generation step while bounding the time cost.

### Gaps
- No PS-I study on experienced programmers or on a self-paced book; every CS study is on novices with an instructor-run consolidation phase.
- Whether a reader will do an attempt exercise before reading the section, with the explanation visible on the next screen, is a behavioural question no source addresses.
- Prior knowledge as a moderator of PS-I is explicitly listed as under-tested in the 2025 review.

## Pre-questions and reading-to-solve: where the exercise pointer sits in the chapter

### Takeaway
Prequestions before text and video reliably raise learning of the prequestioned content (and pretesting beat posttesting by about d = 0.30 across five experiments with 1,573 participants on expository passages); for long lecture material the benefit is concentrated on the questioned content, with little or no spillover, and wrong answers do not hurt. So a pointer placed before a section works when the exercise targets the section's key point, and it earns its place only for the content the author most wants retained.

### Cited Findings
- Carpenter & Toftness (2017): students watched a roughly six-minute video on Easter Island in three segments; half answered two prequestions before each segment, "nearly always failed to answer the prequestions correctly," and got no feedback; the prequestion group still scored higher on post-video questions covering both prequestioned and other content. — [Griffith University summary](https://app.secure.griffith.edu.au/exlnt/entry/4205/view)
- Toftness, Carpenter, Lauber & Mickes (2018), *JARMAC* 7, "The limited effects of prequestions on learning from authentic lecture videos": with 20-plus-minute lectures (signal detection theory, autobiographical memory) the prequestion group beat controls overall and on prequestioned information; non-prequestioned content showed no difference in one experiment and a marginal edge in the other; the benefit did not depend on topic interest. — [Toftness et al. 2018 PDF](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/files/30437641/JARMAC_Toftness_et_al.pdf); [Royal Holloway record](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/the-limited-effects-of-prequestions-on-learning-from-authentic-le/)
- Pan & Sana (2021), *JEP: Applied* 27, 237-257, five experiments, combined n = 1,573, expository text passages each paired with a pretest or a posttest: "pretesting is highly competitive with posttesting and can yield similar, if not greater, pedagogical benefits," across test formats, with or without feedback, and across retention intervals, attributed to deeper processing of the passage. — [Athabasca record](https://pure.athabascau.ca/en/publications/pretesting-versus-posttesting-comparing-the-pedagogical-benefits-/). A later paper reports the pretesting advantage over retrieval practice as d = 0.30. — [Memory & Cognition 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-022-01392-1)
- Rivers, Berdelis & Pan: covert and overt prequestions both beat reading alone, with no reliable difference between them. — [same Memory & Cognition 2022 article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-022-01392-1)
- Mechanisms cited: pretests "enhance attentional processing of pretested information by directing attention to key ... information," and there is "an even greater learning benefit when learners attempt to respond to the pretest questions, even though they score below chance." — [Structural Learning summary](https://www.structural-learning.com/post/pretesting-effect-testing-before-teaching); benefit "occurs even when, indeed especially when, learners answer pretest questions incorrectly." — [Ollie Lovell summary](https://www.ollielovell.com/how-you-can-use-prequestioning-to-boost-student-learning/)
- Pan & Carpenter's review of prequestioning (Educational Psychology Review, 2023, doi 10.1007/s10648-023-09805-6) exists and is cited as offering recommendations; the full text was behind a login and its findings were not retrieved. — [Springer link (paywalled)](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-023-09805-6)
- Rohrer's and Carpenter's textbook observation that end-of-lesson problem sets are the norm (see the spacing section) is the baseline against which a pre-section pointer is a departure. — [Carpenter et al. 2012](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1756)

### Inferences
- A pre-section pointer ("before reading on, try exercise 3") is a prequestion whose question is a programming task; the evidence predicts a gain on what that task targets and little elsewhere, so the pointer should go in front of the section whose idea the author most wants kept, once per chapter at most, not in front of every section.
- The no-feedback result means the pointer does not need the reader to succeed or to check the solution before reading; the ladder's hint and skeleton rungs can stay collapsed until after the section.
- Pre-questions and productive failure converge on the same placement (before the explanation) with different mechanisms (attention to the key point versus awareness of a knowledge gap); for a book the practical difference is length: a prequestion is a one-line task the reader thinks about for a minute, an attempt-first exercise is a program the reader writes.
- Posttest-style end-of-chapter exercises remain the right default for everything the pointer does not target, since pretesting and posttesting were close in effect and posttesting needs no restructuring of the text.

### Gaps
- No study of prequestions on programming text or code reading; the materials were history, psychology, and expository science passages.
- The effect of a pointer that the reader can ignore (a book, not a controlled session) is unmeasured.
- The Pan & Carpenter 2023 review's recommendations were not retrieved.

## Chaining: exercises that extend the reader's own earlier solution across chapters

### Takeaway
No learning-science study of cross-chapter chained exercises was found; the evidence is author practice (Crafting Interpreters keeps the main thread strictly cumulative, supplies every line of code, and tells the reader to do challenges in a copy because later chapters assume the main code is unchanged) plus course studies showing that milestones in cumulative projects improve completion and that cumulative courses strand students who fall behind.

### Cited Findings
- Crafting Interpreters (Nystrom), author practice: each chapter adds one feature to the interpreter the reader is building; "each chapter depends only on earlier chapters"; from the first chapter "you have a working program you can run and play with"; "every single line of code needed is included"; some early code is temporary and replaced later; challenges are optional and separate from the main path, and because many "require changing the interpreter, you should make those changes in a copy of your code. Later chapters assume your interpreter is unchanged"; asides can be skipped because later parts do not depend on them. — [Crafting Interpreters, Introduction](https://craftinginterpreters.com/introduction.html)
- Shaffer & Kazerouni (2021), third-year data structures course: students given milestones "were more likely to finish their projects on time, produced projects with higher correctness, and finished the course with generally better outcomes," with no rise in withdrawal or failure. — [Codio summary](https://www.codio.com/blog/dividing-large-programming-projects-into-smaller-steps) (secondary; the primary paper was not retrieved)
- Erickson, Heckman & Lynch (NC State, SIGCSE 2022) compute 15 progress indicators from automated-assessment data across four milestone-split stages of two CS2 projects to see whether students meet each milestone's goals. — [NSF PAR PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10387187)
- University of Toronto's CSC108 description: course material "is cumulative week-to-week, which presents challenges for students who fall behind, requiring them to catch up with previous material before continuing," the stated motivation for a mastery-learning redesign. — [UofT teaching event page](https://teaching.utoronto.ca/event/mastery-learning-in-an-introductory-computer-science-course/)
- No source was found describing a reference solution released at each milestone and its effect on students who fell behind; the search on that design returned nothing.

### Inferences
- Crafting Interpreters' rules are the documented way to keep a chained thread from stranding a reader: the chain lives in the main text with complete code, and exercises are kept off the chain. Chaining exercises across chapters inverts that: the chain lives in the reader's own exercise code, which the book cannot see, so the stranding risk the book avoids in the text reappears in the exercises.
- A static book with a full-solution rung has a cheap fix the course studies hint at (milestones with a known-good state): a chapter-N exercise that extends a chapter-M solution can say "start from your own chapter M solution, or from the full solution in Solutions/M," so a reader who skipped M, or whose version diverged, has a reference starting point. The cost is that the chapter-N exercise must be written against the published M solution's interface, which then becomes a contract the author maintains.
- Chaining buys spacing for free (the reader retrieves and reads their chapter-M code at a gap of N minus M chapters) and is the one exercise form that also exercises the professional skill of extending existing code. It is the strongest candidate for the "from earlier chapters" slot, limited to one or two chains per part.
- Chains should run within a Part, where the ordering is linear and readers are less likely to skip, and should not cross Part boundaries, where a reader who starts at Functional or Effects has no chapter-M code at all.

### Gaps
- No measured comparison of chained versus independent exercises for learning, retention, or completion in any population.
- No data on how many readers of a technical book keep their exercise code between chapters; the stranding cost is argued, not measured.
- SICP's register machine and other "same program grows through the book" designs were not retrieved in this round; Crafting Interpreters is the one documented case.

## Difficulty progression within a chapter's set: ramp, mixed, or marked core and stretch

### Takeaway
The lab evidence on ordering is mixed and task-dependent: easy-to-hard, hard-to-easy, and random orders were equal for rule-based (verbalizable) categories, hard-first won only for non-verbalizable ones, random order reduced categorization errors on isomorphic items but not transfer items, and an easy-to-difficult progression was not consistently beneficial under load. Nothing found tests marking exercises as core versus stretch beyond the rating schemes the earlier round covered.

### Cited Findings
- Spiering & Ashby (2008), *Psychological Science*, "Initial training with difficult items facilitates information integration, but not rule-based category learning": easy-to-hard, hard-to-easy, and random orders were "equally effective" when the category rule could be stated verbally; when it could not, starting with the hardest items did better, because easy-first learners "were able to come up with simple rules ... which worked for the easy problems, but were not applicable to more complicated problems." — [PMC 2605282](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2605282); [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/using-challenging-concepts-to-learn-promotes-understanding-of-new-material.html)
- Probability word problems, blocked versus random order: random order "reduced participants' total number of categorization errors" on isomorphic test items, but "for transfer test items, ordering did not affect categorization errors"; the authors read this as random order helping schema formation but not schema restructuring. — [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt76g9s17q/qt76g9s17q.pdf)
- A 2014 study of easy-to-difficult versus difficult-to-easy progressions found "an easy-to difficult progression did not consistently induce implicit learning processes and was not consistently beneficial to performance under a secondary-task load"; timing of errors relative to difficulty affected transfer. — [PubMed 24857405](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24857405)
- Daniels et al. (2022), *Journal of Intelligence*, compared a progression of increasingly hard Rubik's cubes, a variable-order condition, and a consistent-difficulty condition; results were not in the retrieved excerpt. — [PMC 9590082](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9590082)
- Machine-learning curriculum studies report ordering effects "can both be positive or negative, depending on the metric of hardness." — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2d5972zq)
- From the interleaving literature: Brunmair & Richter found the interleaving effect "fragile ... when classification of categories required rule-based learning," which matches Spiering & Ashby's rule-based null. — [Brunmair & Richter PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Yan et al. (2021) and the chemistry-education review both report that novices and complex skills need blocked (easier, single-type) practice before mixed practice pays off. — [Yan 2021 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2021-yan.pdf); [RSC Education in Chemistry](https://edu.rsc.org/education-research/tweak-your-questions-to-improve-students-problem-solving-skills/4015382.article)

### Inferences
- For a chapter whose idea is rule-like and stateable (most Python idioms and patterns, which the chapter states in prose), the order within the set probably matters little for learning, and a ramp is justified on completion and motivation grounds rather than on learning evidence. The one case where hard-first has evidence is an idea the reader cannot verbalize from the text, which is rare in a technical book.
- The novice-first-blocked finding supports a within-chapter shape of: blocked practice on the chapter's idiom first (a short ramp), then the contrast or interleaved exercise, then the review-from-earlier-chapters exercise, then the stretch. That ordering follows from the interleaving and spacing evidence, not from ordering studies.
- Marking core versus stretch is unmeasured here; the only related evidence is the milestones result (a stated known-good checkpoint improves completion), which argues for marking which exercises a reader must do before the next chapter's review exercise will make sense.

### Gaps
- No study of exercise ordering within a textbook chapter for any subject, and none on marking core versus stretch; the earlier round's Knuth-style ratings and Bloom labels remain the only documented practice.
- Daniels et al. 2022's result was not retrieved.
- Nothing on adult professionals; the ordering studies are lab tasks with undergraduates.
