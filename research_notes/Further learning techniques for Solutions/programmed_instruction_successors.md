# Programmed instruction and its successors, as sources of techniques for a static Markdown solutions ladder

Scope note.
This round covers the programmed-instruction lineage (Skinner, Crowder, mastery learning, adjunct questions, the feedback literature that grew out of programmed texts) and what survives of it in static technical books.
Worked examples, expertise reversal, completion problems, subgoal labels, and the six exercise formats were covered in the prior round and are left out here.
Where a figure comes from a secondary summary rather than the paper, the entry says so.
SAGE blocked every direct fetch of a *Review of Educational Research* abstract (HTTP 403), so the Kulik-group numbers below are either from the papers' own text where an open copy existed (Hattie & Timperley 2007, St. Hilaire et al. 2024, Smith & Kimball 2010) or from secondary sources, flagged as such.

## Key question 1: What did Crowder's branching ("intrinsic") programming and the scrambled book add, what did evaluations find, and does modern evidence support branching on a wrong answer?

### Takeaway
Crowder's branching added larger frames, a multiple-choice question whose wrong alternatives each route to a remedial page, and learner control over the path; the best-documented 1960s comparison found no criterion-test difference between branching and linear versions, and the branching program produced more errors on the way.
Modern evidence supports the *content* of branching (feedback that explains, beyond the correct answer) more than its *mechanism* (routing), and warns that explanation attached to a wrong answer can underperform a bare correct answer unless the reader is confident and engaged.

### Cited Findings
- Crowder's branching ("intrinsic") program ends each page with a multiple-choice question and routes the reader by the answer; a wrong choice sends the reader to material that explains that error, and frames are larger than linear frames. The print form is the "scrambled text", published as the TutorText series by Doubleday in 1958. — [KnowledgeJump, Branching (Intrinsic Programming)](https://knowledgejump.com/history_learning/branching.html)
- Crowder argued that linear programs assume that a concept broken down finely enough will teach itself and that their exercises are "merely practice"; he called linear programming "an insult to intelligent students". The 1983 article is partly promotional, quoting Crowder's own advocacy. — [Education Week, 1983](https://www.edweek.org/education/branched-programming-gives-life-to-outmoded-teaching-machines/1983/05)
- Crowder's framing was that branching adapts to the student "without a medium of an extrinsic device such as a computer", which is why it worked in print. — [KnowledgeJump, Branching](https://knowledgejump.com/history_learning/branching.html)
- A 1965 symposium chapter says Crowder conceived the "scrambled textbook" in 1955 while training electrical engineers (adult technical learners); Audrey Watters could not verify the story and found almost no archival record of Crowder's industry work, compared with Skinner's Harvard archive. — [Watters, "crowder still", 2018](https://teachingmachin.es/2018/11/20/crowder-still)
- The best-documented direct comparison: a 1964 University of Pittsburgh report for the U.S. Air Force ran two experiments adding branching ("multitracking") to a linear program. Experiment 1 (extra cues at each frame) found "no significant difference in efficiency" between linear and multitrack. Experiment 2 used larger frames with detailed remedial frames after errors; "more errors were made on the large-step branching program, [but] performance on criterion tests was as good as for the regular small-step linear program". The authors suggested less frequent branches "at critical points" and large frames followed by targeted remedial frames. Adult military trainees. — [DTIC AD0609801](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD0609801.pdf); catalog record [IIT, "An Evaluation of Multiple Tracks in a Linear Program"](https://contrails.library.iit.edu/item/160280)
- One Indian school study (NCERT abstract) reports branching > linear > conventional on mean achievement; schoolchildren, details unavailable. — [NCERT abstract, Govil](https://osre.ncert.gov.in/abstract/Govil_P/339)
- Of 36 studies comparing programmed learning with traditional training, 17 found programmed instruction more effective, 18 found no difference, and one favored traditional training (programmed vs. conventional, not branching vs. linear). — [KnowledgeJump, Programmed Learning](https://www.knowledgejump.com/hrd/media/programmed_learning.html)
- Secondary course notes list the standard criticisms of branching: it "encourages guessing" on the multiple-choice item, is "difficult and time consuming" to write, and each frame "presents more text than the average linear frame". — [course notes, CSJMU](https://gyansanchay.csjmu.ac.in/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/programmed-instruction-dr-arock.pdf)
- Modern descendant, routing on answers: VanLehn (2011) classed computer tutors by grain. "Answer-based" systems assign a problem then give feedback and branching that depends on the answer (Crowder's shape); step-based and substep-based systems support each step of a solution. Step-based tutoring came close to human tutoring; one summary reports answer-based systems below step-based (0.40 SD) and substep-based (0.32 SD), another reports step-based at 0.76 vs no tutoring. The two readings disagree; check the primary paper before quoting either. — [ASEE summary](https://peer.asee.org/25090.pdf); [DTIC AD1030353](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD1030353.pdf)
- Kulik & Fletcher (2016): across 50 controlled evaluations of intelligent tutoring systems the median effect was 0.66 SD over conventional instruction; gains were larger on locally developed tests than on standardized tests. — [IDA, Kulik & Fletcher 2016](https://www.ida.org/research-and-publications/publications/all/e/ef/effectiveness-of-intelligent-tutoring-systems-a-meta-analytic-review)
- Modern descendant, content of the branch: Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015), 40 studies, 70 effect sizes (range −0.78 to 2.29) in computer-based environments. Elaborated feedback (an explanation) averaged 0.49, knowledge of correct response (KCR) 0.32, knowledge of results (KR) 0.05. Elaborated feedback was "particularly more effective than KR and KCR for higher order learning outcomes". — [ACU record of Van der Kleij et al. 2015](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis)
- Counter-evidence on explaining wrong answers: Kulhavy, White, Topp, Chan & Adams (1985) gave readers multiple-choice items with feedback of increasing complexity (correct answer; then discussion of the four incorrect responses; then sentence-by-sentence explanation of why each error choice was wrong; then the passage section). "The less complex feedback that provided the correct answer resulted in higher levels of subsequent task performance than the more complex versions", possibly because extra detail about wrong answers made the error itself more memorable. The result "was mediated by the readers' confidence": confident readers used feedback efficiently at any complexity. — [Hattie & Timperley 2007, p. 91](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- Verification alone is too thin a branch: in Marsh et al. (2012), marking a multiple-choice answer right or wrong did not improve a later short-answer test; "error correction required receiving the answer itself", though verification beat no feedback. — [Marsh, Lozito, Umanath, Bjork & Bjork 2012](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Marsh_Lozito_Umanath_Bjork_Bjork2012.pdf)
- Asterhan and colleagues tested feedback that "corrects and contrasts a student's erroneous solutions with the canonical, correct one" against a control that received only the correct answer (natural selection, conceptual change). The design matches the branching question; the results were not visible in the retrieved record. — [Asterhan lab record](https://asterhan.huji.ac.il/node/3131018)

### Inferences
- The thing Crowder added that linear programs lacked is a *diagnosis*: each wrong alternative names a specific misunderstanding and answers it. In a static ladder, that is a rung the reader selects, not a route the book computes: "If your version did X, see this" entries between "The shape" and the full solution. The reader does the routing by recognizing their own attempt in the list.
- The 1964 null result (same criterion performance, more errors en route) is consistent with the later worked-example and completion findings from the prior round: large frames plus targeted remediation cost nothing on the test and save frame-count. For an experienced-programmer book, large steps are the right default and the branch is reserved for predictable wrong turns.
- Van der Kleij's EF > KCR > KR ordering, with EF strongest for higher-order outcomes, supports keeping an explanation of design choices in the solution rung rather than a bare listing. Kulhavy 1985 is the brake: explanation attached to *wrong* answers helped less than the correct answer alone for low-confidence readers. So per-branch text should be short and should point to the correct construct, not catalog every way to be wrong.
- The VanLehn grain finding suggests the ladder's middle rung ("The shape") is doing step-based work: it feeds back on the structure of a solution before the reader finishes it, which is finer than answer-based confirm-and-continue.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evaluation of the TutorText books themselves was found; the 1960s evidence is a handful of small military and school studies, mostly in catalog records.
- Beane (1962, plane geometry) and Bushnell (1963, skip-branching) were cited but their results were not visible.
- The Asterhan contrast-feedback results could not be retrieved.
- VanLehn's step-based effect size appears in two incompatible secondhand forms (0.40 vs answer-based; 0.76 vs no tutoring).
- No study was found that compares error-diagnostic routing against confirm-and-continue in a *print or static* format with adult programmers.

## Key question 2: What did mastery learning (Bloom's LFM, Keller's PSI) add, what do meta-analyses report, and what transfers to a self-study book with no instructor?

### Takeaway
Mastery learning added a stated unit criterion, retesting until the criterion is met, self-pacing (PSI), and proctors who certify mastery; meta-analyses put the achievement effect around 0.5 to 0.7 SD, larger for weaker students and on delayed tests, at the cost of more time and lower completion when self-paced.
The parts that transfer to a book are the criterion and the retest: an explicit "you are done when" check before the solution reveal, and a second variant of the exercise for a reader who missed it.

### Cited Findings
- Kulik, Kulik & Cohen (1979) reviewed 75 comparative studies of Keller's PSI in college teaching; mean effect size "nearly 0.70" on both achievement and course perception (secondary summary). — [KnowledgeJump, Keller PSI](https://knowledgejump.com/hrd/history/psi.html)
- The same review: final-exam performance was higher in the PSI section in 57 of 61 studies; in 48 studies with usable averages, PSI classes averaged 73.6 vs 65.9 for conventional classes; the PSI advantage grew to "about 14 percentage points" on exams given several months after the course; differences were larger on essay than on objective exams; students rated PSI classes more enjoyable, more demanding, and higher in quality. College students. — [Athabasca, PSI: The Data](https://psych.athabascau.ca/open/keller/data.php)
- Taveggia (1976): PSI favored in 28 of 28 comparisons on course-content exams. — [Athabasca, PSI: The Data](https://psych.athabascau.ca/open/keller/data.php)
- Pascarella & Terenzini (1991) cite two PSI meta-analyses: 0.42 SD (19 studies) and 0.49 SD (61 studies, "an achievement advantage of 19 percentile points"). — [KnowledgeJump, Keller PSI](https://knowledgejump.com/hrd/history/psi.html)
- Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), *RER* 60(2), 265–299: 108 controlled evaluations; mastery programs "have positive effects on the examination performance of students in colleges, high schools, and the upper grades in elementary schools"; effects "appear to be stronger on the weaker students in a class"; they "vary as a function of mastery procedures used, experimental designs of studies, and course content"; positive effects on attitudes toward content and instruction; "may increase student time on instructional tasks"; "self-paced mastery programs often reduce the completion rates in college classes". A secondary page gives the mean effect as 0.52 SD; this could not be checked against the paper. — [academia.edu record of Kulik et al. 1990](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis)
- Slavin (1987) reports the earlier Kulik et al. (1986) means as 0.52 for pre-college and 0.54 for college studies. — [Slavin 1987, via gwern](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf)
- Kulik et al. (1990) on completion: "The percentage of students that complete PSI college classes is smaller than the percentage that complete conventional classes" (p. 286), and the better performance "is not an illusion created by the withdrawal of the weaker students before examination time" (p. 286). — [Athabasca, PSI: The Data](https://psych.athabascau.ca/open/keller/data.php)
- A review of PSI for distance education reports Kulik et al. (1990) as finding group-based mastery programs yield larger effects than self-paced ones, "particularly in social science subjects" (secondhand; see Gaps). — [Grant & Spencer 2003, IRRODL](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/152)
- Hattie (2012 edition): mastery learning d = 0.58; "Keller's Master Learning" d = 0.53, listed separately. — [Teachers Toolbox, Hattie list](https://www.teacherstoolbox.co.uk/?p=6501)
- Hattie & Timperley (2007), Table 2 from Hattie's database: "Programmed instruction" d = −0.04 (1 meta-analysis, 40 studies, 23 effects), listed among the least effective forms of feedback alongside praise, punishment, and extrinsic rewards. — [Hattie & Timperley 2007, Table 2](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- PSI components as described: unit mastery via a quiz with a cutoff (for example 80 or 90 percent); at least two further attempts on alternate forms; self-pacing as a consequence of mastery; proctors who administer quizzes, give feedback, and "certify" mastery. — [Grant & Spencer 2003, IRRODL](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/152)
- On the criterion level: a behavior-analysis review states that "the only empirical investigations of mastery criteria come from studies that manipulated criterion levels with typically developing adults who were learning college-level academic content", and those found higher criterion levels produced higher performance. — [JABA/Behavior Analysis in Practice abstract, 2019](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2019/journal-article-abstracts/s40617-019-00365-2/)
- A 1974 dissertation tested the proctor and pacing components: proctors "worked effectively with students regardless of whether their grade was contingent" on their students' exam grades; bonus points for early unit completion affected one part of the final and not progress or attitudes. — [University of Tennessee dissertation, 1974](https://trace.tennessee.edu/utk_graddiss/3229)
- Reboy & Semb (1991): PSI taught complex material above lecture-discussion levels, with effects on higher-order skills shown mostly through transfer measures. — [Athabasca, PSI: The Data](https://psych.athabascau.ca/open/keller/data.php)

### Inferences
- Three PSI components need a person (proctor, grade contingency, pacing deadlines) and two do not (a stated criterion, retest on an alternate form). A solutions file can carry the criterion as a one-line "Done when" statement above the hint (the tests pass, the type checker is clean, a named behavior holds), and the repository's test file is the closest thing to a proctor: it certifies without judging style.
- The completion finding is the warning for a self-paced book: PSI's dropout came with self-pacing. Short units and a visible count of what is left (the ladder's three rungs are already a visible unit) are the print analogues of the proctor's nudge.
- Larger effects for weaker students and on delayed, essay-style tests suggest the criterion should ask for a produced artifact (a working file) rather than recognition, which matches the "The shape" rung: the reader completes the skeleton rather than picks an option.
- Hattie's −0.04 for programmed instruction, beside 0.53 to 0.58 for mastery, says the frame-and-confirm mechanics added nothing on their own; the criterion-and-retest loop did the work.

### Gaps
- The 1990 paper's own moderator tables (criterion level, LFM vs PSI split, retention) were not accessible; the 0.52 overall figure is from an AI-generated summary page and the "group-based > self-paced" claim is secondhand.
- Kulik, Jaksa & Kulik (1978) on PSI component features was cited but not retrieved; which components carry the effect remains undocumented here.
- No study of mastery criteria in self-study with no instructor was found; the criterion-level evidence is from college courses with graders.

## Key question 3: Adjunct questions and pre-questions: where should questions sit, and what is the evidence on pre- versus post-questions for retention and for transfer to untested material?

### Takeaway
Post-questions help both the questioned and the surrounding material; pre-questions help the questioned material (g ≈ 0.54 in adults, larger when the reader must commit a guess) and do almost nothing for untested material (g ≈ 0.04), with text materials at zero.
Recent direct comparisons split: Pan & Sana (2021, n = 1,573 adults) found pretesting at or above posttesting; a 2019 study found posttesting ahead; a classroom STEM study found no prequestion benefit.

### Cited Findings
- Rothkopf (1966) and Rothkopf & Bisbicos (1967): questions placed after a passage produced a direct effect on questioned content and an indirect, incidental effect on unquestioned content. Frase (1967, 1968) found after-questions beat before-questions on the posttest; Sagaria & DiVesta (1978) agreed; Rickards (1976) found different results for conceptual prequestions. — [Najah, "Type and position of adjunct questions"](https://staff.najah.edu/media/sites/default/files/Type_and_position_of_adjunct_Questions_Their_effects_on_memory_and_Application.pdf); [Work-Learning Research on Rothkopf](https://www.worklearning.com/2006/03/14/mathemagenic_pr/)
- Anderson & Biddle (1975), as summarized by later authors: questions help prose learning; "this enhancement is greater when questions are placed after the material to which they refer"; benefits are stronger for constructed answers than multiple-choice; pre-passage factual questions help those facts but "may actually retard retention of indirect information" through selective attention. — [Matuschak note on Anderson & Biddle](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zWQxgb82VhMqsuL71KGWHpW); [Illinois technical report](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/17676/bitstreams/63499/data.pdf)
- Hamaker (1986), *RER* 56, 212–242, examined 13 design variables; effects on unrelated material were small and appeared only under some conditions (higher-order questions, unrestricted time); the benefit on untested material concentrates in *related* untested content. — [SAGE issue listing](https://journals.sagepub.com/toc/rera/56/2); [Matuschak note on Hamaker](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z4m9Gat7zi9YUmZzQRR7pwt)
- St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn (2024), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 31:411–441, preregistered meta-analysis of the prequestion effect: specific effect g = 0.54 (k = 97), general effect g = 0.04 (k = 91). Publication-bias-adjusted specific effect 0.45. — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024, open copy at NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Same paper, moderators of the specific effect: guessing required g = 0.65 vs not required g = 0.22 (p < .001; "amongst all of the moderator variables, prequestion guessing had the strongest influence"); factual prequestions 0.58 vs conceptual 0.28 (p = .073); adults g = 0.62 (k = 77) vs grade-school children 0.22 (k = 20), p = .020, "borderline". Outliers with ~80% prequestion accuracy were "adult experts in their fields studying new information in their area of expertise" (Anderson, 1978). — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Same paper, moderators of the general effect: audiovisual materials g = 0.17 vs text g = −0.005 (p = .017); untested items related to the prequestions 0.17 vs unrelated 0.01 (p = .029); massed study 0.11 vs interleaved −0.07; studies before 2006 showed g = −0.09 (k = 49) and since 2006 g = 0.15 (k = 42). — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Same paper, limits: "Most researchers have explored the magnitude of the PE with college undergraduates"; "few studies have explored the benefits of prequestions in the classroom"; delay between prequestion and study was too rarely manipulated to analyze (k = 1 or 2). The authors suggest factual prequestions build the background knowledge that transfer needs (citing Pan & Rickard 2018). — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Pan & Sana (2021), *JEP: Applied*, five experiments, combined n = 1,573, expository passages paired with a pretest or a posttest (multiple-choice or cued recall, with or without correct-answer feedback, tested at 5 minutes or 48 hours): both beat no test; pretesting scored higher, across formats, feedback, and intervals. — [Athabasca record of Pan & Sana 2021](https://pure.athabascau.ca/en/publications/pretesting-versus-posttesting-comparing-the-pedagogical-benefits-/)
- Riegert et al. (2019, *npj Science of Learning*) found posttesting ahead of pretesting on retention. — [PMC6760123](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6760123/)
- Geller et al. (2017), STEM classroom: a prequestion at the start of class, repeated as a postquestion beside a new question; postquestions showed no advantage over new questions on the end-of-class test; "prequestions do not appear to enhance learning, nor to enhance the effects of retrieval practice". — [Geller et al. 2017, Cognitive Research](https://link.springer.com/10.1186/s41235-017-0078-z)
- Hausman & Rhodes (2018) studied when pretesting fails to enhance learning of concepts from reading texts (boundary conditions; details not retrieved). — [search record](https://brcris.ibict.br/vivo/display/publ_41b56d38-c856-4ba8-a9c4-49063bb91d62)

### Inferences
- The "Where to look" hint is a post-question device: it sends the reader back into the chapter after an attempt, which is the placement with the broadest evidence (direct plus incidental benefit). The ladder already sits in the post position; the open question is whether to add a pre position.
- A pre-question in a static book only pays if the reader commits a guess (0.65 vs 0.22), the question is factual rather than conceptual, and the target is the specific material the exercise will use. A one-line "Before reading this section: what does `X` return when...?" at the top of a section, with no answer shown, is the shape the evidence supports; expecting it to lift the rest of the section is not (general g ≈ 0, and zero for text).
- The prior-knowledge moderator (outliers at ~80% prequestion accuracy were domain experts) means pre-questions in a book for experienced programmers will help least where the reader already knows the answer, so they belong on the book's new material (the 3.15 features, the typing constructs), not on general Python.
- The pre-vs-post split in recent studies means the ladder should not be rebuilt around pretests; adding a committed guess ahead of a section is a low-cost addition whose downside is nil in the meta-analysis (no negative general effect since 2006).

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis compares pre- and post-questions head to head; the three direct comparisons found (Pan & Sana, Riegert, Geller) disagree.
- De Lima & Jaeger (2020) on pre- vs post-questions in children was found only as a citation.
- No study used programming or code reading as the material; every study is prose, lecture, or video.

## Key question 4: Feedback timing and content: immediate vs delayed, correct response vs elaborated, and what applies when the reader chooses when to reveal

### Takeaway
Knowledge of results alone is near zero; the correct answer helps; an explanation helps more, most of all for higher-order outcomes, but explanation attached to wrong alternatives can underperform the bare answer for unconfident readers.
Delay helps on tests of difficult items and hurts on easy ones; the risk unique to a reader-controlled reveal is "presearch availability": feedback seen before an attempt teaches nothing, so the ladder's job is to secure a committed attempt before the reveal.

### Cited Findings
- Bangert-Drowns, Kulik, Kulik & Morgan (1991), *RER* 61(2), 213–238: one summary reports 58 feedback effects, 18 negative, overall d = 0.26; Hattie & Timperley's table lists it as 40 studies, d = 0.28. KR alone "shows almost no effect on achievement, comparable to the absence of feedback"; KCR is superior to KR; feedback works when it "promotes mindfulness of the content". — [EduCaixa technical appendix on feedback](https://educaixa.org/es/-/apendice-tecnico-feedba-1); [Hattie & Timperley 2007, Table 1](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf); [Cambridge Handbook of Instructional Feedback, Theoretical Foundations](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-instructional-feedback/theoretical-foundations/D52C906024655CB38BAFE5FF63F91EDE)
- The same group presented "The impact of peekability on feedback effects" at AERA 1987 (feedback the learner can see before answering); the paper is cited in a dissertation reference list and its findings were not retrievable. — [NCSU dissertation reference list](https://repository.lib.ncsu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7e2a708d-8781-4bec-b362-de7198d727bd/content)
- Kulhavy (1977), *RER* 47, 211–232, reviewing over 60 studies: feedback "increases what a person learns from instruction by confirming correct responses and identifying errors; however, if material is very difficult, students guess at answers and try to match answers and feedback". — [summary in a Slovenian open textbook](https://hippocampus.si/ISBN/978-961-293-256-5/files/basic-html/page307.html)
- Kulhavy & Stock (1989), *Educational Psychology Review* 1, 279–308: feedback split into a verification part (right/wrong) and an elaboration part with three dimensions: task-specific vs instruction-based vs extra-instructional; structural similarity to the original instruction; and topic-contingent, response-contingent, bug-related, or attribute-isolation content. The certitude claim (feedback helps most after a confident error) was not visible in any retrieved source. — [arXiv 2307.00279, "Pedagogical Foundations of Feedback for Language Models"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.00279)
- Kulik & Kulik (1988), *RER* 58(1), 79–97, 53 studies. Hattie & Timperley: "at the task level (i.e., testing situations), some delay is beneficial (0.36), but at the process level (i.e., engaging in processing classroom activities), immediate feedback is beneficial (0.28)". Their table lists the review at 53 studies, 0.28. — [Hattie & Timperley 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- Smith & Kimball (2010) on the same review: delayed feedback beat immediate "in all but one of the 8 feedback timing studies" that used multiple-choice tests with 5–7 day retention; for applied studies the Kuliks "concluded that immediate feedback was preferable". Smith's own review of 39 timing studies: 16 favored delayed, 12 favored immediate, 11 found no effect. The delay-retention effect (Kulhavy & Anderson 1972) has been shown with high-school students and college undergraduates, with delays from 15 s to four days. — [Smith & Kimball 2010](https://www.ou.edu/memorylab/pdfs/SmithKimball_2010_LearningFromFeedback_ms.pdf)
- The Kuliks' own sentence, as quoted in a trade book: "Applied studies using actual classroom quizzes and real learning materials have usually found immediate feedback to be more effective than delay." — [Goodreads note quoting Young, *Ultralearning*](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/44770129-ultralearning/22801001-taylor-dolezal/24531955-5318-40f6-b9a0-0307a19ae578)
- Clariana, Wagner & Roher Murphy (2000): effect of delayed vs immediate feedback by item difficulty: −0.06 for easy items, 0.35 for midrange, 1.17 for difficult items; "difficult items are more likely to involve greater degrees of processing about the task, and delayed feedback provides the opportunity to do this". — [Hattie & Timperley 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- Hattie & Timperley (2007): four levels of feedback (task, process, self-regulation, self); across 12 meta-analyses, 196 studies, 6,972 effects, average d = 0.79; Kluger & DeNisi (1996) 470 effects, d = 0.38, 32% of effects negative; praise 0.14; programmed instruction −0.04. "With inefficient learners, it is better for a teacher to provide elaborations through instruction than to provide feedback on poorly understood concepts." Feedback at the process level "appears to be more effective than at the task level"; too much task-level feedback "may encourage students to focus on the immediate goal and not the strategies". — [Hattie & Timperley 2007](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020): 435 studies, 994 effects, ~61,000 participants; random-effects d = 0.55, trimmed d = 0.48; reinforcement/punishment d = 0.24 (k = 39), corrective d = 0.46 (k = 238), high-information feedback d = 0.99 (k = 42); cognitive outcomes 0.51; the gap from Hattie & Timperley's 0.79 is attributed to duplicate removal and precision weighting. — [Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie 2020, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)
- Van der Kleij et al. (2015): elaborated 0.49, KCR 0.32, KR 0.05; effect sizes were "negatively affected by delayed feedback timing and by primary and high school"; no significant timing-by-level interaction. — [ACU record](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/86y84/effects-of-feedback-in-a-computer-based-learning-environment-on-students-learning-outcomes-a-meta-analysis)
- Hypercorrection: Metcalfe and Eich found participants remembered corrective feedback to high-confidence errors better than to low-confidence errors (adult lab participants). — [Duke publication record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1105284)
- Marsh et al. (2012): feedback "is less useful when participants can peek at feedback before answering a question, and more useful with a delay between answering a question and receiving feedback". — [Marsh et al. 2012](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Marsh_Lozito_Umanath_Bjork_Bjork2012.pdf)

### Inferences
- A collapsible solution is peekable by construction. Kulhavy's 1977 finding (hard material pushes readers to match answers to feedback) and the peekability line of work say the ladder's value depends on the reader committing before opening a rung. The hint rung costs little to peek; "The shape" is the commitment device, since a reader who opens it still has to write the bodies. The full solution is the rung to guard: a one-line prompt above it ("Run your version against the tests before opening this") is the static equivalent of a presearch-availability control.
- Delay is free in a book: the reader's own attempt is the delay. Clariana's gradient (1.17 on difficult items) argues against placing the solution where a glance reveals it; putting the explanation of design choices *after* the code inside the rung, rather than before, keeps a second small delay before the "why".
- The content ordering KR < KCR < elaborated is already the ladder's ordering (hint < shape < solution-with-explanation). The explanation in the solution rung is the high-information feedback that scores 0.99 in Wisniewski; it should state the design choice and the alternative it rejects (process level) rather than only confirm the code (task level).
- Kulhavy 1985 and Hattie's "elaborations through instruction" line together say that when a reader is lost, more feedback does not help; the hint rung's pointer back into the chapter is the right response, since it reroutes to instruction.
- A confidence prompt ("How sure are you that your version handles the empty case?") before the reveal is the one certitude-model device a static page can host; the hypercorrection evidence is adult lab work and has not been tested in a programming book.

### Gaps
- Bangert-Drowns 1991's own numbers for presearch availability (how negative the uncontrolled-peeking effects were) could not be retrieved; the 0.26 vs 0.28 discrepancy between summaries is unresolved.
- Kulhavy & Stock 1989's response-certitude results are unverified here; only the model's structure was found.
- Kulik & Kulik 1988's full abstract was blocked; the 0.36 and 0.28 figures are Hattie & Timperley's reading.
- No feedback-timing study used self-paced reveal by the learner; every study assigned the timing.

## Key question 5: Which devices are in widespread static use today, and which were abandoned and why?

### Takeaway
What survives in print is the hidden answer (card-covered Q&A in *The Little Schemer*, `<details>` blocks on GitHub), graded exercise difficulty (Knuth's 0–50 scale), and the hint-then-solution ladder; what was dropped is the small linear frame, because it bored readers and produced no measurable advantage, and the insistence on overt written responses, because covert responding learned as well in most studies.

### Cited Findings
- Response mode: Evans, Glaser & Homme (1959) first questioned whether learners need to write answers; covert responders "finished faster and scored higher", and later results "have been conflicting"; a thesis reviewer concluded "the type of response seems to have little bearing on the level of learning achieved". — [NIU thesis](https://huskiecommons.lib.niu.edu/allgraduate-thesesdissertations/1376)
- Janove (1962), fifth graders: "little, or no difference" between overt and covert. Valverde (1968), Air Force radar material (adults): covert responders "learned as efficiently" as overt, and covert "did not save instructional time". — [DTIC AD0614014](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD0614014.pdf)
- Csanyi, Glaser & Reynolds (1964), phonetic symbols: overt was better for retention only when the test itself required overt oral responses; a prompted covert condition learned less but needed 30–50 percent of the time. — [IIT record](https://contrails.library.iit.edu/item/160232)
- A computer-based comparison of passive reading, covert responding, and typing: covert and typed responders both beat passive readers on a generalization posttest, with no difference between covert and typed. — [IIT record, confirmation peeking and response mode](https://contrails.library.iit.edu/item/161202)
- Reasons for decline: popularity "faded because of concerns over student boredom and the lack of conclusive evidence supporting its superiority over traditional teaching methods". — [EBSCO Research Starters, Programmed Instruction](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/programmed-instruction)
- The movement "prescribed a very rigid and inflexible method of instruction", which is why it "quickly fell out of favor"; many programs reached schools "without adequate evaluation", contributing to the decline. — [BYU ScholarsArchive, "Learning from programmed instruction"](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/1730); [ERIC ED439464](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED439464.pdf)
- Skinner's design principle was that learning "should be divided into a very large number of very small steps, with rewards for success". — [Open University history blog](https://www.open.ac.uk/blogs/History-of-the-OU/?p=308)
- Watters' *Teaching Machines* (2021), as reviewed: programmed instruction "didn't work, according to two independent research reports at the time"; the machines "could not adapt to diverse ways of thinking, and teachers were confined to managing programmed instruction"; Skinner's machine "was in many ways less sophisticated than those of some of his competitors". — [ICT in Education review](https://ictineducation.org/home-page/review-teaching-machines); [Education Review](https://edrev.asu.edu/index.php/ER/article/download/4587/2007/10611); [WAC Clearinghouse essay](https://wacclearinghouse.org/docs/double-helix/v9/katz.pdf)
- Hattie's database puts programmed instruction at d = −0.04, among "the least effective" feedback forms. — [Hattie & Timperley 2007, Table 2](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Hattie_Timperly_2007.pdf)
- *The Little Schemer*: a programmed text read "with a card in your hand covering up part of the page"; the book asks, the reader answers, then moves the card; it sometimes asks a question the reader cannot yet answer to make a point. Reviewers split: one praised a format "honed through several editions with actual students"; Al Sweigart found it "needed straightforward explanation afterwards" and "not a good guide for beginners". The preface says it grew from a two-week introduction for students with no programming experience. — [Sweigart review](https://inventwithpython.com/blog/book-review-the-little-schemer.html); [Abramowitz review](https://marc-abramowitz.com/?p=867)
- Knuth, *TAOCP*: each exercise carries a rating from 0 (trivial) to 50 (an open research question); sections end with dozens of exercises across that range. — [Wikipedia, The Art of Computer Programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_Computer_Programming)
- GitHub Markdown: collapsible sections are the HTML `<details>`/`<summary>` element; a blank line after `</summary>` is needed or the Markdown inside renders as literal text; GitHub Pages (Jekyll) may leave Markdown inside `<details>` unrendered; Bitbucket strips the tags. — [Heilmann, using details/summary on GitHub Pages](https://christianheilmann.com/2024/01/24/using-details-summary-and-colour-coding-on-github-pages/); [MacMDViewer, Markdown collapsible section](https://macmdviewer.com/blog/markdown-collapsible-section)
- Documentation guidance: hide only optional material (hints, full solutions) behind a collapsed section, never information the reader needs for the main task. — [Databricks docs content model](https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/internal/docs-content-model/content-atoms/details)

### Inferences
- The response-mode literature licenses the ladder's "think, then open" design: covert responding learned as well as writing in most studies, so a book need not demand a written answer at every rung. The exception is retention tested in the response's own mode (Csanyi), which for programming means the reader should produce code at least once per exercise; "The shape" is the rung that asks for it.
- The abandoned device is the small frame, not the confirm step. The ladder's three rungs per exercise are coarse by programmed-instruction standards, which is the direction the 1964 Pittsburgh result and the boredom complaints point.
- *The Little Schemer* shows what a reader-run programmed text looks like in a programming book and what readers complain about: no explanation after the reveal. The solution rung's design-choice paragraph answers that complaint.
- Knuth's rating is the surviving static form of "adaptivity": the reader picks the level. A difficulty mark on each exercise (even three levels) lets a reader route around the ladder, which is the only routing a static file can offer.

### Gaps
- No modern survey of hint/solution formats in technical books was found; the examples here are individual books and platform documentation, not a measured comparison.
- No evidence was found on whether readers of static books open collapsed solutions before attempting; the peekability concern is inferred from lab studies with assigned timing.
- Watters' own chapter on why programmed texts bored readers was not retrievable; the "tedious frames" claim rests on the EBSCO summary and reviews.
