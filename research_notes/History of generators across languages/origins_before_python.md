## Conway's coroutines (1958 idea, 1963 paper), Erdwinn, and Knuth's account

### Takeaway
Conway's 1963 paper defines coroutines as peer modules of a "separable" program that pass discrete items along fixed one-way paths, each written as if it were the main program; the definition is symmetric and has no notion of a generator or a yielded sequence. Conway's own footnote shares credit with Joel Erdwinn. The 1958 date and the Erdwinn/Merner "Bilateral Linkage" story come from Knuth's TAOCP, which I could not open and can cite only through secondary pages.

### Cited Findings
- Publication: M. E. Conway, "Design of a Separable Transition-Diagram Compiler", Communications of the ACM, vol. 6, no. 7, July 1963, pp. 396-408. The work was done at Case Institute of Technology on a Burroughs 220 COBOL compiler. [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Conway's precondition is "separability". A program is separable if its modules communicate under three restrictions: "(1) the only communication between modules is in the form of discrete items of information; (2) the flow of each of these items is along fixed, one-way paths; (3) the entire program can be laid out so that the input is at the left extreme, the output is at the right extreme, and everywhere in between all information items flowing between modules have a component of motion to the right." [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Conway's definition, verbatim: "Under these conditions each module may be made into a coroutine; that is, it may be coded as an autonomous program which communicates with adjacent modules as if they were input or output subroutines. Thus, coroutines are subroutines all at the same level, each acting as if it were the master program when in fact there is no master program. There is no bound placed by this definition on the number of inputs and outputs a coroutine may have." [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Conway's footnote 1, attached to that definition: "To the best of the author's knowledge the coroutine idea was concurrently developed by him and Joel Erdwinn, now of Computer Sciences Corporation." This is the primary source for the first name "Joel". [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Control transfer as Conway describes it: "When coroutines A and B are connected so that A sends items to B, B runs for a while until it encounters a read command, which means it needs something from A. Then control is transferred to A until it wants to 'write,' whereupon control is returned to B at the point where it left off." [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Conway's motivating example is the "asterisk squasher" (replace each `**` in card input with one character). Written as a subroutine it needs a switch that "bifurcates the subroutine and selects the part to be used at each call depending on the situation at the last call"; "The coroutine approach to the same problem accomplishes the switching job implicitly by use of the subroutine calling sequence." [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Implementation: each coroutine gets "an isolated BUN instruction which we can call its router"; a call from A to B is `STP AROUTER` / `BUN BROUTER`. "Thus, the router is actually a generalization of the switch." [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Conway's stated payoff: a pair of coroutines can run interleaved in one pass, or A can write all its items to tape and B read them afterward, "with only trivial modification". He also notes that with parallel processors "the coroutines of a separable program may be executed simultaneously". [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Knuth, in a later paper of his own (with Ruskey, 2004), writes: "Coroutines, originally introduced by M. E. Conway [2], are analogous to subroutines, but they are symmetrical with respect to caller and callee", citing the 1963 CACM paper. The same paper says: "The programming language SIMULA 67 [3] introduced support for coroutines in terms of fundamental operations named call, detach, and resume". [Knuth and Ruskey, arXiv cs/0404058](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0404058)
- Secondary (Wikipedia, citing Knuth TAOCP vol. 1): Conway coined the term coroutine in 1958 when he applied it to the construction of an assembly program; the first published explanation appeared in 1963. [Wikipedia, Melvin Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melvin_Conway)
- Secondary (page author's paraphrase, not a Knuth quotation): coroutines "were independently studied by J. Erdwinn and J. Merner, at about the same time as Conway", and an early UNIVAC publication noted a primitive coroutine linkage as a "programming tip". [Softpanorama](https://softpanorama.org/Lang/coroutines.shtml)
- de Moura and Ierusalimschy date the concept to "the early 1960's" and attribute it to Conway, quoting him loosely as "subroutines who act as the master program". They do not mention 1958 or Erdwinn. [Revisiting Coroutines, section 1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)

### Inferences
- Conway's coroutine is a symmetric, pipeline-shaped construct: two peers joined by a read/write linkage. The data flows one way (A's write to B's read), while control passes both ways. That is the consumer-producer pair that a generator and its `for` loop later specialize, but Conway names neither side as subordinate.
- Conway's "switch" remark is the earliest statement I found of the argument later made for generators: without a coroutine, the producer must encode its position as explicit state.
- The two claims for Erdwinn do not conflict. Conway's footnote names Joel Erdwinn as concurrent co-developer; the "J. Erdwinn and J. Merner" form is how secondary pages report Knuth.

### Gaps
- Knuth, TAOCP vol. 1, section 1.4.5 "History and Bibliography" (the coroutine section itself is 1.4.2): not openable online. From memory, the passage reads approximately: the word "coroutine" was coined by M. E. Conway in 1958, after he had developed the concept, and he first applied it to the construction of an assembly program; coroutines were independently studied by J. Erdwinn and J. Merner at about the same time, in a paper titled "Bilateral Linkage" that was not considered interesting enough to publish, and no copies seem to survive; the first published explanation came much later in Conway's 1963 article. The Wikipedia wording matches this closely. The report writer should treat the exact wording as unverified and check the book.
- Whether Conway himself anywhere dates the idea to 1958: not found. The 1963 paper gives no date for the idea.
- The UNIVAC "programming tip" (Knuth cites an early UNIVAC publication, from memory 1954): not verified.

## Simula 67, IPL-V, and pre-1970 uses of the word "generator"

### Takeaway
In IPL-V (Newell and Tonge, 1959-1961) a "generator" was a subroutine that calls a caller-supplied processing routine on each element of a data structure: internal iteration, the inverse of the modern control direction. Simula 67 supplied the general mechanism (call, detach, resume) and, per Griswold in 1981, is the oldest language in which "generator" names a coroutine usage. Both the Alphard group and the Icon group cite IPL-V as the earliest generator language.

### Cited Findings
- Griswold, Hanson, and Korb (1981): "An early language that has generators is IPL-V [31], in which a generator is a subroutine that calls a processing routine to operate on each object in a data structure." [Generators in Icon, section 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Same source, on other uses of the word: "Other languages, such as ALGOL 68 [38] and COBOL, use the term generator to describe various language features, but this use is unrelated to the Icon notion." [Generators in Icon, section 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Same source, on coroutine languages: "Languages that support coroutines frequently use the term generator to describe a particular coroutine usage. SIMULA [1] is the oldest of such languages; more recent examples include extensions to POP-2 [24] and SL5 [20] and coroutine additions to PASCAL [27]." [Generators in Icon, section 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- The Sather authors (1995) trace generalized iteration to the same root: "The idea of generalizing iteration control structures goes back to early work such as the generators of IPL-V [12] or the generators and 'possibility lists' of Conniver [9]." Their IPL-V reference is Newell and Tonge, "An introduction to Information Processing Language V", RAND Paper P-1929, presented at the ACM National Conference in 1959, published 1960. [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 4.1](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- The RAND paper P-1929 (Newell and Tonge, 1960) is listed by RAND as the introduction to IPL-V. [RAND P-1929](https://www.rand.org/pubs/papers/P1929.html)
- Snippet only: the August 1976 Alphard technical report (Shaw, Wulf, London) says related constructs appear "in IPL-V as generators [Newell64] and in Lisp as the mapping functions". [DTIC ADA030286](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA030286.pdf) (the server refused my fetch with a 403, so this wording is from a search summary)
- Conniver (McDermott and Sussman, MIT AI Memo 259a, May 1974), as described by the Sather authors: a generator feeding a "possibility list" "yields new values and has the option of maintaining its state ('AU_REVOIR') or of quitting ('ADIEU'), similar to the yield and quit statements in Sather". [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 4.1](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Simula 67's coroutine operations are `call`, `detach`, and `resume`, per Knuth and Ruskey. [arXiv cs/0404058](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0404058)
- The Simula 67 definition is Dahl, Myhrhaug, and Nygaard, "SIMULA 67 Common Base Language", Norwegian Computing Center, 1968. [Common Base Language report, softwarepreservation.org](https://www.softwarepreservation.org/projects/ALGOL/manual/NR_report_743_Dahl_Myhrhaug_Nygaard_Simula-67-Common-Base-Language.pdf) (located, not read)
- de Moura and Ierusalimschy: asymmetric coroutines are "more commonly denoted as semisymmetric or semi coroutines [Dahl et al. 1972]"; "The general-purpose coroutine mechanisms implemented by Simula and BCPL provide both types of control transfer"; Simula's mechanism, "a truly complex implementation of coroutines, was practically adopted as a reference for a general-purpose coroutine mechanism". [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Liskov records Simula as CLU's main predecessor for data abstraction (Dahl discussed Simula classes and clusters at the 1973 meeting), but her iterator section credits only Alphard, not Simula. [A History of CLU, sections 2 and 3.10](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Generator" had at least three pre-1975 senses: IPL-V's element-enumerating subroutine that applies a processing routine (a visitor or callback shape), the coroutine-as-producer usage that grew up around Simula, and Conniver's lazily filled possibility lists. The modern sense (a routine that suspends and hands a value to its consumer) is the second.
- IPL-V's generator inverts today's control: the generator drives and calls the consumer. CLU's implementation ("a yield effectively calls the loop body", below) keeps that same implementation shape under a syntax that reads the other way.
- Conniver's AU_REVOIR/ADIEU pair (1972-1974) is a named "yield and stay alive" versus "yield and finish" distinction that predates CLU's `yield`. I found no claim that CLU drew on it.

### Gaps
- I did not read the IPL-V manual (Newell, ed., Prentice-Hall, 1961; 2nd ed. 1964) or the RAND paper itself, so IPL-V's own definition of "generator" is reported only through Griswold (1981) and the Sather authors (1995).
- Which Simula version first had `detach`/`resume` (Simula I, 1965, versus Simula 67): not settled by any source I opened. The Common Base Language report is the place to check.
- Whether Simula's own documents use the word "generator": not verified. Griswold's sentence says languages that support coroutines "frequently use the term", not that Simula's manual does.

## Alphard generators and CLU iterators: priority, influence, and `yield`

### Takeaway
Alphard came first and CLU says so: Liskov writes that iterators "were inspired by a construct in Alphard called a 'generator'", seen on a visit to CMU in summer 1975, and that Russ Atkinson designed iterators on the flight home and wrote them up in September 1975. An Alphard generator was a group of operations (`&init`, `&next`) driven by a `for` statement, with no suspending routine, so the single resumable routine with a `yield` statement is CLU's contribution. CLU restricted iterators to `for` loops, one per loop, nested only, to stay on a single stack.

### Cited Findings
- Priority and influence, verbatim: "Iterators were inspired by a construct in Alphard called a 'generator' [Shaw, 1976; Shaw, 1977]. We first learned about this in the summer of 1975 when we visited the Alphard group at CMU. We were intrigued by generators since they solved some problems with data abstractions, but we thought they were too complicated. Russ Atkinson designed iterators on the airplane going back to Boston after this meeting and described them in a design note in September 1975 [Atkinson, 1975]." [A History of CLU, section 3.10](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- Iterators are the one CLU feature Liskov attributes to an individual: "Usually it is not possible to identify an individual with a feature (iterators are the exception here, as discussed in Section 3.10)." [A History of CLU, section 3](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- What an Alphard generator was, per Liskov: "A generator does this by providing a group of operations, containing at least an operation to get the generation started, an operation to get the next element, and an operation to determine whether there are any more elements. Alphard generators had several more operations, and the Alphard designers worked out a way to use the for statement to call these operations at appropriate points." [A History of CLU, section 3.10](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- What a CLU iterator is, per Liskov: "A CLU iterator is a single operation that yields its results incrementally. For example, `elements = iter (s: set[t]) yields (t)` produces all the elements in set s, but it yields them one at a time. An iterator is called in a for statement: `for x: int in set[int]$elements(coll) do ...`". [A History of CLU, section 3.10](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- Control flow: "The for loop begins by calling the iterator. Each time the iterator yields a result, the loop body is executed; when the body finishes, control resumes in the iterator, and when the iterator returns, the loop terminates. Also, if the loop body causes the loop to terminate, the iterator terminates." [A History of CLU, section 3.10](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- Relation to coroutines and the restriction: "Iterators are related to coroutines; the iterator and the body of the for loop pass control back and forth. However, their use is limited so that CLU programs can make do with a single stack. They are inexpensive: a yield effectively calls the loop body, which returns to the iterator when it is finished. (Calls are very cheap in CLU.) Imposing the limitations on iterators was done to get the efficient, single stack implementation, albeit at the expense of some expressive power. For example, iterators cannot be used to compute whether two lists have the same elements, since to do this you need to iterate through the two lists side-by-side, and CLU only allows iterators to be nested." [A History of CLU, section 3.10](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- Design principle behind the restriction: "we limited the expressive power of the mechanism so that we could implement it using a single stack." [A History of CLU, section 3](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- Dates inside CLU: the January 1975 preliminary manual ("CLU .5") "included all of current CLU (in some form) except for exception handling and iterators", so iterators entered between September 1975 and the 1977 CACM paper (Liskov, Snyder, Atkinson, Schaffert, "Abstraction mechanisms in CLU", cited as [Liskov et al. 1977] by de Moura and Ierusalimschy). [A History of CLU, appendix](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- Built-in use: CLU arrays "provide an iterator ... that yields all elements and a second iterator that yields all legal indices". [A History of CLU, section 3.6](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- Liskov counts iterators among CLU's novel and influential features: "Many of the features of CLU were novel; in addition to the support for data abstraction through clusters, there are iterators, the exception mechanism, and the mechanism for [parametric] polymorphism." [A History of CLU, section 4](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- Alphard publication: Shaw, Wulf, London, "Abstraction and Verification in Alphard: Defining and Specifying Iteration and Generators", CACM 20(8), August 1977, pp. 553-564, DOI 10.1145/359763.359782; preceded by CMU/ISI technical reports of August 1976. The abstract's subject is extending abstraction to the iteration statement and giving proof rules for generators, including a way to show a generator terminates. [CACM](https://cacm.acm.org/research/abstraction-and-verification-in-alphard)
- Snippet only (a course review of the 1977 paper): an Alphard generator is a special kind of form with two operations, `&init` and `&next`; a `for x: gen(y)` loop calls `&init` for the first value and a hidden boolean, then `&next` each pass, and stops when the boolean is false; pre- and postconditions on the two functions plus a generator invariant support loop proofs; the paper also defines a `first` statement. [Queen's University review](https://research.cs.queensu.ca/home/cordy/cisc860/Biblio/drb/CS/shaw77.pdf)
- Secondary: Alphard "was never implemented". [Wikipedia, Alphard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphard_(programming_language))
- Outside view (Icon group, 1981): "In CLU, this procedure, called an iterator, produces its values using the yield statement. When another element is required, execution of the iterator continues where the yield statement left off. In each of these languages, generators are only accessible in a specific context: a particular type of for statement." [Generators in Icon, section 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Outside view (Sather group, 1995): "A CLU iterator is like a routine except that it may 'yield' in addition to returning. It may only be called in the head of a special 'for' loop construct." Their list of CLU limits: "One iterator per loop", "No way to modify elements", "Iterator arguments are loop invariant". [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 1](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Outside view (de Moura and Ierusalimschy, 2009): the iterator abstraction "was originally proposed and implemented by the designers of CLU"; "CLU iterators are confined within a for loop that can invoke exactly one iterator." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.2](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)

### Inferences
- Order of events: Alphard generators existed by summer 1975 (unpublished), CLU iterators were designed in September 1975, Alphard's report appeared August 1976, and both reached CACM in August 1977. Influence ran one way on the record: Alphard to CLU. I found no statement that CLU changed Alphard.
- The Alphard generator is the ancestor of the iterator protocol (an object with start, next, and done operations, driven by `for`). The CLU iterator is the ancestor of the generator function (one routine whose locals and position survive between values). The two 1975 designs are the two halves that Python later joined.
- CLU as the origin of the keyword `yield` for this purpose: every source I read that mentions the word traces it to CLU (Griswold 1981, Sather 1995, Liskov 1992), and Liskov's account of Alphard describes operations, not a yield statement. No source I read states in so many words "CLU introduced the keyword `yield`", so this is an inference, and a safe one given that the Alphard construct had no suspension point to name.
- "No first-class iterator object" follows from Liskov's text: an iterator can only be invoked by a `for` statement and "CLU only allows iterators to be nested". de Moura and Ierusalimschy classify it as a constrained, not first-class, coroutine for that reason.
- The CLU implementation is a callback: `yield` calls the loop body as a procedure on the same stack. So the first `yield` was implemented as internal iteration, IPL-V style.

### Gaps
- Atkinson's design note (CLU Design Note, September 1975) is cited by Liskov but I did not find it online, so the original wording and whether it already used `yield` are unverified.
- I could not read the Alphard 1976 report or 1977 paper in full text (DTIC refused the fetch; CACM is paywalled). Whether the Alphard papers cite CLU iterators in return is unknown.
- Whether CLU iterators could be passed as arguments (iterator-typed parameters exist in the CLU Reference Manual, from memory) was not checked. The manual (Liskov et al., MIT LCS TR-225, 1979) was located at <https://ftp.mirrorservice.org/sites/www.bitsavers.org/pdf/mit/lcs/tr/MIT-LCS-TR-0225.pdf> and not read.

## Icon: generators, `suspend`, goal-directed evaluation, co-expressions

### Takeaway
Icon (Griswold and colleagues at Arizona, from 1977; TOPLAS paper April 1981) made every expression a potential generator and drove resumption by goal-directed evaluation (implicit backtracking), where CLU allowed an iterator only in a `for` head. Its `suspend e` suspends once for every value `e` generates, so `suspend walk(t.ltree | t.rtree)` is delegation to a sub-generator, in print in 1981. Two-way value passing is not in generators; it is in co-expressions (Wampler and Griswold, 1982-1983), where `expr @ x` transmits a value into co-expression `x`.

### Cited Findings
- Lineage: the 1981 paper introduces Icon as "a new programming language that includes a goal-directed expression evaluation mechanism", places it after SNOBOL4 and SL5, and says "As in SL5, control structures are signal driven"; "Icon's type structure is similar to that of SNOBOL4 and SL5." [Generators in Icon, sections 1-2](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Snippet only (1981 Icon reference manual, TR 81-4a): "The development of Icon as a language distinct from SL5 was sparked by the design of a general goal-directed evaluation mechanism". [Icon TR 81-4a](https://smsf.cs.arizona.edu/icon/ftp/doc/tr81_4a.pdf)
- `suspend`, verbatim: "The expression `suspend e` is similar to `return e`, except that the procedure activation is left in suspension so that it may be resumed for additional computation. Execution of the procedure body is resumed at the point of suspension if the context in which the procedure invocation occurs requires another alternative. Thus, suspended procedures are dormant generators. The result of the suspend expression itself is the null value." [Generators in Icon, section 3](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- The last sentence matters for two-way passing: in 1981 the `suspend` expression's own result is the null value, so nothing comes back in through it. [Generators in Icon, section 3](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Delegation, verbatim: "Like every, suspend e produces all alternatives of e as required. For example, `suspend 1 | 2 | 5` suspends with the values 1, 2, and 5 on successive activations of the procedure in which it appears. If the procedure is activated again, execution continues with the expression following the suspend." [Generators in Icon, section 3](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Recursive delegation example, verbatim: `procedure walk(t)` ... `suspend walk(t.ltree | t.rtree)` / `return t.data`, explained as "The suspend expression suspends with each of the values generated by walking the left subtree and then with each of the values generated by walking the right subtree." [Generators in Icon, section 4.3](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- User-defined generators are full peers of built-ins: "Programmer-defined generators are fully recursive and behave exactly like built-in generators. For example, multiple activations of defined generators that are suspended simultaneously do not interfere with each other." [Generators in Icon, section 3](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- A procedure ends a sequence with `fail`; `return e` produces a final value; `every e1 do e2` forces all alternatives. Example: `every i := !x do s := s + i`. [Generators in Icon, section 3](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Icon's own comparison with CLU and Alphard: "In each of these languages, generators are only accessible in a specific context: a particular type of for statement. This is significantly more restricted than in Icon, where generators may appear anywhere." And: "It is the restricted applicability and the lack of goal-directed evaluation that differentiate these constructs from generators in Icon." [Generators in Icon, section 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Credits in the related-work section: IPL-V (earliest generators), Simula (oldest coroutine language using the term), POP-2 extensions, SL5, coroutine additions to Pascal, lazy evaluation in Lisp and APL, Alphard, CLU, and for backtracking PLANNER, PROLOG, CONNIVER, SAIL. [Generators in Icon, section 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Snippet only (a later Griswold report, TR 85-25): "several programming languages, including IPL-V, CLU and Alphard and SETL contain such features. In these languages, however, generators are restricted to certain contexts or data structures." [Icon TR 85-25](https://gmorph.cs.arizona.edu/icon/ftp/doc/tr85_25.pdf)
- Limits, per de Moura and Ierusalimschy: "Icon generators are confined within an expression and can only be invoked by explicit iteration or goal-directed evaluation. Icon generators are easier to use than CLU and Sather iterators, but they are not powerful enough to provide for programmer-defined control structures". [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.2](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Co-expressions: S. B. Wampler and R. E. Griswold, "Co-Expressions in Icon", University of Arizona TR 82-4 (April 1982), then The Computer Journal 26(1), 1983, pp. 72-78. [Icon bibliography, netlib](https://netlib.org/tex/bib/icon.html)
- Snippet only (TR 82-4): the expression `expr @ x` resumes co-expression `x` and supplies it the value `expr` produces; the transmitted value is disregarded when the co-expression is activated for its first result; refresh produces "a copy of the co-expression x restored to its state when it was created"; each co-expression has its own copies of dynamic locals. [Icon TR 82-4](https://smsf.cs.arizona.edu/icon/ftp/doc/tr82_4.pdf) (the fetch returned 404, so this is from a search summary)
- Transmission survives in Icon's descendants: Object Icon documents `val@ce` as transmitting `val` to `ce`, and `&source` as the activating co-expression. [Object Icon, Coexpressions](https://objecticon.sourceforge.net/Coexpressions.html)
- Classification: "First-class coroutines are provided, for instance, by Icon co-expressions and the coroutine facilities implemented by Simula, BCPL, and Modula-2." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.2](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)

### Inferences
- Icon versus CLU, in three points the sources support: (1) where a generator may appear (anywhere versus a `for` head); (2) what drives resumption (failure of the surrounding expression, or `every`, versus the loop); (3) composition (`suspend expr` re-suspends every result of a generator expression, so a recursive tree walk is one line; CLU needs a nested `for` with a `yield` in its body).
- `suspend e` over a generator expression is the earliest delegation form I found in print (April 1981). It forwards values outward only. It has no return value from the sub-generator and no inward channel, since `suspend` itself evaluates to null.
- Icon splits what Python later merged: generators are one-way and expression-bound; co-expressions are first-class, can be activated from anywhere, and accept a transmitted value. The "first transmitted value is discarded" rule is the same problem Python's "can't send non-None value to a just-started generator" rule addresses, solved the other way.
- Icon credits SL5 and SNOBOL4 as parents and treats CLU and Alphard as contemporaries to be contrasted, not as sources.

### Gaps
- Griswold and Griswold, "History of the Icon Programming Language" (HOPL II, 1993): not retrieved. Dates for when `suspend` and co-expressions entered (the 1977-1979 window, Icon version numbers) and Griswold's own statement of influences are therefore missing. A secondary Wikipedia mirror says Griswold returned to the work in 1977 and replaced SL5's procedure mechanism with suspend/resume; I did not use that mirror as a source because the page carried injected text.
- The modern Icon refresh operator is `^x` and creation is `create expr` (from memory, per the Icon book); the 1981 report in the search summary shows an older `*X` notation. Not verified in the book's text.
- SL5's own mechanism (Hanson and Griswold, "The SL5 procedure mechanism", CACM 21(5), May 1978): cited by the Icon paper, not read. From memory it decomposed procedure invocation into create, bind arguments, and resume, which gave coroutines directly; unverified.

## Sather iterators ("iters")

### Takeaway
Sather 1 (designed after the May 1991 release of Sather 0; paper dated October 1995, TOPLAS 1996) took the name and the initial design from CLU, says so explicitly, and removed three CLU limits: several iterators per loop, iterators anywhere in the loop body as expressions, and "hot" arguments re-evaluated and passed in on every resumption. Hot arguments are the first design I found in which a `yield`-style routine receives a fresh value from its consumer at each resumption. Iterators stayed bound to one call point in one loop, so they are still not first-class.

### Cited Findings
- Credit to CLU, verbatim: "The name 'iterator' and the initial design were derived from the iterator construct in the CLU language". Abstract: "Sather iterators were derived from CLU iterators but are much more flexible and better suited for object-oriented programming." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, abstract and section 1](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Dates: "The first version of the language ('Sather 0') was released in May, 1991. Feedback from users and our own use led to the design of 'Sather 1' which incorporated a number of new language constructs." Sather 0 "had a fairly conventional until ... loop ... end statement" and used cursor objects. The statistics in the paper are from version 1.0.7 of the compiler. [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, introduction and section 5](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- The three CLU limits Sather removes: "One iterator per loop: There is no simple way to step through two structures simultaneously in CLU." "No way to modify elements". "Iterator arguments are loop invariant: There is no clean way to pass loop variant values to an iterator." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 1](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Extension 1: "Multiple iterators may be invoked within a single loop, because they may occur as expressions anywhere in the loop and are not restricted to the loop head. ... The loop terminates as soon as the first of the iterators terminates." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 1](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Extension 2, hot arguments: "Sather provides hot iterator arguments which are reevaluated each time the control is passed back to the iterator. Such arguments may be used to pass data to the iterator which varies on each iteration. In contrast to CLU iterators which may only generate a sequence of values, these arguments allow classes to define iterators that modify successive elements of a structure, i.e. to 'consume' a sequence of values. In Sather iterator arguments are hot unless they are declared with the once keyword, but self is never hot." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 1](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- `yield` and `quit`: "Similar to CLU, Sather iterators look like routines except that they may yield or quit instead of returning from a call." "yield statement: ... Its execution causes control and any return values to be passed back to the calling loop statement, resuming execution just after the iterator call." "quit statement: ... Its execution causes the corresponding loop statement to terminate immediately. Exiting from the body of an iterator method is considered an implicit execution of a quit statement." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, sections 1 and 3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Naming and scope: iterator names end in `!` ("This alerts the reader to all places where the control flow may change"); "Iterators may only be called within loop statements"; "An iterator call is always bound to the innermost lexically enclosing loop statement"; "Each textual iterator call maintains the state of execution of its iterator." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, sections 2 and 3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- A consuming iterator, verbatim from the paper: `set!(x:T) is loop [ind!]:=x; yield end end`, used as `loop a.set!(2*a.elt!) end` to double an array, and `sum!(summand:INT):INT` used as `loop x:=sum!(a.elt!*b.elt!) end`. [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, sections 2.2-2.3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Delegation is written as a loop: `inorder!:T is ... loop yield left.inorder! end; yield key; loop yield right.inorder! end`. [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, figure 2](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- `yield` and exceptions: "yield is not permitted within a protect statement, which prevents the creation of dynamically protected regions which overlap instead of properly nesting in time." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Self-classification: "iterators may be thought of as structured coroutines"; "Coroutines with this added restriction of always returning to their caller are sometimes called generators, semicoroutines, or hierarchical coroutines [8]" (Marlin 1980). "However, just as for streams, a generator's activation state is not bound to a single call point within a single loop. Instead, a generator may be left in a half-consumed state at one point and picked up at another. ... this is not possible with iterators." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 4.4](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- First-class variant: "A variant of Sather under development at the University of Karlsruhe, Germany ('Sather-K' ...) does generalize iterators by introducing first class stream objects that retain their state across loop termination" (Goos, TR 8/94, Karlsruhe, 1994). [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 4.3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Measured use: of 980 loops in the 1.0.7 compiler and libraries (about 39,000 lines), "Only 17 percent used two or more [iterators] within a loop, so CLU iterators could have been used for most cases", and "151 (15 percent) used one or more iterators with hot arguments which could not have been expressed in CLU." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 5](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Other credits: IPL-V and Conniver (generalized control), cursors and Oberon riders, SICP streams, Common Lisp series/generators/gatherers, Marlin and Wirth for coroutines, Smalltalk blocks, Scheme closures, Hewitt's same-fringe problem. Acknowledgement: Ari Huttunen "made suggestions which improved the design of iterators". [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, sections 4 and 7](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Outside view: "Sather iterators [Murer et al. 1996], inspired by CLU iterators, are also confined to a single call point within a loop construct. The number of iterators invoked per loop is not restricted as in CLU, but if any iterator terminates, the loop terminates. ... asynchronous traversals, as required for merging data collections, have no simple solution." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.2](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)

### Inferences
- Sather's hot argument is a per-resumption inward channel: the caller's expression is re-evaluated and delivered each time control re-enters the iterator. It is the closest pre-Python relative of `send()`, with one difference: the value arrives through the parameter, not as the value of the `yield` expression.
- Sather's `quit` is the first named "terminate the consumer's loop from inside the producer" statement in this lineage, distinct from falling off the end (which implies it). Conniver's ADIEU is the older analog, which the Sather authors themselves point out.
- The Sather paper already states the distinction de Moura and Ierusalimschy formalized: an iterator is bound to a call point, a generator object can be carried around half-consumed.

### Gaps
- The exact year iters entered Sather (1.0 release, 1993 or 1994): the paper says only "Sather 1" after 1991, and "over the last year" for experience as of October 1995. An earlier ICSI report (Murer, Omohundro, Szyperski, "Sather Iters: Object-Oriented Iteration Abstraction", TR-93-045, 1993, from memory) would date it; not retrieved.
- Whether Python's designers cited Sather is out of my scope (PEPs are assigned to another researcher).

## Scheme continuations, and the de Moura and Ierusalimschy classification

### Takeaway
"Revisiting Coroutines" (TOPLAS 2009; an earlier form is the authors' 2004 work) classifies coroutine facilities on three axes: symmetric versus asymmetric control transfer, first-class versus constrained, stackful versus not. It defines a "full coroutine" as first-class and stackful and proves full coroutines equivalent in expressive power to one-shot continuations. It places CLU and Sather iterators and Icon generators as constrained, Python generators as first-class but not stackful, and Simula, BCPL, Modula-2, Icon co-expressions, and Lua as first-class.

### Cited Findings
- Baseline definition (Marlin's 1980 thesis, as quoted): a coroutine has two fundamental characteristics: "the values of data local to a coroutine persist between successive calls" and "the execution of a coroutine is suspended as control leaves it, only to carry on where it left off when control re-enters the coroutine at some later stage." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- The three axes, verbatim: "the control-transfer mechanism, which can provide symmetric or asymmetric coroutines; whether coroutines are provided in the language as first-class objects, which can be freely manipulated by the programmer, or as constrained constructs; whether a coroutine is implemented as a stackful construct, that is, whether it is able to suspend its execution from within nested calls." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Axis 1: "Symmetric coroutine facilities provide a single control-transfer operation that allows coroutines to explicitly pass control among themselves. Asymmetric coroutine mechanisms (more commonly denoted as semisymmetric or semi coroutines [Dahl et al. 1972]) provide two control-transfer operations: one for invoking a coroutine and one for suspending it, the latter returning control to the coroutine invoker." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Placement on axis 1: Modula-2 is symmetric; iterators (CLU, Sather) and generators (Icon, Python) are asymmetric; "The general-purpose coroutine mechanisms implemented by Simula and BCPL provide both types of control transfer." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Axis 2: in constrained designs "coroutine objects are constrained within a textual bound and cannot be directly manipulated by the programmer." Constrained: CLU iterators, Sather iterators, Icon generators. First-class: "Icon co-expressions and the coroutine facilities implemented by Simula, BCPL, and Modula-2." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.2](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Axis 3: "Stackful coroutine mechanisms allow coroutines to suspend their execution from within nested functions ... Stackful coroutine mechanisms are provided, for instance, by Simula and Modula-2." On Python (2001): "Despite constituting a first-class object, a Python generator is not a stackful construct; it can only suspend its execution when its control stack is at the same level that it was at creation time." Consequence: "if items are produced within recursive or auxiliary functions, it is necessary to create a hierarchy of auxiliary generators that yield in succession until the original invocation point is reached." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.3](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Full coroutine: "We then introduce the concept of a full coroutine as a first-class, stackful object which ... can provide the same expressiveness as obtained with one-shot continuations. Full coroutines can be either symmetric or asymmetric; the selection of a particular control-transfer mechanism does not influence their expressive power." [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.4](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Main results (abstract and section 1): full coroutines "have an expressive power equivalent to one-shot continuations and one-shot delimited continuations", and "symmetric and asymmetric coroutines have equivalent expressive power". Lua "since its version 5.0 ... provides full asymmetric coroutines". [Revisiting Coroutines, sections 1 and 4.1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- On Simula's role in the folklore: its mechanism "greatly contributed to the common misconception that symmetric and asymmetric coroutines are not equally powerful". [Revisiting Coroutines, section 2.1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- On Scheme and continuations: "the introduction of first-class continuations [Kelsey et al. 1998] also contributed to the virtual end of research interest in coroutines as a general control abstraction", since continuations "can be used to implement several interesting features, including generators, exception handling, backtracking [Felleisen 1985; Haynes 1987], multitasking at the source level [Dybvig and Hieb 1989; Wand 1980], and also coroutines [Haynes et al. 1986]. However, with the exception of Scheme, some implementations of ML [Harper et al. 1991], and an alternative implementation of Python [Tismer 2000], first-class continuations are not usually provided". [Revisiting Coroutines, section 1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Why coroutines faded, per the authors: no precise definition, the arrival of first-class continuations, and "the current adoption of multithreading as a de facto standard for concurrent programming". The only general-purpose languages they list with coroutines before the 2000s are "Simula [Birtwistle et al. 1980], BCPL [Moody and Richards 1980], Modula-2 [Wirth 1985], and Icon [Griswold and Griswold 1983]". [Revisiting Coroutines, section 1](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Scheme itself begins with Sussman and Steele, "Scheme: An Interpreter for Extended Lambda Calculus", MIT AI Memo 349, December 1975 (as cited by the Sather authors for closures). [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, references](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)

### Inferences
- A table the report can use, assembled from the placements above (the paper gives no single table):

  | Construct | Transfer | First-class | Stackful |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Conway 1963 | symmetric (by his definition: "no master program") | n/a (assembly routers) | n/a |
  | Simula 67 | both | yes | yes |
  | BCPL (1980) | both | yes | not stated in the section I read |
  | Modula-2 | symmetric | yes | yes |
  | CLU iterators | asymmetric | no (one per `for`) | not stated |
  | Icon generators | asymmetric | no (expression-bound) | not stated |
  | Icon co-expressions | not stated | yes | not stated |
  | Sather iterators | asymmetric | no (one call point) | not stated |
  | Python 2.2 generators | asymmetric | yes | no |
  | Lua 5.0 coroutines | asymmetric | yes | yes |

  The Conway row is my reading of his paper, not the 2009 paper's.
- The "hierarchy of auxiliary generators that yield in succession" sentence describes exactly the problem that delegation syntax (Icon's `suspend expr`, Sather's `loop yield it! end`) works around in non-stackful designs.

### Gaps
- No primary Scheme source was read. The standard citations for generators and coroutines from `call/cc` are Haynes, Friedman, and Wand, "Continuations and coroutines" (1984 Lisp and Functional Programming conference; journal version 1986) and Wand 1980; these are known to me only through the reference list above and memory. The date `call-with-current-continuation` entered Scheme reports (from memory: `catch` in 1975, the long name by the Revised Report of 1978 and R2RS/R3RS in the mid 1980s) is unverified.
- The 2004 version of the paper (de Moura, Rodriguez, Ierusalimschy, "Coroutines in Lua", Journal of Universal Computer Science, 2004) was not read. Whether the three-axis classification already appears there in the same words is unverified.
- Marlin's thesis (Springer LNCS 95, 1980) is quoted only through the 2009 paper.

## Other early languages: BCPL, Modula-2, SL5, Smalltalk streams, Lisp series and generators, ABC

### Takeaway
BCPL (Moody and Richards, 1980) and Modula-2 (Wirth, early 1980s) had library-level first-class coroutines and no generator syntax; Common Lisp got a "generators and gatherers" proposal beside Waters's series in CLtL2 (1990). For SL5, Smalltalk streams, and ABC I found only indirect or no sources, and the notes say so.

### Cited Findings
- BCPL: J. K. Moody and M. Richards, "A coroutine mechanism for BCPL", Software: Practice and Experience 10(10), 1980. The abstract describes a simple coroutine mechanism designed to fit BCPL's philosophy. [Wiley record via Zendy](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/spe.4380101002)
- BCPL's library routines are `createco`, `deleteco`, `callco`, `cowait`, and `resumeco`. [Cambridge exam paper 2004](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/teaching/exams/pastpapers/y2004p4q4.pdf); [Richards, tcobench](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~mr10/tcobench.pdf)
- BCPL offers both symmetric and asymmetric transfer and first-class coroutines. [Revisiting Coroutines, sections 2.1-2.2](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Modula-2: coroutines are created with `NEWPROCESS` (own stack, size supplied by the programmer) and switched with `TRANSFER`, both from module `SYSTEM`; Wirth's "Programming in Modula-2" (Springer, 1982/1983) has a chapter on concurrent processes and coroutines. [Ulm Oberon article on coroutines](https://www.mathematik.uni-ulm.de/oberon/0.5/articles/coroutines.html); [Springer chapter record](https://www.springerprofessional.de/concurrent-processes-and-coroutines/14532908)
- Modula-2 is the paper's example of symmetric coroutines used "to represent independent units of execution", and of a stackful mechanism. [Revisiting Coroutines, sections 2.1 and 2.3](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- SL5: named by Griswold (1981) among coroutine languages in which "generator" names a coroutine usage, citing Hanson and Griswold, "The SL5 procedure mechanism", CACM 21(5), 1978; Icon keeps SL5's signal-driven control structures. [Generators in Icon, sections 2 and 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Coroutine Pascal and POP-2 extensions are also named there as generator-bearing coroutine designs of the 1970s. [Generators in Icon, section 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Common Lisp: CLtL2 (Steele, 1990) Appendix B, "Generators and Gatherers", by Crispin Perdue and Richard C. Waters, presents them as an approach to functional-style iteration closely related to series (Appendix A, Waters). [CLtL2, Appendix B](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/afs/cs.cmu.edu/project/ai-repository/ai/html/cltl/clm/node362.html)
- The Sather authors' judgment of that proposal: "For Common Lisp the incorporation of series, generators and gatherers has been proposed for defining iterative constructs [14]. These constructs are complex and include a large number of built-in operations." [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 4.3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Lazy streams: the Icon paper (1981) notes "a substantial amount of work on the 'lazy evaluation' approach to the incremental generation of sequences, usually in LISP or APL frameworks", and the Sather paper compares iterators to SICP streams (Abelson and Sussman, 1985): "streams may be passed around in a half-consumed state, the state of an iterator is confined to its calling loop statement". [Generators in Icon, section 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf); [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 4.3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Smalltalk: the Sather paper treats Smalltalk-80 (Goldberg and Robson) as the model of block-based internal iteration: "The container classes provide methods to apply a block to all or part of their elements. The execution of such block-based iterations is controlled by the container class", which fails for simultaneous traversal of two structures. [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 4.5](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- C++ and Oberon: "Ellis and Stroustrup [2] call the use of cursor objects in C++ 'iterators'" (1990); Oberon "riders" are the same cursor idea. [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, section 4.2](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)

### Inferences
- By 1995 the word "iterator" already had two meanings in print: CLU's suspending routine and C++'s cursor object. The Sather paper is a dated witness to the split.
- The designs fall into two families by lineage. Library coroutines with explicit transfer (Simula, BCPL, Modula-2, later Lua) descend from Conway and Simula. Loop-bound `yield` routines (CLU, Sather) descend from Alphard's data-abstraction work. Icon sits between: its generators come from SNOBOL/SL5 backtracking, and its co-expressions rejoin the coroutine family.

### Gaps
- ABC (Geurts, Meertens, Pemberton; CWI, 1980s): I found no source stating that ABC had generators, iterators with suspension, or coroutines, and none stating that it lacked them. From memory, ABC had `FOR ... IN` over its built-in collection types and no user-defined iteration construct. Treat as unverified; the ABC Programmer's Handbook is the source to check.
- Smalltalk-80 `Stream` classes (`ReadStream`, `next`, `atEnd`): not sourced. From memory they are external cursor objects in the 1983 Blue Book, with no suspension. Unverified.
- Waters's series: the primary sources (Waters, "Optimization of Series Expressions", MIT AI Memos 1082 and 1083, 1989; CLtL2 Appendix A) were not read. Steele's note that generators were first proposed by Pavel Curtis appeared in a search summary only.
- BCPL: the Moody and Richards paper's text was not read, so the semantics of `callco`/`cowait`/`resumeco` (asymmetric call and wait, symmetric resume, from memory) rest on the 2009 paper's one-line classification.

## Which earlier language each design credited

### Takeaway
The documented credit chain is short and one-directional: IPL-V is cited as the first generator language by Alphard, Icon, and Sather; Alphard is credited by CLU; CLU is credited by Sather; SNOBOL4 and SL5 are credited by Icon; Simula is credited as the first coroutine language by Icon and by Knuth.

### Cited Findings
- Conway (1963) credits no earlier language, and shares the idea with Joel Erdwinn. [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Alphard (1976 report) relates its generators to IPL-V generators and Lisp mapping functions (snippet only). [DTIC ADA030286](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA030286.pdf)
- CLU credits Alphard: "Iterators were inspired by a construct in Alphard called a 'generator'". [A History of CLU, section 3.10](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf)
- Icon credits SNOBOL4 and SL5 as its parents, and lists IPL-V, Simula, POP-2, SL5, coroutine Pascal, Alphard, and CLU as related work. [Generators in Icon, sections 1 and 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)
- Sather credits CLU for the name and initial design, Eiffel for the language as a whole ("originally derived from Eiffel"), and IPL-V and Conniver as the early history of the idea. [Iteration Abstraction in Sather, introduction and section 4.1](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)
- Lua's designers cite Conway, Marlin, Simula, BCPL, Modula-2, Icon, CLU, Sather, and Python, and adopt Dahl's "semi coroutine" term for the asymmetric kind. [Revisiting Coroutines, sections 1-2](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/roberto-ierusalimschy/revisiting-coroutines.pdf)
- Knuth credits Conway for coroutines and Simula 67 for language support. [Knuth and Ruskey, arXiv cs/0404058](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0404058)

### Inferences
- A dated timeline the sources support:
  - 1958: Conway develops and names the coroutine (Knuth, via secondary sources).
  - 1959-1961: IPL-V generators (Newell and Tonge; manual 1961).
  - July 1963: Conway's paper, first publication.
  - 1967-1968: Simula 67, `call`/`detach`/`resume`.
  - 1972-1974: Conniver generators and possibility lists.
  - Summer 1975: Alphard generators shown to the CLU group. September 1975: Atkinson's CLU iterator design note, `yield`.
  - August 1976 and August 1977: Alphard report and CACM paper. 1977: CLU CACM paper.
  - 1977-1978: SL5 procedure mechanism published; Icon begins.
  - 1980: Marlin's thesis; BCPL coroutines paper.
  - April 1981: "Generators in Icon", `suspend`, `suspend expr` delegation.
  - 1982-1983: Icon co-expressions, `@` transmission. Modula-2 book.
  - 1990: CLtL2 series, generators, gatherers.
  - May 1991: Sather 0. By 1995: Sather 1 iters with `yield`, `quit`, hot arguments. 1994: Sather-K first-class streams.
- No source I read claims a direct Icon to CLU or CLU to Icon influence. They are parallel lines that cite each other only to contrast.
