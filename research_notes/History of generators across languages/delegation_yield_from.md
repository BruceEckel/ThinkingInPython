## Earliest precedents: Icon, CLU, Sather, Cω, the 2005 nested-iterators paper, F#, Haskell

### Takeaway
Python's PEP 380 was not the first delegation construct. The direct line that can be documented with quotes is Cω's flattened streams (published 2005, language work from about 2003), then `yield foreach` in Jacobs, Meijer, Piessens, and Schulte (May 2005), which says in so many words that it "follow[s] the design of streams in Cω". F#'s `yield!` (2007) and Icon's `suspend` are earlier than PEP 380 as well, but PEP 380 cites none of them.

### Cited Findings
- Cω: "A vital aspect of Cω streams is that they are always flattened; there are no nested streams of streams. Cω streams thus coincide with XPath/XQuery sequences which are also flattened." The paper's recursive example is `virtual int* FromTo2(int b, int e){ if (b>e) yield break; yield return b; yield return FromTo2(b+1,e); }`, and it says "The recursive call `yield return FromTo2(b+1,n);` yields a stream of integers. As the type system treats the types int* and int** as equivalent this is type correct." So in Cω delegation has no separate keyword: `yield return` of a stream is delegation. ([Bierman, Meijer, Schulte, "The essence of data access in Cω", ECOOP 2005, author copy](https://gavinbierman.github.io/assets/pdf/ecoop2005-corrected.pdf); bibliographic data: ECOOP 2005, LNCS 3586, pp. 287-311, [researchr entry](https://researchr.org/publication/BiermanMS05))
- Cω states the performance reason in the same passage: "Without flattening we would be forced to copy the stream produced by the recursive invocation, leading to a quadratic instead of a linear number of yields", followed by the `foreach (int i in FromTo3(b+1,e)) yield return i;` form as the slow version. ([same paper](https://gavinbierman.github.io/assets/pdf/ecoop2005-corrected.pdf))
- Jacobs, Meijer, Piessens, Schulte, "Iterators revisited: proof rules and implementation" (FTfJP 2005; the PDF's creation date is 2005-05-14). Abstract: "when iterator methods are used to perform recursive enumerations, such as when enumerating the nodes of a tree, the worst-case running time often becomes quadratic. We propose an extension of iterator methods called nested iterator methods, which simplify the coding of recursive enumerations, and which reduce the running time cost of such coding patterns to a linear function by retaining the recursion stack between iteration steps." ([Microsoft Research PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/specsharp-iterators.pdf))
- The paper names its precedent: "Our newly proposed nested iterators, which follow the design of streams in Cω [4], simplify programming of recursive iterators, improve performance and as we will see later also facilitate verification." The syntax is `yield foreach Traverse(c);`, and "Nested iterator methods are iterator methods that contain yield foreach statements." ([same PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/specsharp-iterators.pdf))
- The paper on what Cω lacked: "In fact Cω even proposes the use of nested iterators, but the study outlined in [4] is restricted to semantical issues and doesn't provide any clue for efficient translations of nested iterators, which we address." ([same PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/specsharp-iterators.pdf))
- The paper's related-work section on older languages: "Iterators in CLU, developed at MIT in the early 80's [13] were very restricted". On Sather: "Sather iterators or iters [15, 16] and loops are strictly more expressive than C# 2.0 iterators", and it quotes the Sather authors: "Our current implementation work is focussing on the optimization of recursive iters and some of the more complex loop structures. In appropriate circumstances, recursive iters can use more efficient stack structures than the direct coroutine implementation would." The paper adds: "However, we are not aware of any further results of this work." Icon appears only in the bibliography (Griswold and Griswold, The Icon Programming Language, 1990), with no discussion. ([same PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/specsharp-iterators.pdf))
- The implementation in the paper is a stack of enumerators: generated enumerators derive from `NestedEnumerator<T>`, whose `NestedMoveNext` returns one of `Value, Enumerator, Done, TailEnumerator`, and a root enumerator "operates on a stack of nested enumerators". This is the same idea C++23 later uses (a stack of coroutine handles). ([same PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/specsharp-iterators.pdf))
- A Microsoft patent by Meijer and Schulte, "Fast flattening of nested streams" (US 7,937,694), describes flattening "in linear time, by yielding each value in the nested streams at most once", in contrast with "a quadratic number of yields". ([patent](https://uspto.report/patent/grant/7937694); application [20050183058](https://patents.justia.com/patent/20050183058))
- Icon: a Griswold-authored Icon newsletter/Analyst item says "suspend is like every — it forces its argument to generate all its results" (surfaced in search of the Icon Analyst back issues; the two candidate issues are [IA19](https://smsf.cs.arizona.edu/icon/analyst/backiss/IA19.pdf) and [IA23](https://smsf.cs.arizona.edu/icon/analyst/backiss/IA23.pdf), and I did not open the PDFs to confirm which one holds the sentence).
- F#: `yield!` is the delegation form in computation expressions; the builder method behind it is named `YieldFrom` ("yield! e (requires YieldFrom method)"). ([Don Syme, "The Early History of F#", HOPL 2020](https://fsharp.org/history/hopl-final/hopl-fsharp.pdf))
- F# dating: Don Syme's blog says "One of the things we added to F# in version 1.9.2 is a syntax for computation expressions. These are a generalization of the sequence expressions added in F# 1.9.1." ([Syme, "Some Details on F# Computation Expressions"](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/dsyme/some-details-on-f-computation-expressions)); Petricek and Syme: "Computation expressions have been available in F# since 2007" ([The F# Computation Expression Zoo](https://tomasp.net/academic/papers/computation-zoo)).
- F# background, per Syme: "other important background included Haskell list comprehension syntax, Haskell 'do notation' and Haskell's experimental 'arrow' syntax ... Other background includes C# LINQ expressions". He also notes that for Haskell readers "it is natural to think of this as offering a corresponding notation for MonadPlus". The HOPL paper does not name a precedent for `yield!` specifically. ([HOPL 2020](https://fsharp.org/history/hopl-final/hopl-fsharp.pdf))

### Inferences
- The documented chain is Cω (typed flattening, no keyword) to `yield foreach` (Jacobs et al., explicit keyword plus linear-time implementation). Erik Meijer and Wolfram Schulte are authors of both, so the link is personal as well as cited.
- Cω's own stated precedent for flattening is XPath/XQuery sequences, not an earlier generator language. Haskell's `concatMap`/list monad is a conceptual ancestor only by inference: no Cω or Jacobs sentence found cites it, although Meijer's background makes it plausible. F#'s computation expressions do cite Haskell.
- F# `yield!` (2007) predates PEP 380 by about two years, and its builder method is named `YieldFrom`. Whether that method name predates or postdates Python's `yield from` was not established (see Gaps), so no direction of influence can be asserted.
- Icon's `suspend expr` is delegation in effect, since any Icon expression can be a generator and `suspend` produces each of its results. That makes Icon (1977 on) the earliest, but as a consequence of goal-directed evaluation and not as a designed "delegate" construct.

### Gaps
- I did not verify Icon's `suspend` semantics from the Icon book or reference manual, only from a search snippet of a Griswold newsletter sentence. The exact issue and date need a read of IA19/IA23.
- CLU: nothing found suggesting a delegation construct; CLU iterators can be called recursively from a `for` loop, re-yielding by hand. Stated from the Jacobs paper's characterization ("very restricted"), not from the CLU manual.
- Whether F# 1.9.1's sequence expressions already had `yield!` (and the older `->>` form), or whether it arrived with 1.9.2, was not confirmed. The search found a forum post where Syme recommends "yield/yield!" over "->" and "->>" but no release note. The year 2007 is solid; the point release is not.
- The Cω compiler preview release date (commonly given as 2004) and the earlier "Unifying Tables, Objects and Documents" paper (2003) were not opened; the 2003-2004 dating of nested streams rests on the 2005 paper describing a language "currently under development", and on the patent family.
- Spec#: the Jacobs paper says its rules "are the basis for the Spec# compiler", but I found no source saying shipped Spec# accepted `yield foreach`.

## The performance motive: who stated the quadratic cost first

### Takeaway
The cost was stated in the C# world before Python: Grant Richins (March-April 2004) on allocations, Stephen Toub (October 2004) on O(N log N) `MoveNext` chains, then the Cω and Jacobs et al. papers (2005) with the word "quadratic". Greg Ewing's first proto-PEP (12 February 2009) states the same O(n) to O(n**2) cost with no citation, and Wes Dyer's March 2007 post is a popularization of the 2005 paper, not the first statement. Lippert's contribution is the 2009 explanation of why C# did not ship the fix.

### Cited Findings
- Stephen Toub, 2004-10-29: "As Grant has previously pointed out, the above code will produce O(N) iterator objects, where N is the number of nodes in the tree." The links in that sentence carry the dates of Grant Richins's two posts: `grantri/archive/2004/04/08/110165` and `grantri/archive/2004/03/24/95787`. ([Toub, "Recursive iterator performance"](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/toub/recursive-iterator-performance))
- Toub, same post: "whenever the next value is yielded from an iterator, this value is returned up through all of the yield statements in each iterator (i.e. a node M deep will return through M calls to MoveNext on the various levels of iterators) ... we've also changed our iteration algorithm from O(N), looking at each node once, to approximately O(N log N)". He measured Towers of Hanoi with 23 disks: "The recursive version required 20.94 seconds to run (yes, seconds) versus 0.83 seconds for the iterative version." ([same post](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/toub/recursive-iterator-performance))
- Jacobs et al. cite Richins as prior art: "Grant Richins notes in his weblog [18] the fact that recursive iteration of the nodes of a tree using C# 2.0 iterators performs a number of object allocations that is linear in the number of nodes. To solve this, in a further posting [19] he eliminates the recursive invocation of iterators by keeping track of the current path using a stack data structure." The two references are titled "Recursive iterators (aka perf killers)" and "Recursive iterators made iterative". ([Jacobs et al. 2005](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/specsharp-iterators.pdf))
- Jacobs et al., introduction: "The current iterators will for example have a quadratic behavior when they are used to return all text nodes from an Xml document, since they copy the stream produced by the recursive invocation." ([same PDF](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/specsharp-iterators.pdf))
- Cω: "leading to a quadratic instead of a linear number of yields" (quoted in the previous section). ([ECOOP 2005 paper](https://gavinbierman.github.io/assets/pdf/ecoop2005-corrected.pdf))
- Wes Dyer, 2007-03-23, on chained `Concat`: "The time to evalute the foreach statement is not linearly proportional to the number of concats that are composed together. In fact it is proportional to the square of the number of concats composed together." Under the heading "One Possibility for Language Improvement (not in Orcas)": "Bart Jacobs, Erik Meijer, Frank Piessens, and Wolfram Shulte wrote a very interesting paper on a possible language improvement that would improve both the usability and the performance of iterators. The second half of the paper details what they call nested iterators which avoid the multiple evaluation problem of the composed Concats and implicitly keep an internal stack like the modified n-ary tree iterator." And: "If instead of the yield foreach, there was the foreach expansion that yielded each result then the FromTo method would suffer from quadratic performance; however, with nested iterators the performance would be linear." ([Dyer, "All About Iterators"](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/wesdyer/all-about-iterators))
- A commenter on Dyer's post (2007-03-22): "In the LINQ forums way-back, I'd suggested 'yield enumerable' instead of 'yield foreach', with the same behavior". ([same page](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/wesdyer/all-about-iterators))
- Greg Ewing's opening proto-PEP message, python-ideas, 2009-02-12: the cost of re-yielding through a chain "can cause what ought to be an O(n) operation to become O(n**2)." ([thread "Proto-PEP on a 'yield from' statement"](https://mail.python.org/archives/list/python-ideas@python.org/thread/QYKLE5SBGTV2ZEA3LD3GL7GOXMUL57DL))
- PEP 380, Optimisations: "Using a specialised syntax opens up possibilities for optimisation when there is a long chain of generators." ([PEP 380](https://peps.python.org/pep-0380/))
- C++ repeats the argument in 2022: "a solution that has each generator manually iterating over elements of the child generator requires O(depth) coroutine resumptions/suspensions per element of the sequence." ([P2502R2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2022/p2502r2.pdf))
- JavaScript community, later: a TC39 discourse thread titled "Long Generator Delegation Chain Optimization - O(N^2)" notes that Python addressed this in PEP 380 and that engines could do the same for `yield*`. ([es.discourse.group thread](https://es.discourse.group/t/long-generator-delegation-chain-optimization-o-n-2/2501))

### Inferences
- Earliest located statement: Richins, 24 March and 8 April 2004 (allocation count). Earliest located "quadratic" wording: the Cω/Jacobs papers in the first half of 2005. Toub's O(N log N) is the balanced-tree case of the same effect; quadratic is the degenerate (list-shaped) case.
- Ewing gives the same analysis in 2009 without citing C#. Independent rediscovery is the simplest reading, and no message found contradicts it.
- The brief's attribution to "Wes Dyer and Eric Lippert" is late: Dyer (2007) credits the 2005 paper, and Lippert's quotable statement (2009) is about priorities, not about the cost.

### Gaps
- I did not open Richins's two posts; their dates come from the URL paths in Toub's links and their titles from the Jacobs bibliography.
- The Sather papers (Murer, Omohundro, Stoutamire, Szyperski, about 1993-1996) may contain an earlier complexity statement for recursive iters. I have only the sentence the Jacobs paper quotes, which mentions optimization of recursive iters without the word quadratic.
- PEP 380's Optimisations section was fetched in summary; I did not capture whether the O(n**2) sentence survives verbatim in the final PEP text, only that it is in the 12 February 2009 message.

## Python: PEP 380, the 2009 threads, and what `yield from` added

### Takeaway
Greg Ewing posted the first draft to python-ideas on 12 February 2009; the PEP is dated 13 February 2009, was accepted by Guido van Rossum on 26 June 2011, and shipped in Python 3.3. Neither the PEP nor the thread messages I read cite another language. What is new with PEP 380, compared with Cω, `yield foreach`, and F# `yield!`, is two-way delegation: `send()`, `throw()`, and `close()` pass through to the subgenerator, and the subgenerator's `return` value becomes the value of the `yield from` expression. The motive in the thread is coroutines ("lightweight threads"), replacing PEP 342's trampoline.

### Cited Findings
- Dates: "Created: 13-Feb-2009", "Python-Version: 3.3", "Guido officially accepted the PEP on 26th June, 2011." ([PEP 380](https://peps.python.org/pep-0380/))
- Motivation: "A Python generator is a form of coroutine, but has the limitation that it can only yield to its immediate caller." And: "However, if the subgenerator is to interact properly with the caller in the case of calls to `send()`, `throw()` and `close()`, things become considerably more difficult." ([PEP 380](https://peps.python.org/pep-0380/))
- Semantics: "Any values sent to the delegating generator using `send()` are passed directly to the iterator." "Exceptions other than GeneratorExit thrown into the delegating generator are passed to the `throw()` method of the iterator." "The value of the `yield from` expression is the first argument to the `StopIteration` exception". "`return expr` in a generator causes `StopIteration(expr)` to be raised upon exit from the generator." ([PEP 380](https://peps.python.org/pep-0380/))
- Prior Python proposals, the only precedent the PEP names: "Proposals along similar lines have been made before, some using the syntax `yield *` instead of `yield from`. While `yield *` is more concise, it could be argued that it looks too similar to an ordinary `yield`". The same first sentence is in the 12 February 2009 opening message. ([PEP 380](https://peps.python.org/pep-0380/); [opening message](https://mail.python.org/archives/list/python-ideas@python.org/thread/QYKLE5SBGTV2ZEA3LD3GL7GOXMUL57DL))
- Rejected keywords: "Rejected alternatives include `call`, `delegate` and `gcall`." ([PEP 380](https://peps.python.org/pep-0380/))
- `throw()` and `close()` forwarding entered after the first draft, from Bruce Frederiksen's review. Ewing, 2009-02-13, on forwarding thrown exceptions: "I'd forgotten about that feature!" Frederiksen in the same exchange said he was "against using return as the syntax for a final value from the subgenerator", and Ewing pointed him to the "Generators as Threads" section. ([python-ideas, 2009-02-13](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-ideas/2009-February/002892.html))
- Guido van Rossum, 2009-02-17, on the return value: "There better be a pretty darn good reason to do this." and "I really don't like overloading return this way." On treating `send()` as `next()` for plain iterators: he was "not sure I like this interpretation of .send()". He also wrote: "I recommend that you produce a working implementation of this." ([python-ideas, 2009-02-17](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-ideas/2009-February/002951.html))
- Guido, python-dev, 2009-03-26, giving the motive as he understood it: "ISTR that the motivation for adding new syntax is that the best you can do using a trampoline library is still pretty cumbersome to use when you have to write a lot of tasks and subtasks, ... when using tasks is just a tool for getting things done rather than an end goal in itself." In the same thread P.J. Eby objected: "IMO it otherwise lacks motivation for a language feature vs. say, a stdlib module." Ewing, quoted there: "I wanted a way of writing suspendable functions that can call each other easily." ([python-dev, 2009-03-26](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-dev/2009-March/087658.html))
- The trampoline it replaced, PEP 342 (created 10-May-2005, Python 2.5), Examples item 3: a scheduler that "lets coroutines call other coroutines by yielding the coroutine they wish to invoke. Any non-generator value yielded by a coroutine is returned to the coroutine that called the one yielding the value." And "if a coroutine raises an exception, the exception is propagated to its caller." ([PEP 342](https://peps.python.org/pep-0342/))
- Draft history on python-ideas: "Proto-PEP on a 'yield from' statement" (12 Feb 2009), "Revised revised PEP" and "Revised revised revised PEP" (13 Feb), "Revised^4" and "Revised**5" (to 19 Feb), "Revised**6" (21 Feb), through "Revised**10" and python-dev "Revised**8" (March 2009), then "Yield-From: Finalization guarantees" (April 2009). ([thread index via python-ideas archive, e.g. Revised**6](https://www.python.org/pipermail/python-ideas/2009-February/003142.html); [python-dev Revised**8](https://www.python.org/pipermail/python-dev/2009-March/087678.html); [Finalization guarantees](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-ideas/2009-April/003836.html))
- Participants in the first thread: Antoine Pitrou, Bruce Frederiksen, Bruce Leban, George Sakkis, Greg Ewing, Raymond Hettinger. ([thread page](https://mail.python.org/archives/list/python-ideas@python.org/thread/QYKLE5SBGTV2ZEA3LD3GL7GOXMUL57DL))

### Inferences
- The flattening half of `yield from` was not new in 2009 (Cω 2005, `yield foreach` 2005, F# 2007, Icon). The bidirectional half was: none of the earlier constructs had a `send()` channel to forward, because C#/F#/Cω iterators are one-way. The return-value half has a partial earlier analogue only in PEP 342's own trampoline example, where a yielded non-generator value "is returned to the coroutine that called".
- So the fair statement for the book: PEP 380 is the origin of delegation as a coroutine-call mechanism (forwarding plus return value), and the origin of the `yield from` spelling; it is not the origin of "yield everything from this other generator in one expression".
- Guido's first reaction to the return value was hostile, and that feature is the one asyncio later depended on.

### Gaps
- I read the opening message and three replies, not all of the February-April 2009 traffic. I found no citation of C#, F#, Icon, or JavaScript in what I read, and PEP 380 has none, but a full read of the mbox is needed before writing "nobody cited another language".
- The pre-2009 Python `yield *` proposals the PEP alludes to were not located. A search of 2005-2008 python-dev/python-ideas returned nothing dated before 2009.
- Greg Ewing's own site (his yield-from page and the "cofunctions" follow-up, PEP 3152) was not fetched.
- Python 3.3's release date (September 2012) is from memory, not fetched.

## JavaScript: `yield*` in ES6

### Takeaway
ES6's `yield*` descends from PEP 380 by the designers' own statement: Brendan Eich wrote on es-discuss on 10 May 2012 "PEP-380 is the basis". The star is explained in the same message as meaning "exhaust the operand". The return-value semantics were kept in 2013 with PEP 380 cited again.

### Cited Findings
- Brendan Eich, es-discuss, 2012-05-10, thread "Recursion in generators?": "PEP-380 is the basis:" followed by a quotation of PEP 380's description of the delegating generator. In the same message: "The * means to exhaust the yield operand's value, otherwise you're just yielding that value." and "There's no point requiring an iterator not an iterable given the special form." ([esdiscuss.org, "Recursion in generators?"](https://esdiscuss.org/topic/recursion-in-generators))
- Erik Arvidsson, same thread, same day: "That is what the star is for." and "yield* requires an iterable operand, just like for-of. If you pass something non iterable it throws." ([same thread](https://esdiscuss.org/topic/recursion-in-generators))
- Brendan Eich, September 2013, replying to a proposal that `return value;` in a generator be a SyntaxError: "We deliberated long and hard, after going through a design state without return expr;, on this. We agree with" (the link target is PEP 380). And: "We want return expr; -- not for the 'for-of' special forms, which clients use knowingly to consume in-band values" (the parenthetical link is to task.js). Brandon Benvie in that thread: "An example use case is delegating yield", with a `yield*` example, and "Can't do this without a way to handle a return value from generators." ([esdiscuss.org, "Proposal: Generator returning a value should throw SyntaxError"](https://esdiscuss.org/topic/proposal-generator-returning-a-value-should-throw-syntaxerror))
- Brendan Eich, January 2014, same thread: "PEP-380 is worth a read, IMHO, for anyone who values Python's experience." David Herman, 2014-01-27: "Finally, task.js is just an example of building a control abstraction out of iterators." ([same thread](https://esdiscuss.org/topic/proposal-generator-returning-a-value-should-throw-syntaxerror))
- A Chromium issue on V8's implementation refers to "The original delegating yield proposal" on the harmony:generators wiki page, noting that its error-recovery behavior was not specified at the time; V8's `yield*` landed in 2013. ([Chromium code review 16695006](https://codereview.chromium.org/16695006/); [Andy Wingo, "generators in v8", 2013-05-08](https://wingolog.org/archives/2013/05/08/generators-in-v8))

### Inferences
- JavaScript took both halves of PEP 380: forwarding and the return value as the value of the `yield*` expression. The 2013 quote shows the committee first designed generators without `return expr` and then restored it, citing PEP 380.
- The `yield*` spelling matches the `function*` marker ES6 uses for generator functions. It also happens to be the spelling PEP 380 says earlier Python proposals used and rejected. No source found says TC39 took the star from those Python proposals, so that is coincidence or unrecorded.
- Mozilla's JS 1.7 generators (2006) were modeled on Python 2.5, which had no `yield from`, so they had no delegation. Inferred from timing; not verified against the JS 1.7 documentation this session.

### Gaps
- The archived wiki.ecmascript.org `harmony:generators` page could not be fetched (web.archive.org is blocked for this tool, and a Stack Overflow mirror returned 401). So I cannot quote the proposal page's own "Delegating yield" section, its date, or whether the page text itself cites PEP 380. The Eich quotes are the best primary statement found.
- Dave Herman's authorship and the 2010-2011 date of the proposal are from the brief, not verified here. I found no Herman sentence naming PEP 380, only Eich's.
- TC39 meeting notes (github.com/tc39/notes) were not searched.

## PHP 7.0 `yield from`

### Takeaway
The Generator Delegation RFC names Python 3.3 and JavaScript ES6 as its models and argues for copying their vocabulary. The RFC's author is Daniel Lowrey (with Bob Weinand), not Andrea Faulds as the brief assumed. It first proposed `yield *` and moved to `yield from`.

### Cited Findings
- Metadata: author "Daniel Lowrey", contributor "Bob Weinand", date "2015-03-01", version "0.2.0"; vote 25 yes, 0 no. ([PHP RFC: Generator Delegation](https://wiki.php.net/rfc/generator-delegation))
- Precedent, verbatim: "Python 3.3 generators support the `yield from` syntax:" and "Javascript ES6 generators support the `yield*` syntax:". Also: "Other popular dynamic languages currently support variants of the proposed syntax", "Other popular dynamic languages inhabiting a similar space to PHP implement generator delegation value resolution", and "Sharing common vernacular for similar features lowers cognitive barriers". The references list "New in Python 3.3: yield from" and "JavaScript yield* Syntax". ([same RFC](https://wiki.php.net/rfc/generator-delegation))
- Syntax history: "The original version of this RFC proposed a `yield *` syntax." "The `yield *` syntax was rejected in favor of `yield from` on the basis that `*` would break backwards compatibility." "Additionally, the `yield *` form was considered less readable than the current proposal." Changelog: "v0.2.0 Moved to `yield from` instead of `yield *`". ([same RFC](https://wiki.php.net/rfc/generator-delegation))
- Motivation: "A major impetus for generator delegation is refactoring and readability." and "Generator delegation -- at its heart -- is nothing more than the application of standard factoring practices". ([same RFC](https://wiki.php.net/rfc/generator-delegation))

### Inferences
- PHP is the clearest "X took it from Y" case in the survey: both precedents are named, and the keyword was chosen to match Python after the JavaScript spelling collided with multiplication (`yield * $x` already parses in PHP).
- PHP took the full PEP 380 package: the RFC relies on generator return expressions (a companion RFC for PHP 7) so that `yield from` evaluates to the inner generator's return value.

### Gaps
- PHP 7.0's release date (December 2015) and the companion "Generator Return Expressions" RFC were not fetched.
- Andrea Faulds's role, if any, was not found on the RFC page.

## Later adopters: Dart, Kotlin, C++23, Rust, Ruby, and others

### Takeaway
C++23 and Rust are well documented: C++ cites its own library lineage (cppcoro's `recursive_generator`) and no other language, and Rust's RFC 3513 names Python's `yield from` and defers the feature. For Dart, Kotlin, and Ruby I found the construct but no document naming a precedent, so those links are inferred from timing and spelling. Hack, Scala, Swift, Go, Raku, and Julia were not researched to a citable standard.

### Cited Findings
- C++23: P2502R2, "std::generator: Synchronous Coroutine Generator for Ranges", Casey Carter, dated 2022-06-03. Acknowledgement: "I'd like to thank Lewis Baker and Corentin Jabot, whose P2168 [3] I looted shamelessly for this proposal. This paper is presented as a new revision of their unpublished D2168R4 for continuity of design." ([P2502R2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2022/p2502r2.pdf))
- C++23 definition: "A 'recursive generator' is a coroutine that supports the ability to directly co_yield a generator of the same type as a way of emitting the elements of that generator as elements of the current generator." Example: `if (tree.left) co_yield ranges::elements_of(visit(*tree.left));`. ([P2502R2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2022/p2502r2.pdf))
- Why `elements_of` and not a bare `co_yield g`: "ranges::elements_of is a utility function that prevents ambiguity when a nested generator type is convertible to the value type of the present generator", with the example `generator<any> g() { co_yield f(); // should we yield 42 or generator<int> ? }`. The revision history places it at P2168R1: "Introduce elements_of to avoid ambiguities when a generator is convertible to the reference type". It also accepts any range: "we further propose that co_yield elements_of(x) be extended to support yielding the values of arbitrary ranges beyond generators". ([P2502R2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2022/p2502r2.pdf); predecessor [P2168R3, Jabot and Baker, April 2021](https://wg21.link/p2168r3))
- C++23 precedent: "The recursive form can be implemented efficiently with symmetric transfer. Earlier works in [CppCoro] implemented this feature in a distinct recursive_generator type." Also: "Folly uses a single generator type, which can be recursive but doesn't implement symmetric transfer." and "ranges-v3 also implements a generator type, which is never recursive". The paper's text contains no mention of Python, C#, or JavaScript (checked by searching the extracted text). Motivation: "Writing an efficient recursive generator is non-trivial; the standard should provide one." ([P2502R2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2022/p2502r2.pdf))
- C++23 scope: delegation forwards values and exceptions only ("Exceptions that propagate out of the body of nested generator coroutines are rethrown into" the parent); the `co_yield elements_of(...)` expression has type void in the wording ("yield-expressions that call one of the promise::yield_value overloads have type void"), so there is no return value from the inner generator. ([P2502R2](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg21/docs/papers/2022/p2502r2.pdf))
- Rust: RFC 3513 (`gen` blocks), start date 2023-10-10, has no delegation. Under future possibilities: "Python has the ability to `yield from` an iterator. Effectively this is syntactic sugar for looping over all elements of the iterator and yielding each individually. There is a wide design space here, but some options are included in the following subsections." The options listed: "Instead of adding special support for this, we could expect that users would write" a `for` loop, "We could do something like postfix `yield`", "Alternatively, we could use an entirely new keyword", and "We could add a macro to the standard library and prelude. The macro would expand to a `for` loop + `yield`." The RFC does not raise the quadratic cost. ([RFC 3513](https://rust-lang.github.io/rfcs/3513-gen-blocks.html))
- Kotlin: the coroutines design document (author Andrey Breslav, Roman Elizarov among contributors, implemented in Kotlin 1.1.0) places lazily computed sequences beside generators in C# and Python and mentions `yieldAll(sequence)`; a search of it found no reference to `yield from` or `yield*`. (third-party copy of the design doc: [kotlin-coroutines-informal.md](https://github.com/rvnareshkumar/kotlin-coroutines/blob/master/kotlin-coroutines-informal.md)). `yieldAll` is a suspending library function on `SequenceScope`, with overloads for an `Iterable`, an `Iterator`, and a `Sequence`; the current `sequence { }` / `SequenceScope` API is marked since Kotlin 1.3. ([Kotlin stdlib: yieldAll](https://kotlinlang.org/api/core/kotlin-stdlib/kotlin.sequences/-sequence-scope/yield-all.html))
- Dart: Dart 1.9 (March 2015) introduced `async`/`await` and "generator methods, sync* and async*". ([InfoQ, March 2015, quoting the Dart team's announcement](https://www.infoq.com/news/2015/03/dart-asynchronous-programming/)). The conformance suite files the feature under "Yield_and_Yield_Each/Yield_Each", so the spec's name for `yield*` is "yield-each". ([co19 test](https://dart.googlesource.com/co19/+/a0341f06605da4a989e539a1da8cd119d98f1a92/Language/Statements/Yield_and_Yield_Each/Yield_Each/execution_sync_t02.dart))
- Ruby: there is no delegation syntax. Feature #15618 (2019) added `Enumerator::Yielder#to_proc` so a yielder can be passed as a block, `each_line(&y)`, inside `Enumerator.new`; Matz said it "sounds reasonable" and it was accepted. No `yield_from` request was found. ([Ruby Feature #15618](https://bugs.ruby-lang.org/issues/15618))

### Inferences
- Dart's `yield*` is spelled like JavaScript's, and Dart's `sync*`/`async*` markers mirror `function*`; Dart was designed at Google as a JavaScript successor. Link to JavaScript: inferred from spelling and timing, no source found.
- Kotlin's `yieldAll` is a library function made possible by general suspending functions, so it needed no language feature. Link to Python/C#: only the general `yield` citation, nothing for delegation.
- C++'s design converged on the 2005 Jacobs et al. implementation (a stack of frames with the consumer resuming the leaf directly) without citing it. The lineage it does state is internal: cppcoro `recursive_generator` (Lewis Baker), then P2168 (Jabot and Baker, 2021), then P2502 (Carter, 2022).
- By construct type, three families exist: (a) one-way flattening with an efficiency guarantee (Cω, `yield foreach`, C++23 `elements_of`); (b) one-way flattening as sugar (F# `yield!`, Dart `yield*`, Kotlin `yieldAll`, Ruby `&y`); (c) two-way coroutine delegation with a return value (Python, JavaScript, PHP).

### Gaps
- Dart: I did not find the spec text or any design document (for example Gilad Bracha's async proposal) naming a precedent for `yield*`.
- Kotlin: the official KEEP document was not fetched, only a third-party copy summarized by search. `buildSequence` in Kotlin 1.1 (2017, experimental) is from the brief and the design-doc metadata, not from a release note.
- P2168's own revisions were not read; whether R0 allowed bare `co_yield generator` is inferred from the R1 changelog line.
- Hack, Scala, Swift, Go range-over-func, Raku, and Julia: no reliable source consulted this session. From general knowledge only, and unverified: Go 1.23 range-over-func has no delegation construct (a nested `for ... range` calling `yield` is the idiom); Swift has no generators; Raku's `gather`/`take` is dynamically scoped, so a called routine can `take` directly; Julia uses channels or iterator combinators. None of this should be cited without a check.
- Rust: whether the `gen` blocks tracking issue has discussed `yield from` since 2023 was not checked.

## C#: `yield foreach` status and the design team's reasons

### Takeaway
C# still has no delegation construct. The idea dates from the C# 3.0 design period (Erik Meijer advocating it), was publicly described by Wes Dyer in 2007 as "not in Orcas", and was explained by Eric Lippert in 2009 as never making the priority cut. The current position, stated by Cyrus Najmabadi in csharplang discussion 5303 through May 2025, is that sugar without the linear-time implementation would mislead users, and the efficient implementation must not cost iterators that do not use it.

### Cited Findings
- Eric Lippert, reply in the comments of his 2009-07-13 post, to the question "why no 'yield foreach'?": "The downside is that we'd have to cut something better. We have a list of a couple hundred possible new language features for any given version of C#. We have the design, implementation, testing, documentation and management resources available to actually implement maybe four or five of them in any one version. 'yield foreach' is an awesome feature that I would love to do; it is certainly in the top fifty, but has never made it into the top ten. If it ever makes it into the top five, we'll probably implement it. -- Eric" ([Lippert, "Iterator Blocks, Part Two"](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/ericlippert/iterator-blocks-part-two-why-no-ref-or-out-parameters))
- Matt Warren (C# compiler team), gist "Exploring Non-Quadratic Iterators" (written after a 2021 holiday-break investigation): "As far back as during the design for C# 3.0 the design team contemplated a possible language feature to solve the problem of quadratic iterators (the yield foreach syntax)" ... "that was never implemented for a variety of reasons and put aside". "Eric Meijer did a lot of thinking about this problem back during the development of C# 3.0" and "at the time he was advocating for the team to solve this problem with a new syntax". "He eventually cowrote a paper on iterators where he describes and solves the recursive iterator problem". "this non-trivial change to the code generator was the major stumbling block for moving forward with this feature." "it has never gathered enough traction to move forward within the design team". ([mattwar gist](https://gist.github.com/mattwar/8660692f56c2a15478fbbfd557934a06))
- csharplang discussion 5303, "Feature Request: Syntactic Sugar to yield", opened 2021-10-19, category Language Ideas, unanswered. The proposer notes the keyword "could be yield from, yield all, yield many, yield*, yield!". Cyrus Najmabadi (C# design team), 2021-10-19: "the above concerns would need to be addressed in order for any movement to be made on this." and "Right now there is already a viable solution in the language to syntactically yield an entire enumerable." 2022-06-05: the sugar "is something we feel is actively harmful in that we expect that people will see this and now think they can write these iterators"; the efficient form "should be 'pay to play'". 2025-05-19: "We're not going to add a language feature just to have it immediately warn you the moment you use it." "There's already a one-liner that performs badly for recursive enumeration." "Write a foreach loop if you really want to yield and you're ok with bad performance." "Manually write the iteration if you want the good performance." Commenters cite Python, JavaScript, F#, and TypeScript's `yield*`. ([dotnet/csharplang discussion 5303](https://github.com/dotnet/csharplang/discussions/5303))
- An older request, "Recursive iterators (non-quadratic)", is Roslyn issue 378, referenced from discussion 5303. ([mirror of dotnet/roslyn issue 378](https://lightrun.com/answers/dotnet-roslyn-feature-request-recursive-iterators-non-quadratic))

### Inferences
- The irony for the timeline: the C# research group published the first linear-time delegation design in 2005, four years before PEP 380, and C# is the one major generator language that still lacks the construct twenty years later.
- The reasons given changed over time: resources and priority (Lippert 2009), a non-trivial code-generator change (Warren), and now a principle that syntax must not hide a quadratic cost (Najmabadi 2021-2025).

### Gaps
- Status "as of 2026" rests on the last design-team comment I saw (May 2025) and on the discussion still being unanswered when fetched. I did not find a championed csharplang issue, an LDM note, or any 2026 statement.
- A `yield return!` spelling was not found in the sources read.
- The mattwar gist carries no publication date; the "2021" comes from its text.

## Delegation as the basis of coroutines, and whether `await` descends from it

### Takeaway
In Python the descent is explicit and documented: asyncio coroutines were `yield from` generators (PEP 3156, 2012), and PEP 492 (2015) says `await` "uses the `yield from` implementation". In JavaScript the route is parallel but looser: generators plus a driver (task.js, co) came first, the async functions proposal was specified as sugar over generators and a `spawn` function, and the TC39 design discussion cites task.js as the reason generators keep `return expr`. C# `await` (2012) owes nothing to delegation.

### Cited Findings
- PEP 3156 (created 12-Dec-2012): "Coroutines use the `yield from` syntax introduced in PEP 380, instead of the original `yield` syntax." The abstract describes "a higher-level scheduler based on `yield from` (PEP 380)". "The scheduler has no public interface. You interact with it by using `yield from future` and `yield from task`." asyncio was checked into Python 3.4 on October 20th, 2013. ([PEP 3156](https://peps.python.org/pep-3156/))
- PEP 492 (created 09-Apr-2015, accepted 5 May 2015) on `await`: "It uses the `yield from` implementation with an extra step of validating its argument." And "`await`, similarly to `yield from`, suspends execution of `read_data` coroutine until" the awaitable completes. ([PEP 492](https://peps.python.org/pep-0492/))
- PEP 492 lists the other languages with the feature: C# ("C# has it since long time ago"), a "proposal to add async/await in ECMAScript 7", "Facebook's Hack/HHVM", "Google's Dart language", and Scala. ([PEP 492](https://peps.python.org/pep-0492/))
- Guido van Rossum's 2009 statement of the motive already framed delegation as the coroutine call: trampoline code "is still pretty cumbersome to use when you have to write a lot of tasks and subtasks". ([python-dev, 2009-03-26](https://mail.python.org/pipermail/python-dev/2009-March/087658.html))
- JavaScript: the early async functions drafts described the feature as thin sugar over "generators and a spawn function which converts generators into promise objects"; an `await*` form was in the draft and later removed ("Removed await *"). ([tc39/proposal-async-await commit](https://github.com/tc39/proposal-async-await/commit/2c499feb41df4c79a884d1520518cd00ab623e5b); [Stage 3 draft](https://tc39.es/proposal-async-await/))
- TC39 on why generators keep a return value: Eich, 2013, "We want return expr;" with a link to task.js; Adam Ahmed, 2014-01-28: "usages in the vein of task.js will use the return value as a real return value". ([esdiscuss.org thread](https://esdiscuss.org/topic/proposal-generator-returning-a-value-should-throw-syntaxerror))
- Dart shipped `async`/`await` and `sync*`/`async*` generators together in 1.9, March 2015. ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2015/03/dart-asynchronous-programming/))
- F#'s computation expressions pair `yield!` with `return!` ("return! e (requires ReturnFrom method)"), and Syme writes that "F# computation expression notation began with 'async'". ([HOPL 2020](https://fsharp.org/history/hopl-final/hopl-fsharp.pdf))

### Inferences
- Python: `await` is `yield from` restricted to awaitables, by the PEP's own words. The chain is PEP 342 trampoline (2005), PEP 380 (2009-2012), PEP 3156 asyncio (2012-2013), PEP 492 (2015).
- JavaScript: `await` descends from the generator-plus-driver pattern, where the driver handles each yielded promise. In that pattern a plain `yield` plays the role of `await`, and `yield*` is used only to call one generator-coroutine from another without going through the driver. So JS `await` descends from `yield`, with delegation as a supporting piece, not from `yield*` itself. `await*` was unrelated to delegation (it was a `Promise.all` shorthand).
- F# had the pairing first: `return!`/`let!` in async workflows (2007) is the same "delegate to an inner computation and take its result" idea in monadic form, and C#'s `await` (2012) is usually traced to F# async, not to iterator delegation. That last attribution is general knowledge and was not sourced this session.

### Gaps
- co and task.js documentation was not fetched, so the statement about how they use `yield*` is from general knowledge.
- No source was found for a C# design statement relating `await` to iterators beyond the shared state-machine rewrite.
- PEP 492's rationale section on why `yield from` coroutines were confusing (the motive for a separate keyword) was not quoted.
