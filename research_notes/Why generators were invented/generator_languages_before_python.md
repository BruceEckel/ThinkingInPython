# Generators and iterators as language features before Python (1958 to 2000)

Scope: the line of language features named "generator" or "iterator" that PEP 255 drew on (Icon, CLU, Sather), with their ancestors (IPL-V, Conniver, Alphard) and the functional answer (lazy streams).
Coroutine origins (Conway, Knuth, Simula, Jackson) are covered by another researcher and appear here only where a source ties them to generators.

Method note: every quotation below was read in the source's own text (PDF text extracted locally, or the page fetched), unless a line says otherwise.
Scanned PDFs (the CLU Reference Manual, the Conniver manual) carry OCR errors; where I corrected an OCR error inside a quotation, the line says so.
Sources I could not open are listed under Gaps, and a claim resting only on a secondary source says which one.

## 1. Where the term "generator" comes from: IPL-V, Conniver, POP-2, SAIL, Landin

### Takeaway
The programming sense of "generator" starts in IPL-V (Newell, Shaw, Simon; described by Newell and Tonge in CACM, April 1960), where a generator is a routine that produces a sequence of elements and applies a caller-supplied "subprocess" to each one.
Its stated purpose was to separate "production of elements" from "processing" in a loop, and its designers already saw the central difficulty: the generator and its consumer each need their own context alive at once, which "violates" the strict call hierarchy.
Conniver (1972) then made a generator that returns some values, stays alive, and is resumed later (AU-REVOIR), with a message channel from consumer back to generator.

### Cited Findings
- IPL-V definition and purpose, verbatim: "Repetitive operations can be handled in IPL-V with loops, utilizing the conditional branch, or by a special class of processes, called generators. A generator is a process that produces a sequence of outputs and applies to each output a specified process. The process that the generator applies is an input to the generator and is called the subprocess. The generator is associated with the kind of sequence it produces, and will apply any subprocess to the elements of the sequence. (The subprocess must obey a system convention on how to signal the generator to continue or stop producing elements.) Thus, the generator, just like the "iteration" statements of algebraic compilers, accomplishes a separation of the "production of elements" part of a loop from the "processing" part." (A. Newell and F. M. Tonge, "An Introduction to Information Processing Language V", Communications of the ACM 3(4), April 1960; [CMU Newell archive reprint](https://iiif.library.cmu.edu/file/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001.pdf))
- IPL-V on why a generator is special, verbatim: "The subprocess is executed for each element of the output sequence as though it were a continuation of the process firing the generator (the superprocess) that is, as though the generator had made no use of the Communication Cell or Public Working Storage. Generators are different from all other IPL processes in that two contexts of information in working storage must coexist in the computer that of the generator and that of the superprocess and subprocess. There is an alternation of both control and context between the generator and the subprocess. To produce an element of the sequence, the generator must be in control and its context should occupy the W's; to process the element, the subprocess must be in control and its context (the context of the super-routine) should occupy the W's. Hence the strict hierarchy of routines and subroutines is violated, and special pains have to be taken to see that information remains safe and that each process works in its appropriate context." ([Newell and Tonge 1960](https://iiif.library.cmu.edu/file/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001.pdf))
- IPL-V's fix for the two-context problem, verbatim: "To handle this special housekeeping, the GENERATOR HOUSEKEEPING processes are provided. These processes insure that the generator's context is hidden away before the subprocess is executed, and returned to the W's after the subprocess is completed. The programmer uses these processes in coding generators. Some generally useful generators "generate the symbols on a list," "generate the cells of a data list structure" and "generate the cells of a tree structure" are included among the basic list processes." ([Newell and Tonge 1960](https://iiif.library.cmu.edu/file/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001.pdf))
- The paper's worked example: the subprocess signals through cell H5 ("Set H5+ if (0) occurs; set H5 [-] is it does not"), and "If the two symbols were identical, the subprocess must stop the generator", so the consumer can stop the producer early ([Newell and Tonge 1960](https://iiif.library.cmu.edu/file/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001.pdf); OCR of the minus sign is lost in the scan).
- A 2026 reimplementation of IPL-V describes its ~150 J-functions as "implementing operations from basic list manipulation to "generator" (iterator) protocols", naming J17 to J19 and J100 as the generator functions (J. Shrager, "Executable Archaeology: Reanimating the Logic Theorist from its IPL-V Source", [arXiv 2603.13514](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.13514)). Secondary.
- The Icon designers trace the term to IPL-V and separate it from unrelated uses, verbatim: "The concept of generators has appeared in various forms in many languages. An early language that has generators is IPL-V [31], in which a generator is a subroutine that calls a processing routine to operate on each object in a data structure. Other languages, such as ALGOL 68 [38] and COBOL, use the term generator to describe various language features, but this use is unrelated to the Icon notion." (Griswold, Hanson, Korb, "Generators in Icon", ACM TOPLAS 3(2), April 1981, section 5; [PDF on D. R. Hanson's site](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- Same paper on the coroutine sense of the word, verbatim: "Languages that support coroutines frequently use the term generator to describe a particular coroutine usage. SIMULA [1] is the oldest of such languages; more recent examples include extensions to POP-2 [24] and SL5 [20] and coroutine additions to PASCAL [27]. There also has been a substantial amount of work on the "lazy evaluation" approach to the incremental generation of sequences, usually in LISP or APL frameworks [7, 17, 22]." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- Conniver's generator, verbatim: "The system provides the function CDEFGEN for defining generator-functions, routines which add possibilities to a list being considered by other routines. A generator function, or generator, is like an ordinary program, except that it is expected to return a list of the possibilities it has discovered, which TRY-NEXT uses as described; in this way the generator communicates the values it wishes to return. In the simplest case, the possibilities are actual values; TRY-NEXT pops off and returns the next one, removing it from the possibilities list." (D. V. McDermott and G. J. Sussman, The Conniver Reference Manual, MIT AI Memo 259, 1972; [SAIL file CNVR.RPO dated 1972-10-30](https://www.saildart.org/CNVR.RPO[UP,DOC]); the scan's underlining is dropped)
- Conniver's motivation for a generator that stays alive, verbatim (page 13): "Such a way of generating objects is not always the best. It may be, for example, much more expensive to generate each one than to try it out. Alternatively, the generation of successive possibilities may depend on what the calling function did with the previous ones. Or it may simply be that the generator has no idea how many possibilities its superior wants. (Note that FORCEWIN is interested only in the first.) What is needed is a way of returning some of the possibilities while maintaining the generator in existence for further duty if required." ([Conniver Reference Manual](https://www.saildart.org/CNVR.RPO[UP,DOC]); "some" is underlined in the original)
- Conniver's AU-REVOIR mechanism, verbatim: "The way this is done is with the primitive AU-REVOIR, which behaves something like ADIEU, but pushes one further datum onto PROPOSALS before turning it into a possibilities list. The new datum is a tag to the point just inside the AU-REVOIR in the generator. The POSSIBILITIES returned, therefore, includes a pointer to the generator's activation. When TRY-NEXT encounters such a thing in a possibilities list, it GOes to the tag, reawakening, in effect, the generator's process, including its bindings and context. The generator can then note new values and repeat the AU-REVOIR, or do an ADIEU, either of which this time returns its PROPOSALS to TRY-NEXT after converting it to a possibilities list" ([Conniver Reference Manual](https://www.saildart.org/CNVR.RPO[UP,DOC]))
- Conniver's two-way channel, verbatim: "Communication the other way, from the user of the generated possibilities, is made possible by an optional message argument to TRY-NEXT that it sends to the generator, which is returned, in the generator's activation, as the value of AU-REVOIR." ([Conniver Reference Manual](https://www.saildart.org/CNVR.RPO[UP,DOC])) NOTE pushes a value onto PROPOSALS; ADIEU ends the generator and returns PROPOSALS ("Since ADIEU reverses PROPOSALS before returning it, the proposals will be in the order NOTEd.").
- Sather's designers read Conniver the same way, verbatim: "Special tokens in possibility lists cause an associated generator to be invoked. This is a means for lazily computing lists of values. A generator yields new values and has the option of maintaining its state ("AU_REVOIR") or of quitting ("ADIEU"), similar to the yield and quit statements in Sather." (Murer, Omohundro, Stoutamire, Szyperski, "Iteration Abstraction in Sather", NECI TR 95-153, 1995, section 4.1; [PDF](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf))
- Conniver as a reaction to PLANNER: the DSpace abstract of Sussman and McDermott, "Why Conniving is Better than Planning" (MIT AI Memo 255, February 1972; 255a, April 1972), says PLANNER's automatic backtracking encourages inefficient algorithms, conceals what is happening from the user, and misleads him with primitives having powerful names, and presents CONNIVER as avoiding these problems ([DSpace record](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/6200)). I saw this abstract only through a search summary; the memo PDF returned HTTP 429.
- Icon's verdict on Conniver and SAIL, verbatim: goal-directed facilities "are either cumbersome to use, requiring considerable programming overhead to set up and control the search strategy (as in CONNIVER [30, 37]), or too inaccessible, not allowing the programmer the ability to terminate, prevent, or force the searching for alternatives (see, e.g., PLANNER [23])." And: "SAIL [33] is an ALGOL-based artificial intelligence language that supports backtracking. The backtracking facilities include primitives to REMEMBER, FORGET, and RESTORE variables in different contexts. Like CONNIVER, SAIL requires explicit manipulation of variables by the programmer to effect a backtracking control regime." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- Hewitt's verdict on Conniver, quoted by the Sather paper: "that we can do without the paraphernalia of 'hairy control structures' (such as possibility lists, non-local gotos, and assignments of values to the internal variables of other procedures in Conniver" (C. Hewitt, "Viewing control structures as patterns of passing messages", Artificial Intelligence 8, 1977, p. 341, as quoted in [Murer et al. 1995](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)). The Sather authors add: "As an alternative, Hewitt proposes lazy evaluation (using an explicit delay pseudo-function)."
- Landin: "The use of this mechanism [delayed evaluation] to implement streams was first described by Landin (1965)." (Abelson and Sussman, Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs, 2nd ed., section 3.5, history footnote; [online text](https://sarabander.github.io/sicp/html/3_002e5.xhtml)). Secondary; see section 6.

### Inferences
- The IPL-V passage already contains the whole problem the author asks about. A loop has two parts, producing elements and processing them; IPL-V wants them written separately; and once they are separate, each part has its own working context that must survive while the other runs. A subroutine hierarchy keeps only one such context on top at a time, so IPL-V had to save and restore the generator's context by hand ("GENERATOR HOUSEKEEPING").
- IPL-V's generator is what is now called internal iteration (the producer calls the consumer). Conniver's AU-REVOIR inverts this into external iteration, where the consumer pulls with TRY-NEXT and the producer's activation is kept alive between pulls. Conniver's motivations (generation is expensive, the consumer may want only the first value, the next value may depend on what the consumer did) are the motivations PEP 255 gives for tokenize.
- Conniver's "optional message argument to TRY-NEXT ... returned ... as the value of AU-REVOIR" is a 1972 precedent for Python's `send()` (PEP 342). I found no source that draws this connection; it is my reading.

### Gaps
- I did not read the IPL-V Manual itself (Newell et al., RAND 1961; 2nd ed. Prentice-Hall 1964). The DTIC copy of a related RAND memo (AD0611841) returned HTTP 403. The Newell and Tonge CACM article is primary and sufficient for the definition.
- The date when IPL first had generators (IPL-II/III in 1956 to 1958 versus IPL-V in 1958 to 1960) is unverified.
- POP-2 "dynamic lists" (a list whose elements are produced on demand by a function) and the POP-2 coroutine extension cited by Icon as [24]: I found no primary text; unverified.
- SAIL generators: the Icon paper discusses only SAIL's backtracking; I found nothing on a SAIL "generator" feature.
- "Why Conniving is Better than Planning" full text not read (HTTP 429 from DSpace).

## 2. Alphard generators (Shaw, Wulf, London 1976/1977)

### Takeaway
Alphard's generator answers the data-abstraction question "how does a client loop over a set whose representation is hidden?" A generator is a special form with operations `&init` and `&next` that the `for` statement calls, so the loop states what to do with each element and the form decides how elements are reached.
I could not open the Alphard paper itself; the account below comes from Liskov's history of CLU and a reading-list summary.

### Cited Findings
- Liskov on the problem Alphard's generators and CLU's iterators share, verbatim: "The problem solved by both generators and iterators is the following. Many data abstractions are collections of elements, and the reason for collecting the elements is so that later you can do something to them. Examples of such collections are arrays, sets, and lists. The problem is that for some types there is no obvious way to get to the elements. For arrays, you can use indexes; for lists, you can follow links. But for sets it's not clear what to do. What you would like is an operation of the type to provide the elements. Such an operation could be a procedure that returns an array containing the elements, but that is expensive if the collection is large. Instead, it would be nice to get at the elements one at a time. A generator does this by providing a group of operations, containing at least an operation to get the generation started, an operation to get the next element, and an operation to determine whether there are any more elements. Alphard generators had several more operations, and the Alphard designers worked out a way to use the for statement to call these operations at appropriate points." (B. Liskov, "A History of CLU", MIT/LCS/TR-561, April 1992, section 3.10; [PDF](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf); also [Tufts mirror](https://www.cs.tufts.edu/~nr/cs257/archive/barbara-liskov/clu-history.pdf))
- The Queen's University reading-list summary of the CACM paper (secondary) says the loop control variable can "operate on abstract entities without explicit dependence on the representation of those entities" (quoted from the paper), gives the motivating example `sum <- 0; for x : S do sum <- sum + x` against an array loop and a linked-list loop "which imply an array or a list representation respectively", and states: "A generator is a special type of form with two specific operations '&init' and '&next' which are used to control iteration." It describes `for x : gen(y) while B(x) do ...` as calling `&init`, which sets x and a hidden boolean b, then calling `&next` each iteration until b is false, and a `first` statement for loops with early exits such as searching. It notes "it is not possible to modify the structure on which the generator is operating within the loop that is controlled by the generator." ([Cordy reading summary of Shaw, Wulf, London, CACM 20(8), August 1977](https://research.cs.queensu.ca/home/cordy/cisc860/Biblio/drb/CS/shaw77.pdf))
- The 1976 CMU technical report "Abstraction and Verification in Alphard: Iteration and Generators" is DTIC accession ADA030286 ([DTIC record](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/html/tr/ADA030286/index.html)); a search summary says its contents cover defining generators, proof rules for loops, termination, and a finite-sets example. The PDF returned HTTP 403 to me.
- Icon's comparison, verbatim: "More recently, ALPHARD [43] and CLU [28] have been developed to support data structure abstraction. In these languages generators are used to iterate over the elements of programmer-defined data structures [35]. ... In each of these languages, generators are only accessible in a specific context: a particular type of for statement. This is significantly more restricted than in Icon, where generators may appear anywhere." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))

### Inferences
- Alphard's generator is a cursor protocol (init, next, done) driven by the compiler-generated loop. The producer's position between calls lives in the form's fields, which is the "encode the control structure as state" burden that Sather later names (section 5). CLU's single-operation iterator removes that burden.

### Gaps
- Shaw, Wulf, London (CACM 20(8):553-564, 1977) not read in the original: ACM DL returned a Cloudflare challenge and DTIC returned 403. No verbatim Alphard motivation is available here beyond the phrase the summary quotes. The exact names of Alphard's generator operations (`&init`, `&next`, and possibly `&finished` or others) rest on the summary; Liskov says only that they "had several more operations".

## 3. CLU iterators (Liskov et al., 1975 to 1979)

### Takeaway
CLU's iterator was designed as a "control abstraction" to go with data abstraction: the `for` loop states the action, the iterator (a single procedure-like operation using `yield`) states how a collection's elements are obtained, without exposing the representation and without building the whole collection first.
Liskov says iterators "are related to coroutines" but were deliberately limited so CLU could run on a single stack, and she names the cost: CLU cannot walk two collections side by side.

### Cited Findings
- Origin, verbatim: "Iterators were inspired by a construct in Alphard called a "generator" [Shaw, 1976; Shaw, 1977]. We first learned about this in the summer of 1975 when we visited the Alphard group at CMU. We were intrigued by generators since they solved some problems with data abstractions, but we thought they were too complicated. Russ Atkinson designed iterators on the airplane going back to Boston after this meeting and described them in a design note in September 1975 [Atkinson, 1975]." ([Liskov 1992, section 3.10](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf))
- The problem statement is the long Liskov quotation in section 2 above ("Many data abstractions are collections of elements ... it would be nice to get at the elements one at a time.").
- What an iterator is, verbatim: "A CLU iterator is a single operation that yields its results incrementally. For example, `elements = iter (s: set[t]) yields (t)` produces all the elements in set s, but it yields them one at a time. An iterator is called in a for statement: `for x: int in set[int]$elements(coll) do ...` The for loop begins by calling the iterator. Each time the iterator yields a result, the loop body is executed; when the body finishes, control resumes in the iterator, and when the iterator returns, the loop terminates. Also, if the loop body causes the loop to terminate, the iterator terminates." ([Liskov 1992, section 3.10](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf))
- Relation to coroutines and the single-stack restriction, verbatim: "Iterators are related to coroutines; the iterator and the body of the for loop pass control back and forth. However, their use is limited so that CLU programs can make do with a single stack. They are inexpensive: a yield effectively calls the loop body, which returns to the iterator when it is finished. (Calls are very cheap in CLU.) Imposing the limitations on iterators was done to get the efficient, single stack implementation, albeit at the expense of some expressive power. For example, iterators cannot be used to compute whether two lists have the same elements, since to do this you need to iterate through the two lists side-by-side, and CLU only allows iterators to be nested." ([Liskov 1992, section 3.10](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf))
- The restriction as a design-goal trade-off, verbatim: "A second example is our iterator mechanism; we limited the expressive power of the mechanism so that we could implement it using a single stack." (Liskov 1992, section 3 on design goals, under "Performance"; [PDF](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf))
- Liskov says iterators were the exception to a tenet, verbatim: "One of the tenets of the CLU design was that we were not going to do research in control structures. However, we did such work in defining the exception mechanism, and also in designing certain other control structures to make up for the lack of gotos (e.g., the break statement, which terminates a loop). We also did it in defining iterators." ([Liskov 1992, section 3.10](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf))
- "Control abstraction" defined, verbatim: "A control abstraction defines a method for sequencing arbitrary actions. All languages provide built-in control abstractions; examples are the if statement and the while statement. In addition, however, CLU allows user definitions of a simple kind of control abstraction. The method provided is a generalization of the repetition methods available in many programming languages. Frequently the programmer desires to perform the same action for all the objects in a collection, such as all characters in a string or all items in a set. CLU provides a linguistic construct called an iterator for defining how the objects in the collection are obtained. The iterator is used in conjunction with the for statement; the body of the for statement describes the action to be taken." (Liskov, Snyder, Atkinson, Schaffert, "Abstraction Mechanisms in CLU", CACM 20(8), August 1977, section 1; [PDF](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf))
- The motivating example in the 1977 paper: a general `sorted_bag` should not have a `print` operation, but "there should be some way for the user to obtain the elements of the sorted_bag ... One possible approach is to map the elements of a sorted_bag into a sequence object, a solution potentially requiring a large amount of space. A more efficient method is provided by CLU and is discussed below. This solution computes the sequence one element at a time, thus saving space. If only part of the sequence is used (as in a search for some element), then execution time can be saved as well." ([Liskov et al. 1977, section 4.1](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf))
- Separation of selection from action, verbatim: "The purpose of many loops is to perform an action on some or all of the objects in a collection. For such loops, it is often useful to separate the selection of the next object from the action performed on that object. ... CLU provides a control abstraction that permits a complete decomposition of the two activities. The for statement available in many programming languages provides a limited ability in this direction: it iterates over ranges of integers. The CLU for statement can iterate over collections of any type of object. The selection of the next object in the collection is done by a user-defined iterator. The iterator produces the objects in the collection one at a time (the entire collection need not physically exist); each object is consumed by the for statement in turn." ([Liskov et al. 1977, section 4.2](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf))
- How `yield` resumes, verbatim: "Iterators work as follows: A for statement initially invokes an iterator, passing it some arguments. Each time a yield statement is executed in the iterator, the objects yielded are assigned to the variables declared in the for statement (following the reserved word for) in corresponding order, and the body of the for statement is executed. Then the iterator is resumed at the statement following the yield statement, in the same environment as when the objects were yielded. When the iterator terminates, by either an implicit or explicit return, the invoking for statement terminates. The iteration may also be prematurely terminated by a return in the body of the for statement." Footnote: "Zero or more objects may be yielded, but the number and types of objects yielded each time by an iterator must agree with the number and types of variables in a for statement using the iterator." ([Liskov et al. 1977, section 4.2](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf))
- Hiding representation, verbatim: "While iterators are useful in general, they are especially valuable in conjunction with data abstractions that are collections of objects (such as sets, arrays, and sorted_bags). Iterators afford users of such abstractions access to all objects in the collection without exposing irrelevant details. Several iterators may be included in a data abstraction. When the order of obtaining the objects is important, different iterators may provide different orders." ([Liskov et al. 1977, section 4.2](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf))
- The 1977 paper's recursive example: the `increasing` iterator of a binary tree "yields all items in the current tree that are less than" the node by invoking itself on the left subtree inside a `for`, then yields the node, then the right subtree, making "a complete walk over the tree" ([Liskov et al. 1977, section 4.3](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf)). Recursion works because each nested iterator is a nested call on the one stack.
- Implementation, verbatim: "Procedure invocation is relatively efficient. A single program stack is used, and argument passing is as efficient as assignment. Iterators are a form of coroutine; however, their use is sufficiently constrained that they are implemented using just the program stack. Using an iterator is therefore only slightly more expensive than using a procedure." ([Liskov et al. 1977, section 6](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf))
- Reference manual, `for`-only restriction, verbatim (OCR corrected): "The only way an iterator (see Section 13.2) can be invoked is by use of a for statement. The iterator produces a sequence of items (where an item is a group of zero or more objects) one item at a time; the body of the for statement is executed for each item in the sequence." And: "Execution of the for statement proceeds as follows. First the iterator is invoked, and it either yields an item or terminates. If the iterator yields an item, its execution is temporarily suspended, the objects in the item are assigned to the loop variables, the body of the for statement is executed, and then execution of the iterator is resumed (from the point of suspension). Whenever the iterator terminates, the entire for statement terminates." (Liskov, Atkinson, Bloom, Moss, Schaffert, Scheifler, Snyder, CLU Reference Manual, MIT/LCS/TR-225, October 1979, section 11.5.2; [bitsavers mirror](https://ftp.mirrorservice.org/sites/www.bitsavers.org/pdf/mit/lcs/tr/MIT-LCS-TR-0225.pdf))
- Reference manual, the `yield` statement, verbatim (OCR corrected): "Yield statements may occur only in the body of an iterator. The form of a yield statement is: `yield [ ( expression , ... ) ]`. It has the effect of suspending operation of the iterator, and returning control to the invoking for statement. The values obtained by evaluating the expressions (left to right) are passed to the for statement to be assigned to the corresponding list of identifiers." ([CLU Reference Manual 1979, section 11.8](https://ftp.mirrorservice.org/sites/www.bitsavers.org/pdf/mit/lcs/tr/MIT-LCS-TR-0225.pdf))
- Reference manual overview, verbatim (OCR corrected): "An iterator computes a sequence of items based on its input arguments. These items are provided to its invoker one at a time. Each item consists of zero or more objects. An iterator is invoked by a for statement. The iterator provides each item by yielding it. ... Then control is returned to the iterator so it can yield the next item in the sequence. The for loop is terminated when the iterator terminates, or the for loop body may explicitly terminate itself and the iterator." ([CLU Reference Manual 1979, section 1.2](https://ftp.mirrorservice.org/sites/www.bitsavers.org/pdf/mit/lcs/tr/MIT-LCS-TR-0225.pdf))

### Inferences
- CLU's answer to IPL-V's two-context problem: the producer owns the stack and the loop body runs as a nested call ("a yield effectively calls the loop body"). This is IPL-V's internal iteration with the subprocess written inline as the loop body, so both contexts sit on one stack with no housekeeping. The price is that two producers cannot be interleaved, which is the samefringe problem Liskov names (section 7).
- CLU is where the keyword `yield` and the phrase "yields one at a time" enter the lineage; Ruby's Matz is reported to have said the name comes from CLU (section 8, unverified).

### Gaps
- Atkinson, Liskov, Scheifler, "Aspects of Implementing CLU" (ACM Annual Conference 1978) not read: ACM DL returned a Cloudflare challenge. Its details of the single-stack iterator implementation are not here.
- Atkinson's September 1975 design note is cited by Liskov but I found no copy.
- The HOPL-II version of "A History of CLU" (SIGPLAN Notices 28(3), 1993) not compared with the TR-561 text I read; the quotations are from TR-561 (April 1992).

## 4. Icon generators (Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981; Griswold and Griswold)

### Takeaway
Icon's generators came from string processing and search, not from data abstraction. SNOBOL4's pattern matching had backtracking locked inside a pattern sublanguage; Icon made backtracking part of ordinary expression evaluation, so any expression can produce a sequence of values on demand, and failure resumes the most recent dormant generator.
A procedure becomes a generator with `suspend`, which returns a value while leaving the activation resumable. Co-expressions (later) free a generator from its syntactic position and can serve as coroutines.

### Cited Findings
- Motivation, verbatim: "One of the major motivations for Icon is the continuing effort to provide better string manipulation facilities. ... Briefly stated, the major issue is that, in most languages, string manipulation facilities constitute a separate sublanguage, and interaction between the sublanguage and the language proper is awkward at best. In addition, if the facilities include a search and backtrack mechanism, its use is usually restricted to the sublanguage, and the programmer typically has little control over its operation." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981, section 1](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- Generalization, verbatim: "One of the major differences between Icon and previous work is that the evaluation mechanism, which includes a limited form of backtracking, is not restricted to the string processing facility but is used everywhere. Unlike SNOBOL4, for example, the string processing facilities can be thought of as a natural consequence of the evaluation mechanism. The central concept in the Icon evaluation mechanism is the generator. A generator is an expression that is capable of producing a sequence of values. The first value is produced when the generator is evaluated during ordinary computation. Subsequent values are produced when alternatives are requested. ... It is this integration of generator control with evaluative control that gives Icon its flexibility and power and permits concise, natural solutions to typical search and backtrack problems. Perhaps more important, generators facilitate the composition of conventional and unconventional control structures from more basic constituents." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981, section 1](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf); "SNOBOL4" OCR-corrected from "SNOBOIA")
- Limited backtracking, verbatim: "Backtracking associated with the production of alternative values is limited in its extent by syntactic constructions. This limited "scope" of generators avoids the inefficiency of uncontrolled backtracking and gives the programmer more explicit control in the search for alternatives. This latter capability is especially important in light of the observation that, in many cases, backtracking is simply not needed [12, 25]." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- Dormant generators, verbatim: "Generators that appear in an expression are evaluated, produce their first value, and become dormant. A dormant generator is an operation whose evaluation has produced (at least) one value and which has the capacity to produce more, but which is not presently being evaluated. Failure of an operation initiates backtracking, in which dormant generators are activated to produce alternate values. The activation of a dormant generator transfers control to the point in the expression where that generator became dormant." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981, section 3.1](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- `every`, verbatim: "The every expression is used to force a generator to produce all its alternatives. The expression `every e1 do e2` evaluates e1 and repeatedly reactivates it until all of its alternatives are produced. After each alternative of e1, the expression e2 is evaluated. For example, if sum is initialized to zero, the expression `every i := !x do sum := sum + i` finds the sum of the elements of list x. Note that, by using the element generator ! instead of explicit indexing, this expression is independent of not only the size of x but also the type of x." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- `suspend`, verbatim: "The expression `suspend e` is similar to `return e`, except that the procedure activation is left in suspension so that it may be resumed for additional computation. Execution of the procedure body is resumed at the point of suspension if the context in which the procedure invocation occurs requires another alternative. Thus, suspended procedures are dormant generators. The result of the suspend expression itself is the null value." Example: `procedure toby(i, n, inc) ... while i <= n do { suspend i; i := i + inc } fail end`, and "The procedure suspends evaluation for each value in the sequence and fails after the sequence is exhausted." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981, section 3](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- `suspend` over a generator and recursion, verbatim: "Like every, suspend e produces all alternatives of e as required. For example, `suspend 1|2|5` suspends with the values 1, 2, and 5 on successive activations of the procedure in which it appears. ... Programmer-defined generators are fully recursive and behave exactly like built-in generators. For example, multiple activations of defined generators that are suspended simultaneously do not interfere with each other." Tree walk: `procedure walk(t) if null(t) then fail else { suspend walk(t.ltree | t.rtree); return t.data } end`, and "The suspend expression suspends with each of the values generated by walking the left subtree and then with each of the values generated by walking the right subtree." A `leaves(t)` procedure generates only the leaves the same way. ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981, sections 3 and 4](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- Icon book on `suspend`, verbatim: "A procedure also may generate a sequence of values by using suspend ... The suspend expression produces a value from the procedure call in the same manner as return, but the call is suspended and can be resumed. If it is resumed, evaluation continues following the point of suspension." And on what "suspends" means generally: "An expression that produces a value and may be capable of producing another one suspends. Instead of just producing a value and "going away", it keeps track of what it was doing and remains "in the background" in case it is needed again. Failure causes a suspended generator to be resumed so that it may produce another value." (R. E. Griswold and M. T. Griswold, The Icon Programming Language, 3rd ed., chapters 2 and 6 area; [Arizona PDF](https://www2.cs.arizona.edu/icon/ftp/doc/lb1up.pdf))
- Co-expressions, verbatim: "In normal expression evaluation, the results produced by an expression are limited to the place where that expression appears in the program. Furthermore, the results of an expression can be produced only by iteration or goal-directed evaluation; there is no mechanism for explicitly resuming an expression to get a result. Consequently, the results produced by an expression are strictly constrained, both in location and in the sequence of program evaluation. Co-expressions overcome these limitations. A co-expression "captures" an expression so that it can be explicitly resumed at any time and place." And: "Although co-expressions are motivated by the need to control the results produced by generators, they also can be used as coroutines." ([Griswold and Griswold, The Icon Programming Language, 3rd ed., chapter 9](https://www2.cs.arizona.edu/icon/ftp/doc/lb1up.pdf))
- The book's lockstep example: a `Parallel{expr1, expr2}` procedure built from co-expressions alternates results ("the results for Parallel{!&lcase, !&ucase} are "a", "A", "b", "B" ... "z", and "Z"") ([Griswold and Griswold, chapter 9](https://www2.cs.arizona.edu/icon/ftp/doc/lb1up.pdf)). This is the side-by-side traversal that CLU's single stack rules out.
- Icon versus CLU and Alphard, verbatim: "Superficially, generators in ALPHARD and CLU appear to correspond to the every expression in Icon. But while every has the syntactic appearance of a standard control structure, the nonstandard goal-directed evaluation mechanism makes its semantics more general than the corresponding ALPHARD and CLU constructs. It is the restricted applicability and the lack of goal-directed evaluation that differentiate these constructs from generators in Icon." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981, section 5](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- Conclusion, verbatim: "Generators provide a linguistic facility that allows concise expression of solutions to problems suitable for goal-directed programming. These problems are typically found in string processing, artificial intelligence, and other areas in which combinatorial searching is performed." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981, section 6](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf))
- Implementation history: "Implementations of Icon for the DEC-10 and CDC Cyber have been operational since mid-1978" ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)).

### Inferences
- `suspend walk(t.ltree | t.rtree)` is a 1981 form of Python's `yield from` over a recursive generator; Python's `yield from` (PEP 380) was not modeled on it as far as I know, so treat this as a parallel, not a lineage.
- Icon solved the two-context problem by keeping dormant generator activations alive (the Icon implementation book describes the mechanism; not read here). The search framing (produce a candidate, let failure ask for the next) is the third independent motivation in this survey, beside IPL-V/CLU's loop separation and Conniver's lazy possibilities.

### Gaps
- Griswold and Griswold, "History of the Icon Programming Language" (HOPL-II, 1993) not read: ACM DL returned a Cloudflare challenge and no Arizona copy turned up. Its account of SL5's coroutines and of why co-expressions were added (Icon Version 5, about 1982, unverified) is missing.
- SL5's coroutine design (Hanson and Griswold 1978, Icon's reference [20]) not read.
- The Icon book's edition year: the PDF is the third edition (describes Version 9.3); I believe it was published in 1996 but did not confirm it from the PDF.

## 5. Sather iterators (Murer, Omohundro, Stoutamire, Szyperski 1993 to 1996)

### Takeaway
Sather replaced C++-style cursor objects, which force the programmer to encode the iteration's control flow as data, with CLU-style iterators written "in an active style". It then removed CLU's limits: several iterator calls may appear anywhere in one loop (so two structures can be walked together, solving samefringe), and "hot" arguments pass a new value in on each resumption.
The authors call iterators "structured coroutines" and distinguish them from generators, whose half-consumed state can move between call sites.

### Cited Findings
- Abstract, verbatim: "Sather extends the notion of an iterator in a powerful new way. We argue that iteration abstractions belong in class interfaces on an equal footing with routines. Sather iterators were derived from CLU iterators but are much more flexible and better suited for object-oriented programming. We retain the property that iterators are structured, i.e. strictly bound to a controlling structured statement." (Murer, Omohundro, Stoutamire, Szyperski, "Iteration Abstraction in Sather", NECI TR 95-153, October 1995; [PDF](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf); published in ACM TOPLAS 18(1):1-15, 1996, per the [ICSI publications page](https://ftp.gwdg.de/pub/gnu/www/software/sather/ICSI_Sather/Publications/toplas.html))
- Motivation, verbatim: "The original Sather had a fairly conventional until ... loop ... end statement. While this suffices for the most basic iterative tasks, we felt the need for a more general construct. As with C++, Sather 0 libraries made heavy use of cursor objects to iterate through the contents of container objects [13]. While these work quite well in certain circumstances, they have a number of problems ..." ([Murer et al. 1995, Introduction](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf))
- Motivation continued, verbatim: "Like the language designers of CLU [7], we felt a need to encapsulate the common operation of iterating through a data structure. Typical loops (such as Sather until loops) initialize some iteration variables and then repeatedly execute the body of the loop, updating the variables in some way, and testing for the end of the loop. Important examples of this pattern arise when stepping through the elements of container objects. The code for initializing, updating, and testing iteration variables is often complex and error prone. Errors having to do with the initialization or termination of iteration ("fencepost errors") are very common. The code to step through complex containers such as hash tables typically must utilize the detailed internal structure of the container, sometimes causing duplication of virtually the same code in many places. Each of these observations argues for making the iteration operation a part of the interface of the container class rather than a part of the code in the client of the container." ([Murer et al. 1995](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf))
- The active-style goal, verbatim: "Another goal for the iterator design was to allow iterators to be programmed in an active style, without having to explicitly encode the control structure as state, as is required for cursors and most other iteration constructs." ([Murer et al. 1995](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf))
- CLU's limitations, verbatim: "The name "iterator" and the initial design were derived from the iterator construct in the CLU language: A CLU iterator is like a routine except that it may "yield" in addition to returning. It may only be called in the head of a special "for" loop construct. The loop is executed once each time the iterator yields a value. Upon termination of the iterator, the loop exits. While CLU iterators can deal with the simplest iteration situations, such as stepping through the elements of arrays and other containers, they have several limitations which Sather iterators remove: One iterator per loop: There is no simple way to step through two structures simultaneously in CLU. No way to modify elements: While CLU iterators support the retrieval of elements from a structure, there is no straightforward way to add or modify elements. Iterator arguments are loop invariant: There is no clean way to pass loop variant values to an iterator." ([Murer et al. 1995](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf))
- The two extensions, verbatim: "Multiple iterators may be invoked within a single loop, because they may occur as expressions anywhere in the loop and are not restricted to the loop head. This allows stepping through multiple structures simultaneously. The loop terminates as soon as the first of the iterators terminates." And: "In contrast to CLU, Sather provides hot iterator arguments which are reevaluated each time the control is passed back to the iterator. Such arguments may be used to pass data to the iterator which varies on each iteration. In contrast to CLU iterators which may only generate a sequence of values, these arguments allow classes to define iterators that modify successive elements of a structure, i.e. to "consume" a sequence of values." ([Murer et al. 1995](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf); "CLU" OCR-corrected from "OLU")
- `yield` and `quit`, verbatim: "Iterators may only be called within loop statements. When an iterator is called, its body is executed until it either quits or yields. If it yields, any return value is returned to the loop and execution continues as if it were a routine call. The execution state of the iterator is maintained, however. The next call to the iterator will transfer control to the statement following the yield. The local variables and once arguments retain their previous values. When an iterator quits instead of yielding, the loop is immediately broken and execution continues with the statement which follows the loop. In order to be able to distinguish visually between iterator and routine calls, the language requires iterator names to end with an exclamation mark." ([Murer et al. 1995, section 2](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)) The built-in `while!` "yields without returning a value. It is merely used to control the loop."
- Cursors, verbatim: "We found that while cursors work quite well in certain circumstances, they can also become quite cumbersome. They require maintaining a parallel cursor object hierarchy alongside each container class hierarchy. Normally it is required to explicitly create and garbage collect cursor objects. Cursors can be semantically confusing since they maintain a location in a container for an indefinite period of time during which the container may change. Since the storage associated with a cursor is explicit, it is inconvenient to use them to describe nested or recursive control structures. Because cursors explicitly describe their implementation, they prevent a number of important optimizations on inner loops." And: "Note that Ellis and Stroustrup [2] call the use of cursor objects in C++ "iterators"." ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.2](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf))
- Iterators versus cursors, verbatim: "The state of iterators is maintained only for the duration of a single loop. Iterators may be arbitrarily nested and support recursion just like routines. The iterator implementation manages the use of storage and can use the stack instead of the heap unless there are multiple recursive iterators." ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.2](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf))
- Iterators as structured coroutines, and generators distinguished, verbatim: "More precisely, iterators may be thought of as structured coroutines. In many languages, coroutines can call other coroutines in an arbitrary fashion. Structured programming replaced the undisciplined transfer of control by "goto" statements with structured loop constructs. Iterators are more structured than coroutines with respect to the freedom in passing control. While a suspending coroutine may transfer control to any other waiting coroutine, in the case of iterators the flow of control is structured by the "loop"-statement. ... Coroutines with this added restriction of always returning to their caller are sometimes called generators, semicoroutines, or hierarchical coroutines [8]. Iterators share this property. However, just as for streams, a generator's activation state is not bound to a single call point within a single loop. Instead, a generator may be left in a half-consumed state at one point and picked up at another. ... this is not possible with iterators." ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.4](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf); reference [8] is C. D. Marlin, Coroutines, 1980)
- Streams versus iterators, verbatim: "Whereas streams may be passed around in a half-consumed state, the state of an iterator is confined to its calling "loop"-statement, and even more so, to a single call point within that loop. It is not possible to suspend iteration in one loop and to resume it with the same internal state in another loop." ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf))
- Summary, verbatim: "The four constructs most commonly used for iteration are streams, cursors, built-in looping constructs (of which the Common Lisp variant is the most complex), and explicitly passing the loop body (as a closure, block, or otherwise). The main points when comparing these constructs with Sather iterators are that cursors are harder to use and less suitable for compiler-based optimizations (but can be passed around), built-in loop constructs are not general enough, and passing closures does not effectively support the simultaneous traversal of multiple data structures. Streams and in particular generators come closest to Sather iterators, but are not bound to a specific loop." ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.6](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf))
- The 1993 ICSI report, "Sather Iters: Object-Oriented Iteration Abstraction" (TR-93-045, Murer, Omohundro, Szyperski), is listed with the summary "iterators ... encapsulate iteration ... derived from CLU iterators but are much more flexible and better suited for object-oriented programming" ([ICSI Sather publications page](https://ftp.gwdg.de/pub/gnu/www/software/sather/ICSI_Sather/Publications/tr-93-045.html)). The PDF at the old ICSI URL returned 404; I read the 1995 version.
- PEP 255 cites the TOPLAS paper as reference [2] and calls Sather's iterators "inspired by iterators in CLU" ([PEP 255](https://peps.python.org/pep-0255/)).

### Inferences
- Sather's "active style, without having to explicitly encode the control structure as state" is the clearest single-sentence statement in this literature of the problem generators solve. It is the same complaint PEP 255 makes about tokenize as an iterator ("this shifts the burden on tokenize to remember *its* state between .next() invocations").
- Sather's "hot arguments" are another precedent (with Conniver's TRY-NEXT message) for passing values into a resumed producer, which Python gained in PEP 342.
- Python's generator is, in Sather's own taxonomy, a "generator" rather than an "iterator": its frame lives on the heap and can be "left in a half-consumed state at one point and picked up at another". That is the capability CLU gave up for the single stack.

### Gaps
- The 1993 TR text and the 1996 TOPLAS text were not compared with the 1995 NECI report; wording may differ slightly.

## 6. Lazy streams and demand-driven evaluation (the functional answer)

### Takeaway
The functional line states the same problem as a cost: writing a program as transformations of whole lists is elegant but builds huge intermediate structures and computes elements nobody uses. Delayed evaluation (Landin 1965; Friedman and Wise 1976 in Lisp) lets a sequence exist only as far as the consumer has demanded it.
I could read this line only through SICP and through mentions in the Icon and Sather papers; the primary papers were not available to me.

### Cited Findings
- SICP's problem statement, verbatim: "Unfortunately, if we represent sequences as lists, this elegance is bought at the price of severe inefficiency with respect to both the time and space required by our computations. When we represent manipulations on sequences as transformations of lists, our programs must construct and copy data structures (which may be huge) at every step of a process." ([Abelson and Sussman, SICP 2nd ed., section 3.5.1](https://sarabander.github.io/sicp/html/3_002e5.xhtml))
- SICP's example, verbatim: "The inefficiency in using lists becomes painfully apparent if we use the sequence paradigm to compute the second prime in the interval from 10,000 to 1,000,000 ... This expression does find the second prime, but the computational overhead is outrageous. We construct a list of almost a million integers, filter this list by testing each element for primality, and then ignore almost all of the result. In a more traditional programming style, we would interleave the enumeration and the filtering, and stop when we reached the second prime." ([SICP section 3.5.1](https://sarabander.github.io/sicp/html/3_002e5.xhtml))
- SICP's solution, verbatim: "Streams are a clever idea that allows one to use sequence manipulations without incurring the costs of manipulating sequences as lists. With streams we can achieve the best of both worlds: We can formulate programs elegantly as sequence manipulations, while attaining the efficiency of incremental computation. The basic idea is to arrange to construct a stream only partially, and to pass the partial construction to the program that consumes the stream." ([SICP section 3.5.1](https://sarabander.github.io/sicp/html/3_002e5.xhtml))
- SICP's history footnote, verbatim: "Delayed evaluation, which is the key to making streams practical, was inherent in Algol 60's call-by-name parameter-passing method. The use of this mechanism to implement streams was first described by Landin (1965). Delayed evaluation for streams was introduced into Lisp by Friedman and Wise (1976). In their implementation, cons always delays evaluating its arguments, so that lists automatically behave as streams." ([SICP section 3.5, footnote](https://sarabander.github.io/sicp/html/3_002e5.xhtml))
- SICP on streams and hidden state, verbatim: "As we saw in 3.1.2, one of the major benefits of introducing assignment is that we can increase the modularity of our systems by encapsulating, or "hiding," parts of the state of a large system within local variables. Stream models can provide an equivalent modularity without the use of assignment." ([SICP section 3.5.5](https://sarabander.github.io/sicp/html/3_002e5.xhtml))
- Friedman and Wise, "CONS Should Not Evaluate its Arguments", in Automata, Languages and Programming (Michaelson and Milner, eds.), Edinburgh University Press, 1976, pp. 257-284; also Indiana University TR44 ([citation per a Haskell-cafe thread](https://mail.haskell.org/pipermail/haskell-cafe/2008-January/038189.html)). Not read.
- Sather's view of streams: "Iterators also share many characteristics with streams [1]" (reference [1] is SICP), and the half-consumed-state distinction quoted in section 5 ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)). On Hewitt's proposal of lazy evaluation as the alternative to Conniver: "While lazy evaluation allows for the effective handling of multiple recursive data structures, it also poses a particularly difficult problem for efficient implementations." (same source, section 4.1)

### Inferences
- The functional line and the generator line target the same two costs (building the whole sequence; computing elements no one needs) that Liskov names for CLU ("expensive if the collection is large"; "If only part of the sequence is used ... execution time can be saved as well") and PEP 255 names for tokenize ("no a priori bound can be placed on the memory"; "a severe waste of time"). The difference is where the producer's state lives: in a chain of delayed thunks (streams) or in a suspended activation (generators).

### Gaps
- Not read, so no verbatim problem statements: P. J. Landin, "A Correspondence between ALGOL 60 and Church's Lambda-Notation" (CACM 8(2-3), 1965); Friedman and Wise 1976; P. Henderson and J. H. Morris, "A Lazy Evaluator" (POPL 1976; ACM DL blocked); G. Kahn and D. B. MacQueen, "Coroutines and Networks of Parallel Processes" (IFIP 1977). Any claim about how each paper states its problem is unverified here.

## 7. The samefringe problem

### Takeaway
Samefringe (do two trees have the same leaves in left-to-right order?) is the standard test case for the limitation every single-stack or callback design shares: each tree walk is a recursion in progress, and the comparison needs both recursions suspended at once.
Liskov names it as what CLU cannot do; Sather's paper solves it with two iterators in one loop and credits Hewitt for the problem; a 1977 SIGART debate argued whether it needs coroutines.

### Cited Findings
- Gabriel's account, verbatim: "The samefringe problem is this: two binary trees have the same fringe if they have exactly the same leaves reading from left to right." "The problem, as stated in 1976, was to write a samefringe program that does not consume a lot of storage". "In 1977, in the pages of the ACM SIGART Newsletter, a debate raged over whether samefringe is the simplest problem that requires multiprocessing or coroutines to easily solve. A number of people contributed solutions - including John McCarthy." "John's solution was startlingly simple." (R. P. Gabriel, "The Design of Parallel Programming Languages", [dreamsongs.com](https://dreamsongs.com/10ideas.html); the fetch tool returned the sentences in fragments, joined here as they appeared)
- Sather paper, verbatim: "Consider the task of comparing the elements of two trees according to a pre-order traversal. This is the classical "same fringe problem" as defined by Hewitt [5, page 344-347]. A simple solution using iterators is shown in Fig. 2." And: "In this kind of situation with more than one structure, it is not possible to pass the body of the routine to one of the trees for execution. Thus, in cases requiring the traversal of multiple structures, the use of blocks or closures is impractical, while the situation is easily handled by the iterator construct. This approach cannot be used in CLU, which allows only a single iterator per loop." ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.5](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf); Hewitt [5] is "Viewing control structures as patterns of passing messages", Artificial Intelligence 8, 1977)
- The Sather solution (Figure 2) defines a recursive `inorder!` iterator (`loop yield left.inorder! end; yield key; loop yield right.inorder! end`) and a `same_fringe` routine that calls `closed_inorder!` on both trees inside one `loop` ([Murer et al. 1995, Figure 2](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)).
- Liskov's CLU limitation (verbatim in section 3): "iterators cannot be used to compute whether two lists have the same elements, since to do this you need to iterate through the two lists side-by-side, and CLU only allows iterators to be nested." ([Liskov 1992](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf))
- A 2016 paper on functional coroutines says the problem's first appearance in the literature seems to illustrate a coroutine mechanism and that researchers disagreed whether coroutines were required, quoting McCarthy's view on assignment-and-goto programs ([arXiv 1606.06376](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1606.06376), seen through a search summary only, not read).
- Commentators note that solving by flattening both trees can be quadratic for left-degenerate trees ([c2 wiki SameFringeProblem](https://kidneybone.com/c2/wiki/SameFringeProblem), via search summary; secondary).

### Inferences
- Samefringe separates the designs cleanly. Internal iteration (IPL-V generators, Smalltalk `do:`, Ruby blocks) and CLU iterators cannot express it without flattening, because only one traversal can own the stack. Cursors can, but the programmer must convert each recursive walk into an explicit stack object. Lazy streams, Icon co-expressions, Sather's multiple iterators per loop, and Python generators can, because each traversal's suspended state is kept apart from the other.

### Gaps
- Hewitt's 1977 paper and the 1977 SIGART Newsletter exchange not read; Hewitt's original wording and McCarthy's text are not quoted here.

## 8. Other pre-2001 designs (one line each)

### Takeaway
Most other designs before Python chose internal iteration (the collection calls a block), which needs no suspended producer but cannot walk two collections together. Ruby kept CLU's word `yield` for calling the block.

### Cited Findings
- Smalltalk-style block iteration, as Sather describes it: "Traditionally, iteration abstraction is supported in object-oriented languages by providing anonymous blocks [3], lambda expressions [1], or closures [16]. The container classes provide methods to apply a block to all or part of their elements. The execution of such block-based iterations is controlled by the container class. With iterators, the control is shared by the iterator and the calling loop." ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.5](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf); [3] is Goldberg and Robson, Smalltalk-80, 1983)
- Ruby (1995): "Basically, blocks are designed for loop abstraction. The most basic usage of blocks is to let you define your own way for iterating over the items." "By using blocks, we can hide the loop logic inside the method or function." "In fact, in early versions of Ruby, the methods called with blocks were referred to as iterators, because they were designed to iterate." "Inside the method, you can call the block using the `yield` keyword with a value." (Y. Matsumoto interviewed by B. Venners, "Blocks and Closures in Ruby", Artima, December 2003; [Artima](https://www.artima.com/intv/closures.html)) The interview does not mention CLU.
- Ruby's `yield` named after CLU: a search summary reports a ruby-talk post by Matsumoto saying "`yield' is the historical name inherited from CLU, which is the foundation of the concept of iterator" ([ruby-talk archive](https://blade.ruby-lang.org/ruby-talk/1017)). Unverified: I did not open the post.
- Common Lisp: Sather notes the `loop` macro's clauses are all built in ("there is currently no specified portable method for users to add extensions to the Loop Facility") and that "the incorporation of series, generators and gatherers has been proposed" ([Murer et al. 1995, sections 4.1 and 4.3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)).
- Oberon and Ethos "riders" are cursor objects ("Riders are a similar idea introduced in Oberon [19] and generalized in Ethos [17]") ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.2](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)).
- Modula-2: Sather cites Wirth's Programming in Modula-2 (1983) as its coroutine reference when it says "we may model iterators and the loop body as coroutines [18]" ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.4](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)). Modula-2's coroutines (the NEWPROCESS and TRANSFER primitives) are general coroutines, not generators; this is from memory and unverified.
- Sather-K (Karlsruhe) "does generalize iterators by introducing first class stream objects that retain their state across loop termination" ([Murer et al. 1995, section 4.3](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)).
- PEP 255's own list of the pre-generator options for Python: a callback added "to the producer's argument list" (pleasant for the producer, convoluted for the consumer, as in tabnanny's tokeneater "maintaining a state machine in global variables"); producing the whole result as a list (impractical when "no a priori bound can be placed on the memory" and when a client "only want[s] to see whether something specific appears early"); an iterator object, which "shifts the burden on tokenize to remember *its* state between .next() invocations", and for a recursive tree walk "requires removing the recursion manually and maintaining the state of the traversal by hand"; threads; and Stackless. Then: "Some other high-level languages provide pleasant solutions, notably iterators in Sather [2], which were inspired by iterators in CLU; and generators in Icon [3], a novel language where every expression *is a generator*. There are differences among these, but the basic idea is the same: provide a kind of function that can return an intermediate result ("the next value") to its caller, but maintaining the function's local state so that the function can be resumed again right where it left off." ([PEP 255, Motivation](https://peps.python.org/pep-0255/))

### Inferences
- PEP 255's list of four rejected alternatives maps onto the earlier designs: the callback is IPL-V's generator and Smalltalk/Ruby block iteration; the whole list is SICP's "outrageous" list of a million integers; the iterator object is the cursor that Sather rejected; threads and Stackless are general coroutines. PEP 255 chose the Icon/Sather answer, without CLU's and Sather's tie to one loop.

### Gaps
- Scheme generators built on `call/cc` (for example Haynes, Friedman, Wand, "Continuations and Coroutines", 1984): not researched; unverified.
- BETA: nothing found in this pass.
- Lua coroutines (Lua 5.0, 2003) and C# 2.0 `yield return` (2005) are later than the period; mentioned from memory, unverified.
- Smalltalk's `do:` primary text (Goldberg and Robson) not read; the description is Sather's.

## 9. Recurring problem statements across the sources

### Takeaway
Five problem statements recur, and the earliest source (IPL-V, 1960) already names the first two and the cause of the fifth.
The core idea underneath all of them: a loop has a producing half and a consuming half, each with its own state (local variables and a position in its own control flow), and a call stack lets only one half keep that state in its natural form. Generators let both keep it.

### Cited Findings
- (a) Iterating over an abstract collection without exposing its representation. IPL-V: separating "the "production of elements" part of a loop from the "processing" part" ([Newell and Tonge 1960](https://iiif.library.cmu.edu/file/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001.pdf)). Alphard: the loop variable can "operate on abstract entities without explicit dependence on the representation" ([summary](https://research.cs.queensu.ca/home/cordy/cisc860/Biblio/drb/CS/shaw77.pdf)). CLU: "for some types there is no obvious way to get to the elements" and iterators give "access to all objects in the collection without exposing irrelevant details" ([Liskov 1992](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf); [Liskov et al. 1977](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf)). Sather: "making the iteration operation a part of the interface of the container class rather than a part of the code in the client" ([Murer et al. 1995](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)). Ruby: "hide the loop logic inside the method" ([Artima 2003](https://www.artima.com/intv/closures.html)).
- (b) A producer whose natural form is a loop or a recursion. Sather: "to allow iterators to be programmed in an active style, without having to explicitly encode the control structure as state, as is required for cursors" and cursors make it "inconvenient ... to describe nested or recursive control structures" ([Murer et al. 1995](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)). PEP 255: tokenize as an iterator is "a horrid chore", and a recursive tree producer "requires removing the recursion manually and maintaining the state of the traversal by hand" ([PEP 255](https://peps.python.org/pep-0255/)). Icon's and CLU's recursive tree walks ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf); [Liskov et al. 1977](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf)).
- (c) Search with backtracking. Conniver's possibility lists and "the generator has no idea how many possibilities its superior wants" ([Conniver manual](https://www.saildart.org/CNVR.RPO[UP,DOC])). Icon: "concise, natural solutions to typical search and backtrack problems" and "areas in which combinatorial searching is performed" ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)).
- (d) Avoiding an intermediate data structure, or handling an unbounded or partly used sequence. CLU: a procedure returning an array of the elements "is expensive if the collection is large"; "the entire collection need not physically exist"; "If only part of the sequence is used ... execution time can be saved as well" ([Liskov 1992](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf); [Liskov et al. 1977](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~weimerw/2008-615/reading/liskov-clu-abstraction.pdf)). Conniver: "much more expensive to generate each one than to try it out" ([Conniver manual](https://www.saildart.org/CNVR.RPO[UP,DOC])). SICP: "construct and copy data structures (which may be huge) at every step" ([SICP 3.5](https://sarabander.github.io/sicp/html/3_002e5.xhtml)). Icon's `fib` generates an "(infinite) sequence" ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)). PEP 255: "no a priori bound can be placed on the memory" ([PEP 255](https://peps.python.org/pep-0255/)).
- (e) Keeping the implementation on one stack, and what it costs. IPL-V: "two contexts of information in working storage must coexist ... Hence the strict hierarchy of routines and subroutines is violated" ([Newell and Tonge 1960](https://iiif.library.cmu.edu/file/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001/Newell_box00004_fld00298_doc0001.pdf)). CLU: "their use is limited so that CLU programs can make do with a single stack ... iterators cannot be used to compute whether two lists have the same elements" ([Liskov 1992](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf)). Sather: iterators "can use the stack instead of the heap unless there are multiple recursive iterators", but their state cannot be "left in a half-consumed state at one point and picked up at another" ([Murer et al. 1995](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)). Conniver kept the generator's frame as a first-class object ("a pointer to the generator's activation") ([Conniver manual](https://www.saildart.org/CNVR.RPO[UP,DOC])).

### Inferences
- An answer to the author's "WHY", built from the sources: generators were invented because separating "what elements" from "what to do with each" (a) is good design, and the separation creates two pieces of code that each have state best kept as local variables and a position in a loop or recursion (b). A subroutine call keeps only the callee's state alive across the call, so one side must flatten its state into data: the consumer becomes a callback with a state machine (IPL-V subprocess, PEP 255's tokeneater), or the producer becomes a cursor object with its recursion unwound (Alphard, C++, PEP 255's iterator option), or the whole sequence is built first (SICP's million-integer list). A generator keeps the producer's activation alive between values, so both sides keep their natural form. Search (c) and laziness (d) are the payoffs that drove particular designs (Conniver, Icon, streams), and (e) is the implementation question of where the suspended activation lives, which decides whether two producers can run side by side (samefringe).
- The sequence of designs reads as a progression on (e): IPL-V saves the generator's context by hand; CLU nests the loop body on one stack and gives up side-by-side iteration; Icon and Sather keep several suspended producers per expression or loop; Conniver, Icon co-expressions, streams, Sather-K, and Python let the suspended producer be a value that can be passed around.

### Gaps
- The sorting under (a) to (e) is my synthesis; no single source proposes it.

## 10. Verification of two sentences from the chapter

### Takeaway
Both sentences are supported, with small precision issues: Icon calls `suspend` an expression, not a statement, and resumes it only when the context asks for another value; a CLU `yield` passes an "item" of zero or more objects, and a Sather `yield` may pass no value at all.

### Cited Findings
- Sentence 1: "Icon's `suspend` statement produces a value and leaves the procedure ready to produce another." Supported by: "The expression `suspend e` is similar to `return e`, except that the procedure activation is left in suspension so that it may be resumed for additional computation. Execution of the procedure body is resumed at the point of suspension if the context in which the procedure invocation occurs requires another alternative." ([Griswold, Hanson, Korb 1981, section 3](https://drh.github.io/documents/generators-in-icon.pdf)) And: "The suspend expression produces a value from the procedure call in the same manner as return, but the call is suspended and can be resumed. If it is resumed, evaluation continues following the point of suspension." ([Griswold and Griswold, The Icon Programming Language, 3rd ed.](https://www2.cs.arizona.edu/icon/ftp/doc/lb1up.pdf))
- Precision notes on sentence 1: both sources call it "the suspend expression" (Icon is expression-oriented: "Icon is an expression-oriented language"; "The result of the suspend expression itself is the null value"), so "statement" is loose. "`suspend e` produces all alternatives of e as required", so one `suspend` can produce several values if e is itself a generator (`suspend 1|2|5`). Sources as above.
- Sentence 2: "CLU and Sather use the word `yield` for the same step inside an iterator, which hands one element at a time to a loop." CLU support: "Yield statements may occur only in the body of an iterator ... It has the effect of suspending operation of the iterator, and returning control to the invoking for statement. The values obtained by evaluating the expressions (left to right) are passed to the for statement to be assigned to the corresponding list of identifiers." ([CLU Reference Manual 1979, section 11.8](https://ftp.mirrorservice.org/sites/www.bitsavers.org/pdf/mit/lcs/tr/MIT-LCS-TR-0225.pdf)) and "A CLU iterator is a single operation that yields its results incrementally ... it yields them one at a time." ([Liskov 1992](https://publications.csail.mit.edu/lcs/pubs/pdf/MIT-LCS-TR-561.pdf)). Sather support: "When an iterator is called, its body is executed until it either quits or yields. If it yields, any return value is returned to the loop ... The execution state of the iterator is maintained, however. The next call to the iterator will transfer control to the statement following the yield." ([Murer et al. 1995, section 2](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)) Both papers name the construct "iterator", so "inside an iterator" matches both.
- Precision notes on sentence 2: a CLU `yield` hands over an "item", which "consists of zero or more objects" ([CLU Reference Manual, section 1.2](https://ftp.mirrorservice.org/sites/www.bitsavers.org/pdf/mit/lcs/tr/MIT-LCS-TR-0225.pdf)), so it can be a tuple or nothing. Sather's `while!` "yields without returning a value" ([Murer et al. 1995](https://steveomohundro.com/wp-content/uploads/2009/03/murer_omohundro95_iteration_abstraction_in_sather.pdf)). In CLU the loop is a `for` statement whose head calls the iterator; in Sather the call can sit anywhere in a `loop ... end`, and control returns to that call point. "One element at a time to a loop" is accurate for the collection case both papers lead with.

### Inferences
- If the chapter wants to tie Icon to Python precisely, Icon's `suspend` is closer to Python's `yield from` than to `yield` when its operand is a generator (`suspend walk(t.ltree | t.rtree)`).

### Gaps
- None for the two sentences; both rest on primary sources read in full text.
