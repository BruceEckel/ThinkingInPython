# The Origin of Coroutines (1958 to about 1980) and the Subroutine Problem They Solved

Scope: the pre-history of the generator.
A generator is a restricted coroutine, so this file collects what the people who invented and used coroutines said the subroutine could not do.
Every quotation below was read in the source named beside it unless it is marked "secondary" (quoted by another author) or "unverified".
Where a page could only be reached through an unofficial copy, the note says so.

Working copies of the primary texts used here (extracted with `pdftotext`):
Conway 1963, Nygaard and Dahl 1978, Dahl 2001, Ritchie 1979/1984, McIlroy 2016, de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004, Hoare 1977 (edited 2009), and Jackson 2001.

## 1. Conway: what problem the coroutine solved, and how the 1963 paper defines it

### Takeaway
Conway's paper defines a coroutine as a module "coded as an autonomous program which communicates with adjacent modules as if they were input or output subroutines", so that "coroutines are subroutines all at the same level, each acting as if it were the master program when in fact there is no master program."
His motivating example is a subroutine that must keep a "switch" to remember "the situation at the last call", because each call must return exactly one output; the coroutine replaces the switch with the program's own position, "implicitly by use of the subroutine calling sequence."

### Cited Findings
- Bibliographic facts: Melvin E. Conway, "Design of a Separable Transition-Diagram Compiler", *Communications of the ACM* 6(7), July 1963, pp. 396-408. Affiliation printed: "Directorate of Computers, USAF, L. G. Hanscom Field, Bedford, Mass." The title footnote reads: "The work described here was performed at Case Institute of Technology in 1961 and was supported in part by Univac Division of Sperry Rand Corporation." The paper itself gives no 1958 date. Source: [Conway 1963, PDF on melconway.com](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The paper's stated purpose (Introduction): "This paper is written in rebuttal of three propositions widely held among compiler writers, to wit: (1) syntax-directed compilers [1] suffer practical disadvantages over other types of compilers, chiefly in speed; (2) compilers should be written with compilers; (3) COBOL [2] compilers must be complicated. The form of the rebuttal is to describe a high-speed, one-pass, syntax-directed COBOL compiler which can be built by two people with an assembler in less than a year." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The abstract frames the goal as memory economy: "A COBOL compiler design is presented which is compact enough to permit rapid, one-pass compilation of a large subset of COBOL on a moderately large computer. Versions of the same compiler for smaller machines require only two working tapes plus a compiler tape." And later: "what this exposition has to offer is a collection of space-saving techniques". The first technique listed is "the coroutine method of separating programs". Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Definition of *separable* (section "Coroutines and Separable Programs"): "A program organization is separable if it is broken up into processing modules which communicate with each other according to the following restrictions: (1) the only communication between modules is in the form of discrete items of information; (2) the flow of each of these items is along fixed, one-way paths; (3) the entire program can be laid out so that the input is at the left extreme, the output is at the right extreme, and everywhere in between all information items flowing between modules have a component of motion to the right." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Definition of *coroutine*, immediately following: "Under these conditions each module may be made into a coroutine; that is, it may be coded as an autonomous program which communicates with adjacent modules as if they were input or output subroutines. Thus, coroutines are subroutines all at the same level, each acting as if it were the master program when in fact there is no master program. There is no bound placed by this definition on the number of inputs and outputs a coroutine may have." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Footnote 1 to that definition: "To the best of the author's knowledge the coroutine idea was concurrently developed by him and Joel Erdwinn, now of Computer Sciences Corporation." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The motivation, stated as a condition: "The coroutine notion can greatly simplify the conception of a program when its modules do not communicate with each other synchronously." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The motivating example (the "asterisk squasher"): a program "which reads cards and writes the string of characters it finds, column 1 of card 1 to column 80 of card 1, then column 1 of card 2, and so on, with the following wrinkle: every time there are adjacent asterisks they will be paired off from the left and each "**" will be replaced by the single character "↑"." (The scan renders the replacement character imperfectly; it is the exponentiation operator, as the next sentence says.) "This operation is done with the exponentiation operator of FORTRAN and COBOL." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The subroutine's problem, in Conway's words: "Notice that the simple requirement of compressing asterisks requires the introduction of a switch which bifurcates the subroutine and selects the part to be used at each call depending on the situation at the last call. The reason for the switch is that each call of the subroutine must result in output of exactly one character. A programmer well versed at inventing counterexamples could dream up a requirement for a subroutine which would necessitate an absurd arrangement of switches in order to preserve the one-output-for-each-call relationship required of the subroutine." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The coroutine's answer: "The coroutine approach to the same problem accomplishes the switching job implicitly by use of the subroutine calling sequence. When coroutines A and B are connected so that A sends items to B, B runs for a while until it encounters a read command, which means it needs something from A. Then control is transferred to A until it wants to "write," whereupon control is returned to B at the point where it left off." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- One-pass versus two-pass (the "tapes" point): "Figure 3 illustrates the essence of separability. Instead of having modules A and B communicating as coroutines with a coroutine linkage between write statements in A and read statements in B such that control is passed back and forth once each time an item is transferred, it is possible without changing anything in A or B except the read and write linkages to have A write all its items on a tape, to rewind the tape and then to have B read all the items from the tape. In this sense, then, the pair of programs A and B can operate as a one-pass or a two-pass processor with only trivial modification." Figure 3's caption: "A. A and B, linked as coroutines, communicate directly. B. A writes its entire output before B reads anything." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Implementation on the Burroughs 220: the standard subroutine call is `STP EXIT` / `BUN ENTRANCE`. "A pair of subroutines becomes a pair of coroutines by adding to each an isolated BUN instruction which we can call its router, and by changing the addresses of the STP, BUN calls as follows: when coroutine A calls coroutine B the call is STP AROUTER / BUN BROUTER. Thus, the router is actually a generalization of the switch of Figure 1. Getting a system of coroutines started is a matter of properly initializing the routers." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Serial, alternating, or parallel execution: "Figure 3 shows that the coroutines of a separable program may be executed alternately or serially. When true parallel processors are available the fact that the coroutines of a separable program may be executed simultaneously becomes even more significant." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- Conway's conclusion ties speed to one pass: "the really fast compilers will have only one pass; that's the result of an analysis of how much extra work must be done by a multi-pass compiler." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The 1958 date and the "assembly program" come from Knuth, not from Conway's paper: Wikipedia states "Melvin Conway coined the term coroutine in 1958 when he applied it to the construction of an assembly program," citing Knuth, TAOCP Vol. 1, 3rd ed. (1997), section 1.4.5, "History and Bibliography", p. 229 (secondary; Knuth's own wording not seen). Source: [Wikipedia, "Coroutine"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coroutine)
- McIlroy (2016) learned the idea from a colleague rather than from the paper: "Pipes came out of an interest in coroutines, which had fascinated me ever since Bob McClure introduced me to Melvin Conway's concept." The interview gives no date for that introduction. Source: [McIlroy interviewed by Rik Farrow, ;login: Spring 2016](https://www.usenix.net/system/files/login/articles/login_spring16_05_mcilroy.pdf)
- A commonly repeated quotation of Conway is inaccurate: de Moura and Ierusalimschy write that Conway "described coroutines as "subroutines who act as the master program"". Conway's printed words are "subroutines all at the same level, each acting as if it were the master program when in fact there is no master program." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy, "Revisiting Coroutines", PUC-Rio MCC 15/04, 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf); [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)

### Inferences
- Conway's own example states the subroutine problem directly: a subroutine whose caller dictates "one-output-for-each-call" must carry a switch recording "the situation at the last call". The coroutine moves that record from a data switch into the program counter (the router), which Conway calls "a generalization of the switch". This is the clearest primary-source statement of the generator's reason for existing.
- "Do not communicate with each other synchronously" means the input and output rates differ (two asterisks in, one character out). That is the same mismatch Jackson later calls a boundary clash (section 4).
- "Separable" is a property of the program's dataflow (one-way, left-to-right item flow), and coroutines are the execution technique that exploits it. Separability is what lets the same modules run as a one-pass compiler (coroutine linkage) or a multi-pass compiler (tapes between them), changing only the read and write linkages. The "each module is written as if it were the main program" idea is the phrase "each acting as if it were the master program".
- Conway's practical motive was memory: one pass avoids tape passes, and separability lets the compiler be cut into segments that fit small machines. The program-structure benefit ("greatly simplify the conception of a program") is stated alongside it.

### Gaps
- The 1958 date and the "assembly program" are in Knuth's history section, which I could not read directly; Conway's paper dates the compiler work to 1961 at Case.
- I did not find a later Conway reminiscence about the coroutine's invention on melconway.com (not searched exhaustively).

## 2. Knuth, TAOCP Volume 1, section 1.4.2 "Coroutines"

### Takeaway
Knuth presents subroutines as the special, asymmetric case of coroutines, which "call on each other" symmetrically. The wording below is confirmed only through secondary quotations, because the book text was not available online.

### Cited Findings
- Location: TAOCP Vol. 1, *Fundamental Algorithms*, 3rd ed. (1997), section 1.4.2 "Coroutines", pp. 193-200; history in section 1.4.5, p. 229 (citation details from Wikipedia's footnotes). The first edition is 1968. Source: [Wikipedia, "Coroutine"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coroutine)
- Opening of 1.4.2 (secondary, quoted in a 2015 Google Groups post): "Subroutines are special cases of more general program components, called coroutines. In contrast to the unsymmetric relationship between a main routine and a subroutine, there is complete symmetry between coroutines, which call on each other." Source: [Ged Byrne, "Why isn't Flow Based Programming More Popular?", flow-based-programming group, 2015](https://groups.google.com/g/flow-based-programming/c/hmeZYztfaQg)
- Also secondary: "It is rather difficult to find short, simple examples of coroutines which illustrate the importance of the idea;" Source: [Softpanorama, "Coroutines"](https://softpanorama.org/Lang/coroutines.shtml)
- Knuth and Ruskey's later paper on coroutine-generated Gray codes refers readers to section 1.4.2 for the definition and describes coroutines as symmetric with respect to caller and callee (search-result summary; not quoted). Source: [Knuth and Ruskey, "Efficient Coroutine Generation of Constrained Gray Sequences", arXiv cs/0404058](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0404058)

### Inferences
- Knuth's framing (symmetry versus the main-routine/subroutine asymmetry) and Conway's ("all at the same level ... there is no master program") say the same thing: the subroutine relationship is a hierarchy, and the coroutine removes it.

### Gaps
- Unverified: Knuth's statement relating coroutines to multipass algorithms (that a process done by n coroutines can often be transformed into an n-pass process, so coroutines and multipass algorithms are closely related). A search summary attributed this to the Byrne post, but the fetched text of that post did not show it, so I give no quotation.
- Unverified: Knuth's history note naming J. Erdwinn and J. Merner as independent co-discoverers, a paper titled "Bilateral Linkage", and a primitive coroutine linkage described as a "programming tip" in an early UNIVAC publication. Softpanorama paraphrases the Erdwinn/Merner and UNIVAC-tip claims, but I found no direct quotation and could not confirm the "Bilateral Linkage" title or the UNIVAC citation. Conway's own footnote names only Joel Erdwinn. Source: [Softpanorama](https://softpanorama.org/Lang/coroutines.shtml); [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The exact text of 1.4.2 needs a library copy of TAOCP Vol. 1 before any quotation goes into the chapter.

## 3. Simula 67 and Dahl/Hoare's semi-coroutine

### Takeaway
Simula's processes ran "in quasi-parallel", and each inactive process kept "a 'reactivation point'" saying where to resume, which is the coroutine's saved control state made explicit. Dahl and Hoare (1972) described a coroutine as a "complete self-contained program whose input and output instructions have been replaced by calls". The asymmetric "semi-coroutine", which returns control to its caller, is attributed to Dahl and Hoare 1972, and it is the form that generators take.

### Cited Findings
- Quasi-parallel processes and the reactivation point (SIMULA I, 1964): "Quasi-parallel execution of processes implied that control would switch from one process to another as the result of special sequencing statements such as "hold". Each temporarily inactive process had a "reactivation point" (represented by a system variable local to the process) which identified the program point where control should resume operations next time it entered the process. With the new storage allocation package quasi-parallel sequencing statements could be allowed at arbitrary program points, e.g. inside procedures called by the processes, since their data stacks could grow and shrink independently (Dahl 1964)." Source: [Nygaard and Dahl, "The Development of the SIMULA Languages", HOPL I, 1978](https://hannemyr.com/cache/knojd_acm78.pdf)
- Detach and resume in Simula 67: "An object would start its life like an instance of a function procedure, invoked by the evaluation of a generating expression. During this phase the object might initialize its own local variables. Then, on passage through the end of the object or as the result of a new basic operation "detach", control would return to the generating expression delivering a reference to the object as the function value. In the former case the object was "terminated" with no further own actions, in the latter case it had become a "detached object" capable of functioning as a "coroutine"." Source: [Nygaard and Dahl 1978](https://hannemyr.com/cache/knojd_acm78.pdf)
- "The basic coroutine call "resume (<object reference>)" would make control leave the active object, leaving behind a reactivation point at the end of the resume statement, and enter the referenced object at its reactivation point." Source: [Nygaard and Dahl 1978](https://hannemyr.com/cache/knojd_acm78.pdf)
- The main program as a coroutine: "an instance of a prefixed block is a detached object by definition". Thereby the main program could function as a coroutine in quasi-parallel with its local process objects. (It was later discovered that this effect could have been achieved in a somewhat more natural way, (Wang and Dahl 1971))." Source: [Nygaard and Dahl 1978](https://hannemyr.com/cache/knojd_acm78.pdf)
- Quasi-parallel sequencing outgrew simulation: "certainly the multistack structure was a big advantage, but quasi-parallel sequencing had many applications independent of the concept of simulated time." Source: [Nygaard and Dahl 1978](https://hannemyr.com/cache/knojd_acm78.pdf)
- Earlier prior art acknowledged: "SOL (Knuth and McNeley 1964a,b) came to our attention too late (July 1964) to have an effect on our design, but we were impressed with its beauty and realized that others before us had had the idea of quasi-parallel processes in an ALGOL-like setting." Source: [Nygaard and Dahl 1978](https://hannemyr.com/cache/knojd_acm78.pdf)
- Why Simula needed a new storage scheme (Dahl's retrospective): "We realised that in order to profit from block structure in simulation models it would be necessary to break out of the strict LIFO regime of block instances in Algol. Thus, a new storage management system was developed based on a list structured free store". Processes "could range from record-like data structures to block structured programs executing in a coroutine-like fashion". Source: [Dahl, "The Birth of Object Orientation: the Simula Languages", 2001](https://www.mn.uio.no/ifi/english/about/ole-johan-dahl/bibliography/the-birth-of-object-orientation-the-simula-languages.pdf)
- Dahl and Hoare, "Hierarchical Program Structures" (in Dahl, Dijkstra, Hoare, *Structured Programming*, Academic Press, 1972), on coroutines (secondary, quoted in a blog summary): "This suggests that a single coroutine may profitably be regarded as a complete self-contained program whose input and output instructions have been replaced by calls upon those coroutines to produce and consume the data." The blog's surrounding paraphrase: the chapter's example is a two-pass compiler whose first pass emits messages consumed by the second, rearranged so the whole translation runs in one pass with messages passed piecewise. Source: [Adrian Colyer, "Hierarchical Program Structures", The Morning Paper, 2016](https://blog.acolyer.org/2016/10/18/hierarchical-program-structures/)
- Semi-coroutines: "Asymmetric coroutine mechanisms (more commonly denoted as semi-symmetric or semi coroutines [Dahl et al. 1972]) provide two control-transfer operations: one for invoking a coroutine and one for suspending it, the latter returning control to the coroutine invoker. While symmetric coroutines operate at the same hierarchical level, an asymmetric coroutine can be regarded as subordinate to its caller, the relationship between them being somewhat similar to that between a called and a calling routine." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- The same paper ties the asymmetric form to generators: "coroutine mechanisms intended for implementing constructs that produce sequences of values typically provide asymmetric coroutines. Examples of this type of construct are iterators [Liskov et al. 1977; Murer et al. 1996] and generators [Griswold and Griswold 1983; Schemenauer et al. 2001]. The general-purpose coroutine mechanisms implemented by Simula and BCPL provide both types of control transfer." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- Samet (1985, search-result summary, not read in full) reports that Dahl and Hoare use "call-detach" for the master-slave relationship and call the slave a "semicoroutine", and that Wang and Dahl term such arrangements "semi-symmetric". Source: [Samet, Information Processing Letters, 1985](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/hjs/pubs/SametIPL85.pdf)

### Inferences
- Simula's "reactivation point (represented by a system variable local to the process)" is the subroutine's missing piece named outright: per-process storage of where to continue. That storage, plus a separate data stack per process, is what the LIFO call stack could not provide.
- The Dahl/Hoare sentence about a coroutine as a "complete self-contained program whose input and output instructions have been replaced by calls" restates Conway's "autonomous program which communicates ... as if they were input or output subroutines", and anticipates Jackson's inversion (section 4).
- The semi-coroutine is the earliest named asymmetric form I found in the sources: invoked by a caller, suspends back to that caller. Whether it is the *origin* of the generator-like form is supported by attribution (de Moura and Ierusalimschy credit "Dahl et al. 1972") but not by a primary quotation of the 1972 definition.

### Gaps
- I could not read "Hierarchical Program Structures" itself, so the semi-coroutine definition and the exact "call"/"detach" semantics are not quoted from the primary text.
- Earlier asymmetric forms (for example in SOL, 1964, or in assembly practice) were not checked.

## 4. Jackson: structure clash and program inversion

### Takeaway
Jackson's remedy for a structure clash is the coroutine design described as a design method: write two simple programs joined by an intermediate sequential stream, then "invert" one into a subroutine that the other calls per record. Jackson himself says inversion "is closely related to the Simula [Dahl 70] concept of semi-coroutines", and Hoare (1977, as summarized by Daniel Jackson in 2009) notes that COBOL "did not provide coroutines", which is why inversion existed as a transformation.

### Cited Findings
- Source note: Michael A. Jackson, "JSP in Perspective", sd&m Pioneers' Conference, Bonn, June 2001. The author's own copy (mcs.open.ac.uk/mj665/JSPPers1.pdf) failed to load (TLS error); quotations are from an unofficial copy whose page footer reads "JSPPers1.doc 11/04/01". Source: [Jackson 2001, unofficial copy on studfile.net](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- Three clashes: "In an interleaving clash, data groups that occur sequentially in one structure correspond functionally to groups that are interleaved in another structure. ... In an ordering clash, corresponding data item instances are differently ordered in two structures. ... In a boundary clash, two structures have corresponding elements occurring in the same order, but the elements are differently grouped in the two structures. The boundaries of the two groupings are not synchronised." Source: [Jackson 2001](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- Examples of boundary clashes: days grouped by months versus weeks; book lines grouped by paragraphs versus pages; variable-length records stored in fixed 512-byte blocks ("the boundaries of the records can not be synchronised with the boundaries of the blocks"). Source: [Jackson 2001](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- The decomposition: "The JSP technique for dealing with a structure clash is to decompose the original program into two or more programs communicating by intermediate data structures. A boundary clash, for example, requires a decomposition into two programs communicating by an intermediate sequential stream. The structure of the intermediate stream is based on the 'highest common factor' of the two clashing structures." Source: [Jackson 2001](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- Inversion: "The underlying idea of program inversion is that reading and writing sequential files on tape is only a specialised version of a more general form of communication. In the general form programs communicate by producing and consuming sequential streams of records, each stream being either unbuffered or buffered according to any of several possible regimes. The choice of buffering regime is, to a large extent, independent of the design of the communicating programs. But it is not independent of their scheduling." Source: [Jackson 2001](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- Inverting either side: "In the upper diagram two 'main' programs, P and Q, communicate by writing and reading an intermediate tape file F. First P is run to completion; then F is rewound; then Q is run to completion. In the lower left diagram the intermediate tape file has been eliminated: the 'main' program Q has been [inverted with respect to F]. This inversion converts Q into a subroutine Q′, invoked by P whenever P requires to produce a record of F. Q′ functions as a 'consume next F record' procedure. Similarly, in the lower right diagram the 'main' program P has been [inverted]. This inversion converts P into a subroutine P′, invoked by Q whenever Q requires to consume a record of F. P′ functions as a 'produce next F record' procedure. Both inversions interleave execution of P and Q as tightly as possible: each F record is consumed as soon as it has been produced." (The bracketed words are italic labels the text extraction split off.) Source: [Jackson 2001](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- The design consequence: "Inversion also has an important effect on the program designer's view of a sequential program. The 'main program' P, the subroutine 'P inverted with respect to F', and the subroutine 'P inverted with respect to E', are seen as identical at the design level." And: "procedures with internal state can be designed as if they were main programs processing sequential message streams; and JSP design becomes applicable to all kinds of interactive systems, and even to interrupt handlers." Source: [Jackson 2001](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- Jackson's own link to coroutines and pipes: "The idea of program inversion is closely related to the Simula [Dahl 70] concept of semi-coroutines, and, of course, to the later Unix notion of pipes as a flexible implementation of sequential files." Source: [Jackson 2001](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- Efficiency: inversion "eliminates the storage space and device costs of the intermediate tape file. It eliminates the time required by the device to write and read each record of F, and also the 'rewind time' to reposition the newly written file for reading". Source: [Jackson 2001](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- Daniel Jackson's 2009 introduction to Hoare's 1977 report: "For a boundary clash, an intermediate stream is created, and the program is either run in two phases, or two programs are run in parallel with a stream between them. To eliminate the stream, one program can be run as a coroutine of the other. COBOL, of course, did not provide coroutines, so JSP offered a program transformation (automated by the JSP-COBOL tool) that converted stream reads and writes into calls, and let the programmer choose whether the reader or writer had initiative without changing to the code." Source: [C.A.R. Hoare, "The Michael Jackson Design Technique: A study of the theory with applications" (1977), edited and introduced by Daniel Jackson, 2009](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/sdg/pubs/2009/hoare-jsp-3-29-09.pdf)
- Same introduction, on the tangled alternative: "many of the techniques used at the time encouraged the use of flags and complex control flow, resulting in a bug-ridden program with little semblance of structure. JSP identified the essential difficulties and prescribed simple and systematic remedies for them: readahead for recognition difficulties; interposed streams for structure clashes; inversion for overcoming lack of multithreading, etc." Source: [Hoare 1977, ed. D. Jackson 2009](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/sdg/pubs/2009/hoare-jsp-3-29-09.pdf)
- Hoare (1977) treats a clash as a memory problem: a structure clash occurs "when it proves impossible to perform an output instruction until a whole group of input instructions has been completed. If there is enough room in main store to hold a sufficiently detailed summary of the input data, then the structure clash causes no problem, and can be ignored. If there is not enough room, other measures must be adopted: for example, the use of an intermediate data file, sorting, program inversion, etc." Source: [Hoare 1977, ed. D. Jackson 2009](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/sdg/pubs/2009/hoare-jsp-3-29-09.pdf)
- JSD (Jackson's later method) kept processes whose states were "frozen in a database between uses". Source: [Hoare 1977, ed. D. Jackson 2009](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/sdg/pubs/2009/hoare-jsp-3-29-09.pdf)

### Inferences
- Jackson's two-program decomposition is Conway's separable program, and inversion is Conway's coroutine linkage implemented by a source transformation in a language without coroutines. "Procedures with internal state can be designed as if they were main programs" is the same claim as Conway's "each acting as if it were the master program".
- The "flags and complex control flow" that Daniel Jackson describes as the alternative are Conway's "absurd arrangement of switches".
- Conway's asterisk squasher is a small boundary clash (pairs of input characters regrouped into single output characters).

### Gaps
- I did not read *Principles of Program Design* (Academic Press, 1975) itself. The mechanics of inversion as the book presents them (a saved state variable and a multiway jump on entry that resumes at the last read or write) are widely described but I found no quotable source, so they are unverified here.
- The 2001 text's sentence beginning "Program decomposition into sequential processes communicating by a coroutine-like" was cut off at the end of the available preview.

## 5. McIlroy: garden hose, coroutines, and pipes

### Takeaway
McIlroy says pipes "came out of an interest in coroutines", since "Coroutine connections look very much like I/O", and Ritchie calls the pipeline "merely a specific form of coroutine".

### Cited Findings
- The 1964 memo (summary page, item 1, dated "Oct. 11 1964"): "We should have some ways of coupling programs like garden hose--screw in another segment when it becomes necessary" (the fetched page text ended the quotation there; check the rest of the sentence against a scan of the memo before quoting further). Source: [McIlroy memo summary, thocp](https://thocp.callapple.org/biographies/papers/pipeline_mcIllroy.htm)
- McIlroy (2016) on the origin of pipes: "Pipes came out of an interest in coroutines, which had fascinated me ever since Bob McClure introduced me to Melvin Conway's concept [6]. Coroutine connections look very much like I/O. This led me to write (in a 1964 Multics document) about screwing processes together like garden hose." Source: [McIlroy, interviewed by Rik Farrow, ;login: 41(1), Spring 2016](https://www.usenix.net/system/files/login/articles/login_spring16_05_mcilroy.pdf)
- McIlroy (2016) on what pipes add over files: "I totally failed to perceive the fact that connecting processes via pipes is logically more powerful than via stored serial files. You can replace an intermediate file with a pipe, but not always vice versa. An interactive session, such as dc|speak (a talking desk calculator), won't work if it has to treasure up all the output of dc before running speak. Bob Morris pointed this out on the very day pipes first worked." Source: [McIlroy 2016](https://www.usenix.net/system/files/login/articles/login_spring16_05_mcilroy.pdf)
- Ritchie, "The Evolution of the Unix Time-sharing System" (presented 1979, published in the AT&T Bell Laboratories Technical Journal, 1984): "One of the most widely admired contributions of Unix to the culture of operating systems and command languages is the pipe, as used in a pipeline of commands. Of course, the fundamental idea was by no means new; the pipeline is merely a specific form of coroutine. Even the implementation was not unprecedented, although we didn't know it at the time; the 'communication files' of the Dartmouth Time-Sharing System [10] did very nearly what Unix pipes do, though they seem not to have been exploited so fully." Source: [Ritchie 1979/1984](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse550/20au/papers/CSE550.ritchie84evolution.pdf)
- "Pipes appeared in Unix in 1972, well after the PDP-11 version of the system was in operation, at the suggestion (or perhaps insistence) of M. D. McIlroy, a long-time advocate of the non-hierarchical control flow that characterizes coroutines." Source: [Ritchie 1979/1984](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse550/20au/papers/CSE550.ritchie84evolution.pdf)
- On Multics stream splicing: "Not only were coroutines well-known already, but their embodiment as Multics spliceable IO modules required that the modules be specially coded in such a way that they could be used for no other purpose. The genius of the Unix pipeline is precisely that it is constructed from the very same commands used constantly in simplex fashion." Source: [Ritchie 1979/1984](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse550/20au/papers/CSE550.ritchie84evolution.pdf)

### Inferences
- Ritchie's "non-hierarchical control flow that characterizes coroutines" is Knuth's symmetry and Conway's "no master program" again. A pipe gives each stage its own process, so each stage keeps its control state in its own stack and program counter; the operating system provides what the language lacked.
- McIlroy's dc|speak point matches Conway's Figure 3 in one direction: coroutine (pipe) linkage and tape (file) linkage are interchangeable for a batch, but only the coroutine linkage works when the consumer must respond before the producer has finished.

### Gaps
- Date conflict: Ritchie says pipes appeared in 1972; the request (and common accounts) give 1973 (Third Edition Unix). I did not resolve this.
- I found no evidence that a McIlroy paper titled "Coroutines: semantics in search of a syntax" (1968) exists; a direct search returned nothing. Treat it as unverified until a bibliography or archive shows it.
- The full memo text and its scan (on the Bell Labs/Nokia archive) were blocked (HTTP 403).

## 6. Other early treatments

### Takeaway
Through the 1970s coroutines were a recognized research topic and appeared in a few languages (Simula, BCPL, Modula-2, and Icon's generators). Marlin's 1980 thesis gave the definition later authors cite: local data persist between calls, and execution resumes where it left off.

### Cited Findings
- Marlin's characterization (via de Moura and Ierusalimschy, who call the thesis "widely acknowledged as a reference for this mechanism"): "the values of data local to a coroutine persist between successive calls"; "the execution of a coroutine is suspended as control leaves it, only to carry on where it left off when control re-enters the coroutine at some later stage". Citation: Christopher D. Marlin, *Coroutines: A Programming Methodology, a Language Design and an Implementation*, Lecture Notes in Computer Science 95, Springer, 1980 (secondary quotation). Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- Range of uses in the two decades after Conway: "The aptness of coroutines to express several useful control behaviors was widely explored during the next twenty years in several different contexts, including simulation, artificial intelligence, concurrent programming, text processing, and various kinds of data-structure manipulation [Knuth 1968; Marlin 1980; Pauli and Soffa 1980]." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- Wang and Dahl, "Coroutine Sequencing in a Block Structured Environment", BIT 11 (1971): 425-449 (citation from the Simula HOPL paper's references). Nygaard and Dahl say it showed a "more natural way" for the main program to act as a coroutine. Source: [Nygaard and Dahl 1978](https://hannemyr.com/cache/knojd_acm78.pdf)
- Moody and Richards, "A coroutine mechanism for BCPL", *Software: Practice and Experience* 10(10), 1980, pp. 765-771 (bibliographic details from a search-result summary of the Crossref record; abstract not read). Source: [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002%2FSPE.4380101002)
- Languages that did adopt coroutines: "designers of general-purpose languages have disregarded the convenience of providing a programmer with this powerful control construct, with rare exceptions such as Simula [Birtwistle et al. 1980], BCPL [Moody and Richards 1980], Modula-2 [Wirth 1985], and Icon [Griswold and Griswold 1983]." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- Icon's generators as a constrained coroutine: "Icon's goal-directed evaluation of expressions [Griswold and Griswold 1983] is an interesting language paradigm where backtracking is supported by another constrained form of coroutines, named generators -- expressions that may produce multiple values. ... Icon also supports user-defined generators, implemented by procedures that suspend instead of returning." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- CLU iterators (1977) as a restricted coroutine: "Because a CLU iterator preserves state between successive calls, they described it as a coroutine ... However, CLU iterators are confined within a for loop that can invoke exactly one iterator." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- Python's generator in this taxonomy: "In Python [Schemenauer et al. 2001], a function that contains an yield statement is called a generator function. When called, this function returns an object that can be resumed at any point in a program, so it behaves as an asymmetric coroutine. Despite constituting a first-class object, a Python generator is not a stackful construct; it can only suspend its execution when its control stack is at the same level that it was at creation time." (Commentary from after the period; useful for placing the generator.) Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- Modula-2 "as defined by Wirth implements coroutines as part of the standard SYSTEM library" (Wikipedia, used as a lead). Source: [Wikipedia, "Coroutine"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coroutine)

### Inferences
- The generator lineage visible in these sources: Conway's symmetric coroutine (1963), Simula's detach/resume (1967) and the Dahl/Hoare semi-coroutine (1972), CLU iterators (1977), Icon generators (late 1970s to 1983), then Python. Each later form gives up generality (symmetry, first-class status, or stackfulness) for a simpler control discipline.

### Gaps
- I did not read Marlin 1980, Wang and Dahl 1971, or Moody and Richards 1980 directly.
- Pre-Conway assembly-language coroutine linkage (the UNIVAC "programming tip" Knuth reportedly mentions) is unverified.

## 7. The shared statement of the problem: testing the hypothesis

### Takeaway
The sources support the hypothesis, often in nearly its words. Conway's switch that records "the situation at the last call", Simula's "reactivation point", Marlin's "carry on where it left off", Dahl/Hoare's "complete self-contained program", and Jackson's "procedures with internal state can be designed as if they were main programs" all describe a routine that, under call and return, must store its position in data, and that, as a coroutine, keeps its position in its own control flow.

### Cited Findings
- Giving up control state forces a switch (state in data): "requires the introduction of a switch which bifurcates the subroutine and selects the part to be used at each call depending on the situation at the last call. The reason for the switch is that each call of the subroutine must result in output of exactly one character." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The coroutine keeps control state instead: "accomplishes the switching job implicitly by use of the subroutine calling sequence ... control is returned to B at the point where it left off." Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf)
- The saved position, named: "Each temporarily inactive process had a "reactivation point" ... which identified the program point where control should resume operations next time it entered the process." Source: [Nygaard and Dahl 1978](https://hannemyr.com/cache/knojd_acm78.pdf)
- Each side written as a main program: "each acting as if it were the master program when in fact there is no master program" Source: [Conway 1963](https://www.melconway.com/Home/pdf/compiler.pdf); "a single coroutine may profitably be regarded as a complete self-contained program whose input and output instructions have been replaced by calls" (secondary) Source: [Colyer 2016 quoting Dahl and Hoare 1972](https://blog.acolyer.org/2016/10/18/hierarchical-program-structures/); "procedures with internal state can be designed as if they were main programs processing sequential message streams" Source: [Jackson 2001](https://studfile.net/preview/429617/)
- Persistence of local data and position: "the values of data local to a coroutine persist between successive calls"; "the execution of a coroutine is suspended as control leaves it, only to carry on where it left off" (secondary) Source: [Marlin 1980 via de Moura and Ierusalimschy](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- The rewritten-as-flags alternative: "techniques used at the time encouraged the use of flags and complex control flow, resulting in a bug-ridden program with little semblance of structure" Source: [D. Jackson 2009, introducing Hoare 1977](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/sdg/pubs/2009/hoare-jsp-3-29-09.pdf)
- The asymmetry is the root: "In contrast to the unsymmetric relationship between a main routine and a subroutine, there is complete symmetry between coroutines" (secondary) Source: [Knuth via Byrne 2015](https://groups.google.com/g/flow-based-programming/c/hmeZYztfaQg); "the non-hierarchical control flow that characterizes coroutines" Source: [Ritchie 1979/1984](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse550/20au/papers/CSE550.ritchie84evolution.pdf)

### Inferences
- Verdict: supported, with three refinements the sources add.
  1. The trigger is a rate or grouping mismatch between the two routines (Conway: modules that "do not communicate with each other synchronously"; Jackson: a structure clash). When one call produces exactly one item for every item consumed, call and return is enough; the switch appears only when the routines' natural loops do not line up.
  2. Which routine loses its control state is a choice. Jackson's P can be inverted into a "produce next F record" procedure or Q into a "consume next F record" procedure, and JSP-COBOL let the programmer choose "whether the reader or writer had initiative without changing [the] code". Call and return forces one of the two to be the subordinate; the coroutine lets neither be.
  3. The historical alternative to a coroutine was usually not a hand-written state machine but an intermediate tape: run the producer to completion, rewind, run the consumer (Conway's two-pass configuration, Jackson's intermediate file). Both programs then keep their control state, at the price of storage and time and of losing interactivity (McIlroy's dc|speak). The coroutine keeps each program's control state *and* eliminates the tape. So the problem had two bad solutions under call and return: rewrite one routine around a switch, or serialize the routines through a file.
- A suggested one-sentence statement grounded in these sources: two routines that each have a natural loop over a stream cannot both be written as loops under call and return, because the subroutine forgets its place on every return; the coroutine lets each keep its place, so each is written as if it were the main program.

### Gaps
- None of the period sources uses the phrase "state machine" for the switch-based rewrite; that wording is a modern gloss (Conway says "switch" and "router", Jackson's commentators say "flags").

## 8. Why coroutines receded from mainstream languages

### Takeaway
The period sources point to the single LIFO stack of ALGOL-family languages (Simula had to "break out of the strict LIFO regime" with a new storage manager). Later commentary adds the lack of a precise definition, the rise of first-class continuations as the research construct, and threads as the standard concurrency construct.

### Cited Findings
- Stack discipline: "We realised that in order to profit from block structure in simulation models it would be necessary to break out of the strict LIFO regime of block instances in Algol. Thus, a new storage management system was developed based on a list structured free store". Source: [Dahl 2001](https://www.mn.uio.no/ifi/english/about/ole-johan-dahl/bibliography/the-birth-of-object-orientation-the-simula-languages.pdf)
- Simula's quasi-parallel suspension at arbitrary points required per-process stacks: suspension was allowed "inside procedures called by the processes, since their data stacks could grow and shrink independently". Source: [Nygaard and Dahl 1978](https://hannemyr.com/cache/knojd_acm78.pdf)
- Dahl's own verdict: "The coroutine-like sequencing of Simula has not caught on as a general purpose programming tool. A natural development, however, would have been objects as concurrent processes, e.g. as in COM." Source: [Dahl 2001](https://www.mn.uio.no/ifi/english/about/ole-johan-dahl/bibliography/the-birth-of-object-orientation-the-simula-languages.pdf)
- No precise definition: "The absence of coroutine facilities in mainstream languages can be partly attributed to the lacking of an uniform view of this concept, which was never precisely defined." Also: "Simula's mechanism, a truly complex implementation of coroutines, was practically adopted as a reference for a general-purpose coroutine mechanism and greatly contributed to the common misconception that symmetric and asymmetric coroutines have no equivalent power." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- Continuations: "the introduction of first-class continuations also greatly contributed to the virtual end of research interest in coroutines as a general control abstraction." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- Threads: "Another significant reason for the absence of coroutines in modern languages is the current adoption of multithreading as a de facto standard for concurrent programming." Source: [de Moura and Ierusalimschy 2004](https://www.inf.puc-rio.br/~roberto/docs/MCC15-04.pdf)
- Operating-system processes took over the job: Ritchie notes that coroutines "were well-known already" and that the Unix pipeline is "merely a specific form of coroutine" built from ordinary commands. Source: [Ritchie 1979/1984](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse550/20au/papers/CSE550.ritchie84evolution.pdf)
- Wikipedia (lead only) attributes the absence in C and its descendants to "the limitations of stack-based subroutine implementation" and names threads as the mainstream alternative. Source: [Wikipedia, "Coroutine"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coroutine)

### Inferences
- The disappearance and the generator's later form share a cause. A full coroutine needs its own stack, which a single-stack language does not have; a generator that suspends only in its own body (de Moura and Ierusalimschy's "not a stackful construct") needs one saved frame, which fits a stack-based implementation. That restriction is a reasonable explanation for why the generator was the form that returned first, though no period source states it.
- Pipes and processes met the separable-program need at the operating-system level from 1972-73 on, which reduced pressure to put coroutines into languages; this is an inference from Ritchie and McIlroy, not a claim either makes.

### Gaps
- I found no period source (before 1980) that discusses why language designers declined coroutines; the explanations above are from 2001 and 2004.
