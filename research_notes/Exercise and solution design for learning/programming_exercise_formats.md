# Programming exercise formats in computing-education research: what each teaches, the evidence, and what a static book can use

Scope note.
These notes cover the exercise formats computing-education research (CER) has studied, the evidence for each, the frameworks that sequence them, and what each one needs from its medium.
The reader of *Thinking in Python* already programs, so every finding below names its sample (CS1 novice, non-major, second-year, upper-division) and flags where the evidence runs out before it reaches intermediate learners.
A recurring caveat: almost all of the controlled evidence is on CS1 novices, and the one direct test on upper-division students (Weinman 2022, chapter 3) found no learning gain from the novice-proven format.

Medium legend used throughout.
"Paper" means the format works in print, EPUB, or a static web page with a solutions folder.
"Runtime" means the format needs the reader to run code (which a static site cannot supply, but a reader with Python installed can).
"Tool" means it needs an interactive widget (drag-and-drop, autograder) and so suits only a live site, if at all.

## Key question 1: Parsons problems

### Takeaway
Parsons problems (reorder scrambled correct code) give CS1 novices learning gains equal to writing the code, in roughly half to 70% of the time; the two randomized comparisons that exist are both on undergraduate Python novices.
For learners who already program, the one direct study found no learning gain and that the puzzles could be "guessed through" from variable names, and the review literature names the expertise-reversal effect as the open risk.
They originated as paper exam questions and work on paper; the adaptive and faded variants need a tool.

### Cited Findings
- The ITiCSE 2022 working group report (Ericson, Denny, Prather, Duran, Hellas, Leinonen, Miller, Morrison, Pearce, Rodger; ITiCSE-WGR 2022, pp. 191-234, DOI 10.1145/3571785.3574127) reviewed 141 papers; median study size 102 participants; 52.9% of studies were tertiary CS1/CS2, 29% in Python, 19% in Java | [Parsons Problems and Beyond (Aalto PDF)](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Definition from the report: "Parsons problems are tasks where the user needs to construct a solution by placing fragments into a correct order"; expected time on task "around a minute to less than 10 minutes" | [same report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Variants defined in the report: two-dimensional (order plus indentation, first proposed in js-parsons by Ihantola and Karavirta 2011, "inspired by Python"); distractors ("extra fragments that are not needed in a correct solution"); paired distractors (shown beside their correct line; "generally recommended, as it can be overwhelming for students when a large number of distractors are randomly intermixed with correct options"); faded Parsons (Weinman, Fox, Hearst: "some incomplete blocks where the student needs to type missing code within the block"); adaptive (on request "the system can remove a distractor or combine fragments"); design-level Parsons (blocks are design steps, not code); Parsons as optional fallback inside a write-code problem; paper-based (Denny 2008 exam questions, bubble sheets, paper strips). "Micro" and "hybrid" Parsons are not defined in the report | [same report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Learning versus writing: "Only two randomized controlled studies have been done comparing learning gains from solving Parsons problems versus writing the equivalent code [59, 62]. These were both conducted with undergraduate students in Python." These are Ericson, Margulieux, Rick (Koli Calling 2017) and Ericson, Foley, Rick (ICER 2018); the report describes them as significantly faster "with no negative effects on learning" | [same report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Weinman's dissertation summarizes Ericson et al. 2017 as Parsons with worked examples giving "similar learning gains to writing or fixing code in about 70% of the time"; the report's blocks-based analogue (Zhi et al.) is "nearly half of total problem solving time" | [Weinman 2022, UCB/EECS-2022-257](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html); [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Ericson's own summary slides (Raspberry Pi seminar, January 2024): "Parsons problems are significantly faster than fixing and writing code. Equivalent learning gains from pretest to posttest"; "People are nearly twice as likely to solve adaptive Parsons"; "Most students (75+%) find them useful"; "Some would rather write the equivalent code"; "Parsons problems are not faster to solve if the solution is unusual" | [Ericson and Hou, January 2024 seminar slides](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2024/01/January-2024-seminar-slides.pdf)
- Null speed result: Haynes and Ericson (CHI 2021) found a Parsons problem whose solution did not match the common student solution "was not significantly faster to solve than writing the equivalent code," and it raised cognitive load | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Population split: Fabic et al. compared activity types in a mobile Python tutor; "Parsons problems were beneficial for novice students, but ... fix and write code activities were more beneficial for more advanced students" | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- The report's open question for non-novices: "further studies should look into to what extent students' prior programming background influences the utility of Parsons problems, and also into at what point can Parsons problems become harmful (e.g. due to the expertise reversal effect)"; Dale Parsons herself, interviewed in the appendix: "They didn't really take off with my more advanced students" | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Upper-division direct test (Weinman 2022, chapter 3): 84 UC Berkeley students past CS2 in an interview-prep workshop, Parsons versus code editor on DFS, topological sort, tries, Huffman coding (11-28 line problems). Parsons solved 59-74% more problems per session (p<.01) but "I did not observe any changes in learning gains between Weeks 1 and 4," and participants "could deduce which statements belonged to which functions" from variable names ("edge pieces in a jigsaw puzzle"). Author's verdict: "Parsons Problems were not particularly effective practice for upper division students." Attrition was severe (84 to 19) | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Transfer null (Weinman 2022, chapter 4, n=13 CS1): the three students who solved a multiple-recursion problem as a standard Parsons all failed to write the same program as code about 30 minutes later; "Solving standard Parsons Problems don't immediately lead to an ability to write code for the same problem" | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Faded Parsons for patterns (Weinman, Fox, Hearst, CHI 2021; dissertation chapter 5): CS1, 237 consenting students, extra-credit exercises on seven patterns (premature return, enumerate, last/current, stateful tree traversal, multiple-recursion game simulation, stateful generators, mixins with super). Faded Parsons versus code writing: pattern exposure 97.2% vs 39.2% and active exposure 92.4% vs 4.4% (study 1), 87.8% vs 64.9% and 70.9% vs 36.9% (study 2), p<.001; pattern acquisition 55.3% vs 20.7% (study 1) and 43.3% vs 33.7% (study 2, p<.01); code-writing transfer not significantly different (27.7% vs 28.7% in study 2). "Open-ended Code Writing exercises... often do not expose students to the intended patterns even when a well-documented instructor solution is provided after the fact." One pattern (stateful generators) reversed in favor of writing | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Blanking rules used for faded Parsons: 0% to 75% of code blanked (32% average); required function arguments and top-level control-flow tokens never blanked; if a variable's assignment is blanked, every reference is blanked too | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Parsons as optional scaffold inside a write-code problem (Hou, Ericson, Wang, ICER 2022, DOI 10.1145/3501385.3543977): think-aloud with 11 CS1 Python students, then a classroom study with 81 students in a second Python course. Scaffolded group finished practice faster (median 19.42 vs 23.94 min, U=504.5, p=0.003, CLES=0.31, "a 19% reduction") but "there was no significant learning gain in either condition from pretest to posttest" (ceiling effect; pretest write-code median 18.18/20). Students opened the scaffold while debugging 91% of the time; 36% found the Parsons solution hard because "I was thinking differently on how to approach it" | [Hou, Ericson, Wang ICER 2022 (NSF PAR)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10432947)
- Follow-up with a new topic (Hou, Ericson, Wang, ITiCSE 2023 poster, DOI 10.1145/3587103.3594182): 89 undergraduates, writing Python classes before the course covered them. Parsons-help group: practice score 20.98 vs 10.83 (p=0.003, f²=0.11), efficiency 1.34 vs 0.81 points/min (p=0.041), posttest 15.15 vs 11.51 of 40, p=0.349 (null) | [Hou et al. ITiCSE 2023](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10510493)
- Experienced learners new to Python (Haynes-Magyar, arXiv 2512.22407, n=9, 1-13 years of prior programming): "Learners selectively used Faded Parsons problems for syntax/structure and Pseudocode Parsons problems for high-level reasoning"; costs were drag-and-drop time and "the confusion experienced when a solution diverges from a learners' mental model"; "When the scaffolding's solution diverges from the learner's mental model, the scaffolding may increase rather than decrease cognitive load." A 13-year veteran actively removed the correct block because it did not match his strategy. No learning measure | [Haynes-Magyar 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22407)
- Prior experience and affect, as cited by the above: novices with prior experience "can have strong adverse reactions to Parsons problems"; learners with high self-efficacy "prefer to write code from scratch and use Parsons scaffolding sparingly" (Hou et al., Koli Calling 2023) | [Haynes-Magyar 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22407)
- Analogue in proofs (Poulsen, Gertner, Cosman, West, Herman, arXiv 2211.09609; discrete-math students, n=332 after exclusions): Proof Blocks (reorder proof lines) vs writing proofs vs hybrid gave equal gains (Kruskal-Wallis χ²=0.54, p=0.76) in 11.1 vs 43.4 vs 32.1 minutes (Written minus Blocks d=2.09); "students in the early phases of learning ... are able to learn just as much ... but in far less time on task" | [Poulsen et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.09609)
- Distractors, the only with/without learning comparison: Harms, Chen, Kelleher (ICER 2016, young blocks-based novices): "We found no evidence that distractors promote learning. Further, distractors increased learners' cognitive load, decreased their success at completing code puzzles by 26%, and also increased their time on task by 14%"; transfer did not differ (p=.16) | [Harms 2016 slides](https://kharms.infosci.cornell.edu/downloads/harmsk-icer-2016-slides.pdf); [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Distractors in a summative exam (Smith and Zilles, SIGCSE 2023, N=494 intro Python): time rose 23-37% (p<.001 to <.01), scores fell slightly (one of three significant), discrimination barely changed; "Distractors should not be used in summative assessments because they significantly increase the problem's completion time without a significant increase in problem discrimination" | [Smith and Zilles 2023](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/smith_parsons_sigcse23.pdf)
- Distractors in Proof Blocks (Poulsen et al., arXiv 2311.00792, n=201): paired distractors with misconception feedback gave +9.0 points over off-topic control and +3.6 over no-distractors, neither significant (p=0.114, p=0.348), at about twice the time (19.3 vs 10.5 min); "the results of this study are inconclusive" | [Poulsen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.00792)
- Dale Parsons on distractors (working group appendix): "Nobody could ever prove that they were useful" | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Paper and exam use: Denny, Luxton-Reilly, Simon (ICER 2008, DOI 10.1145/1404520.1404532) "had students solve problems on paper by writing in full each line that they selected from the set of options"; performance "was highly correlated with performance on code writing tasks"; marks "make clear what students don't know ... much less ambiguously than marks from a code writing problem"; recommendations: pair distractors with their correct alternatives, and let braces and indentation hint structure | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf); [Denny et al. 2008 record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F1404520.1404532)
- Parsons herself distinguished "Parsons problem = exam question" from "puzzle = electronic rearranging" and used them "primarily... post-lab and for assessments" | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- What Parsons problems measure: in Lopez et al. 2008 the Parsons puzzle scored 83% (easiest category) and sat *below* iterative tracing in the skill hierarchy; the authors suspected "shallow heuristics" such as putting `return` last, and that provided braces "gave away too much" | [Lopez, Whalley, Robbins, Lister ICER 2008](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/10806/1/2008001530.pdf)
- Two-dimensional (indentation) Parsons are harder than one-dimensional (Ihantola and Karavirta 2010/2011, as cited by Smith and Zilles) | [Smith and Zilles 2023](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/smith_parsons_sigcse23.pdf)

### Inferences
- For this book's readers the efficiency argument for classic Parsons collapses: it rests on novices needing less time to reach the same gain, and the one upper-division study found neither a gain nor a real challenge. A plain reorder-the-lines puzzle in a design-patterns chapter is likely to be solvable from data-flow cues without engaging the design idea.
- The faded Parsons result is the one Parsons finding that targets what this book teaches (idioms and patterns rather than syntax) and it shows the mechanism: open-ended "write it" exercises let readers solve the problem without ever using the idiom, so an exercise meant to teach an idiom must constrain the solution shape. On paper, the nearest equivalent is a skeleton with blanks (a completion problem; see question 2), since drag-and-drop is a tool feature but typing into blanks is not.
- Distractors are the weakest part of the Parsons literature and every controlled test is null or negative on learning; a book should not add "wrong line" distractors to a reorder puzzle expecting a learning benefit. If the aim is to teach recognition of a wrong idiom, a refute or critique question (question 6) is the format with that purpose.
- A Parsons puzzle whose canonical solution differs from the reader's own approach raises load rather than lowering it; in a book with one printed solution per exercise, this argues for exercises whose solution shape is constrained by the chapter (a named pattern) so the mismatch cannot arise.
- Parsons problems work on paper (Denny 2008's exam format: list the lines in the order you would use them, write out the selected lines) and in a static page as a numbered list of shuffled lines with the answer in the solutions folder. Adaptive, intra-problem help, and faded-with-autograding need a tool.

### Gaps
- Sample sizes and effect sizes for the two Ericson RCTs (Koli 2017, ICER 2018) do not appear in any document I could read; the review reports only "significantly faster, no negative effects on learning."
- No study tests Parsons problems whose blocks are design decisions (the report's "design-level Parsons") for learning outcomes.
- No study of Parsons problems in a print or static-web self-study setting without an autograder.

## Key question 2: Faded worked examples and completion problems

### Takeaway
Van Merriënboer's completion strategy (1990: high-school COMAL, 28 vs 29 students, 10 lessons) found completion learners built programs better, dropped out less, and used language templates more, with no difference on program interpretation; the strategy's mechanism is that a learner "cannot complete a program without carefully studying it."
Later cognitive-load work shows the worked-example advantage fades and reverses with expertise (Kalyuga et al. 2001), so for this book's readers the fading should start late and move quickly.
Completion problems are the one scaffolded format that works fully on paper.

### Cited Findings
- Van Merriënboer, "Strategies for programming instruction in high school: program completion vs. program generation," Journal of Educational Computing Research 6(3), 1990, pp. 265-285, DOI 10.2190/4NK5-17L7-TWQV-1EHL: two matched groups, 28 (completion) and 29 (generation) grade 10-12 students, 10-lesson COMAL-80 course. "The completion group was superior to the generation group in measures concerning the construction of programs; furthermore, it was characterized by a lower mortality." The completion group "showed a superior use of programming language templates in both a program construction test and a multiple choice test." No significant difference on program interpretation (understanding and explaining program logic) | [University of Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/strategies-for-programming-instruction-in-high-school-program-com/)
- The completion strategy "emphasizes the modification and extension of existing programs, as opposed to a traditional strategy that emphasizes the design and coding of new programs"; hypothesis: "the direct availability of examples in the form of incomplete computer programs facilitates the acquisition of programming language templates, especially because students cannot complete a program without carefully studying it so that 'mindful abstraction' is explicitly provoked" | [Twente record (computer-based study)](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/strategies-for-computer-based-programming-instruction-program-com/)
- CASCO (van Merriënboer and de Croock, 1992): an intelligent tutor that "generates completion assignments consisting of a problem description together with a solution (program) that may be incomplete, and the learner has to complete increasingly larger parts of the given program as programming experience develops" | [Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/strategies-for-computer-based-programming-instruction-program-com/)
- Garner's CORT (Code Restructuring Tool), part-complete solutions: "Initial evaluations indicate that students using CORT require less time to solve problems and require fewer resources than non-CORT students" | [Garner 2001, Informing Science proceedings](https://proceedings.informingscience.org/IS2001Proceedings/pdf/GarnerEBKATool.pdf)
- Expertise reversal, the primary: Kalyuga, Chandler, Tuovinen, Sweller, "When problem solving is superior to studying worked examples," Journal of Educational Psychology 93(3), 2001, pp. 579-588. Inexperienced mechanical-trade apprentices "benefited most from the worked examples condition with lower mental load ratings, while with more experience, worked examples became redundant and problem solving proved superior." "The relative effectiveness of either worked examples or problem solving depends heavily on levels of learner knowledge" | [UOW record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/139/)
- Fading as the fix: "To facilitate the transition from learning from worked examples in earlier stages of skill acquisition to problem solving in later stages, it is effective to successively fade out worked solution steps. Fading out steps in worked example triggers self-explanation activities" | [Expertise reversal effect (Wikipedia summary of Renkl and Atkinson)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)
- Order matters by prior knowledge: "Low prior knowledge learners benefited most from traditional example-problem pairs while their high prior knowledge counterparts benefited most from problem-example pairs" | [Expertise reversal effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)
- Weinman cites Renkl, Atkinson, Maier, Staley 2002 ("From example study to problem solving: Smooth transitions help learning," J. Experimental Education 70(4)) for faded scaffolding across domains, and frames faded Parsons as its programming form: faded scaffolding "limits the new knowledge that must be integrated... compared to fully removing the scaffolding" | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Blank-variable Parsons as a completion form (Weinman chapter 4, n=13 CS1): with all variable names blanked, completion fell from 3/3 and 4/6 (standard Parsons) to 0/3 and 0/5, the same as writing from scratch (0/7, 0/2); students rated it harder than standard Parsons (p<.02) and 9 of 11 preferred it; the author's suggested uses are "blank targeted pieces, for example all list indexing or base-case code" and "misconception-based fading" | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Linn and Clancy (1992), cited by Xie et al.: "learners who read expert solutions and commentary outperformed learners who wrote their own," and a "repertoire of templates" reduces the cognitive demand of writing; their suggested exercises are code comprehension, spotting opportunities and "nonopportunities" for reuse, multiple representations, and comparing related patterns | [Xie et al. 2019](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2019IntroCSTheoryOfInstruction.pdf)
- Language-learning cloze and fading within PRIMM (Parry, Raspberry Pi 2020, one school, ages 12-13): colour-coded statements, cloze exercises, "fading worked examples, and fading cloze exercises" versus the standard PRIMM materials; baseline Mann-Whitney p=0.191, posttest p=0.001 in favor of the modified materials; no means or effect sizes reported | [Parry 2020](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/04/Investigating-the-relationship-between-programming-and-natural-languages-and-the-impact-of-applying-language-learning-tools-within-the-PRIMM-framework.pdf)
- The ICAP framing Ericson uses for the same idea: "Completion tasks can reduce cognitive load"; worked examples plus practice best; "avoid the expertise reversal effect" | [Ericson slides](https://www.it.uu.se/research/group/upcerg/events/CompetenciesAI/EricsonSlides.pdf)

### Inferences
- The completion result is the strongest paper-only finding in this set: a partial program with the chapter's idiom removed forces the reader to study the surrounding code, which is the "mindful abstraction" van Merriënboer measured as better template use. For a book whose chapters already present full listings, the natural exercise is the listing with the pattern-bearing lines removed, followed by a problem-only exercise.
- Expertise reversal says the fade should be fast for this audience: one completion exercise per idiom, then write-from-spec, rather than a long ladder. The "problem-example pairs" result for high-prior-knowledge learners also supports putting a write-it exercise *before* the worked solution in the solutions folder (the solution then acts as the example).
- Weinman's blank-variable result warns that blanking the wrong thing (names) turns a scaffold into a harder-than-writing puzzle; blank the lines that carry the idea, never the identifiers.

### Gaps
- No programming-specific replication of the completion strategy on adult or intermediate learners; both van Merriënboer studies are high school, and CASCO's evaluation data were not found.
- The Gray and Renkl LISP lineage named in the brief was not located under those names; the LISP worked-example line is usually Pirolli and Anderson 1985, which I did not fetch.
- No effect size for the 1990 study was found in the records read.

## Key question 3: Subgoal-labeled worked examples

### Takeaway
Subgoal labels (short, function-stating, imperative phrases such as "determine loop condition" set above a group of steps) improve novices' code writing from worked examples; the aggregated loop studies (220 CS1 students, three universities) give a medium effect (ω²=.08) with two winning combinations: given labels with far-transfer practice (d=.59 over no labels) or learner-generated labels with near-transfer practice (d=.44 over given).
The semester-long study (N=307) found no difference in average exam score but half the withdrawal and failure rate.
Labels add 12-20 minutes per session; they are pure paper.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: "Subgoal labels are function-based instructional phrases that explain to the learner the purpose of that step, or subgoal, in the problem-solving process"; good labels are "action-based phrases (i.e., similarly to imperative sentences like 'Close the door,' or 'Press the button'); they tell the problem solver what to do next"; they are context independent and group steps. "Subgoals are NOT Comments" (control and labeled groups saw identical comments) | [Morrison, Margulieux, Decker, "The Curious Case of Loops" (NSF PAR)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483); [Morrison seminar slides](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf)
- Verbatim loop labels used: "define and initialize variables"; "initialize the loop"; "determine loop condition" (sub-subgoals: "determine termination condition of loop," "invert the termination condition into a continuation condition"); "update loop"; "process body of loop (why did we write it?)"; "determine results" | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Verbatim selection-statement labels (writing): "Define how many mutually exclusive paths are needed"; "Order from most restrictive/selective group to least restrictive"; "Write if statement with Boolean expression"; "Follow with true bracket including action"; "Follow with else bracket"; "Repeat until all groups and actions are accounted for" | [Morrison slides](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf)
- Labels are derived by Task Analysis by Problem Solving (TAPS), a subject-matter expert plus a knowledge-extraction expert, to expose tacit steps (Margulieux, Morrison, Decker, ITiCSE 2019, "Design and Pilot Testing of Subgoal Labeled Worked Examples for Five Core Concepts in CS1") | [Morrison slides](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf)
- Aggregated design (three experiments, 220 intro-programming students, three universities, while loops in pseudocode, ~2 hours): 3 label formats (none, given, learner-generated) × 2 transfer distances (isomorphic practice vs contextual practice); outcome four novel code-writing problems scored to 44. "Problem-solving score depended on the interaction of the worked example format and transfer distance, F (2, 188) = 5.23, p = .028, est. ω2 = .08" (medium). Simple effects: contextual transfer, no labels vs given labels d=.59 (p=.01); isomorphic transfer, given vs generated d=.44 (p=.02). Conclusion: best are "1) Given subgoal labeled worked examples with farther transfer ... or 2) Student generated subgoal labels for worked examples with nearer transfer" | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Experiment 3 (100 students, northeastern technical university) had *already learned* while loops (94% had a prior course); the aggregated effect did not interact with university (p=.12 to .65), and generated labels with isomorphic practice "performed the best" there | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Null results in the same data: Parsons problem outcome (F=1.11, p=.41), multiple-choice tracing posttest (p=.34), and self-reported cognitive load (p=.63) showed no label effect; "students did not perceive a meaningful difference in mental workload" | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Time cost: labeled conditions took 12-20 minutes longer (52 vs 72 min isomorphic, d=.83; 59 vs 79 min generated-contextual, d=.66), but performance was not explained by time alone: "the benefit of learning subgoals (under particular circumstances) is due to more than coaxing students to spend more time on task" | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Learner-generated label quality (Study 2): twice as many learners wrote specific labels as general ones; with contextual transfer, specific-label writers "performed as poorly as the worst performing group," general-label writers "performed better than any other group" | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Theoretical basis cited: Catrambone 1998, abstract labels beat context-specific labels for learners with sufficient prior knowledge, on delayed or variant problems, because abstract labels force self-explanation; unprompted, "10% or less of learners self-explained" | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Earlier results: Margulieux, Guzdial, Catrambone (ICER 2012, App Inventor, psychology undergraduates): "Subgoal labeled worked examples were found to improve problem-solving performance by 8%" without more study time; Morrison, Margulieux, Ericson, Guzdial (SIGCSE 2016): given labels beat both no-label and generated groups on Parsons problems | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Semester-long study (Margulieux, Morrison, Decker, International Journal of STEM Education 2020; N=307, University of Nebraska Omaha CS1, fall 2018, same instructors/exams in both arms): quizzes "medium effect size" in favor of subgoals; exam total "small effect size"; "For average, no statistical difference between groups, but subgoal group has lower variance"; "Students in subgoal group were half as likely to withdraw and half as likely to fail than the control group"; "Subgoals increased persistence, particularly for students at risk" | [Morrison slides](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf); [IJSTEM 2020 record](https://doaj.org/article/9a2c85d66fd54ec9ab019e9a510bbf17); [Never Work in Theory summary](https://neverworkintheory.org/2023/03/10/reducing-withdrawal-and-failure-rates-with-labeled-subgoals.html)
- Explanation quality: on quiz "explain in plain English" items analyzed with SOLO (Decker, Margulieux, Morrison, ICER 2019), the subgoal group "Gave more complete answers, often including relational and abstract information, on three of the four quiz questions" | [Morrison slides](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf)
- Practical rule from the authors: give labels if students struggle or cannot self-explain; have students generate labels if they already self-explain, and then give feedback *or* hints but not both (Margulieux and Catrambone 2019: "providing both hints and feedback did not improve performance") | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Scope limits stated by the authors: single lab sessions, no delayed test, only while loops and CS1 topics; "Need replication at other institutions, broader population," other languages and classes listed as "Unknown" | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483); [Morrison slides](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf)

### Inferences
- Subgoal labels are the format with the best fit to a book of worked listings: a listing of a pattern becomes a subgoal-labeled worked example by inserting three to six imperative, context-free phrases above the step groups (not comments, which the control group also had). The 2019 Catrambone rationale says these labels should name the function of the step ("register the observer"), not its content ("append to the list").
- For readers with prior knowledge, the evidence favors learner-generated labels with near-transfer practice: a book can print a listing with blank "Subgoal:" slots, ask the reader to fill them, and give the expert labels in the solutions folder. Specific labels hurt on far transfer, so the solution should model general labels.
- The persistence result (half the withdrawals) is from a CS1 course, so it does not transfer to a self-study reader directly, but it suggests the format's benefit is in keeping weaker readers moving rather than raising the average.

### Gaps
- No study applies subgoal labels to design-level content (patterns, class design) or to programmers learning a second language; the planned extensions were recursion and inheritance.
- IJSTEM 2020 numeric effect sizes and raw withdrawal counts were not in the documents read; only the slide-level statements.

## Key question 4: PRIMM and Use-Modify-Create

### Takeaway
PRIMM (Predict, Run, Investigate, Modify, Make) is a lesson sequence built on the reading-before-writing evidence and on Use-Modify-Create's ownership transfer; its one controlled evaluation (13 schools, 493 pupils aged 11-14, 8-12 weeks, non-equivalent control group) found a significant post-test advantage with no reported effect size.
Its author says "further research is needed to examine what specific elements of it make a difference."
Predict and Investigate are paper; Run needs a runtime.

### Cited Findings
- Stages as defined by the Raspberry Pi Foundation quick read: Predict ("discuss a program and predict what it might do"); Run ("test their prediction"); Investigate ("tracing, explaining, annotating, debugging"); Modify ("edit the program to change its functionality via a sequence of increasingly more challenging exercises"); Make ("design a new program that uses the same structures, but solves a new problem") | [PRIMM quick read](https://static.raspberrypi.org/files/curriculum/quickreads/11-Pedagogy_Summary_PRIMM_V4_2023.pdf)
- The study: Sentance, Waite, Kallia, "Teaching computer programming with PRIMM: a sociocultural perspective," Computer Science Education 29(2-3), 2019, pp. 136-176, DOI 10.1080/08993408.2019.1608781. 13 schools, 493 students aged 11-14, 8-12 weeks, mixed methods with control group, baseline and post-test; "learners performed better in the post test than the control group"; teachers said it "helped them to teach effectively in mixed-ability classes." The abstract reports no effect size | [Glasgow eprints record](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/229013)
- Design detail from Sentance's seminar: "a type of quasi-experimental design known as the non-equivalent control group post-test design"; ten Python lessons on sequence, selection, iteration; "The results showed a statistically significant difference in the score between the control and experimental groups for all students in favour of the experimental group" | [Sentance 2021 seminar proceedings](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2022/08/Teaching_Programming_with_PRIMM-1.pdf)
- Five principles: "Read code before you write code"; "Work collaboratively to talk about programs"; "Focus on code comprehension (Block Model)"; "Use existing starter programs"; "Gradually take ownership" | [Sentance 2021](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2022/08/Teaching_Programming_with_PRIMM-1.pdf)
- Lineage: "PRIMM has partly built on Use-Modify-Create (UMC) (Lee et al., 2011) to gradually transfer ownership"; it cites Lister et al. 2004 and Lister, Fidge, Teague 2009 for reading before writing, Vygotsky for the social-to-cognitive plane, and Denny et al. 2008 for "Actually writing code (as opposed to reading) is particularly hard for novice programmers" | [Sentance 2021](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2022/08/Teaching_Programming_with_PRIMM-1.pdf)
- Prediction is meant to be unassessed: "Prediction should be unassessed and low stakes," done in pairs; giving learners a program to run rather than copy "reduces anxiety" | [PRIMM quick read](https://static.raspberrypi.org/files/curriculum/quickreads/11-Pedagogy_Summary_PRIMM_V4_2023.pdf)
- Author's own caveat: "PRIMM is certainly a popular approach but further research is needed to examine what specific elements of it make a difference to learner outcomes" | [Sentance 2021](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2022/08/Teaching_Programming_with_PRIMM-1.pdf)
- Use-Modify-Create: Lee, Martin, Denner, Coulter, Allan, Erickson, Malyn-Smith, Werner, "Computational Thinking for Youth in Practice," ACM Inroads 2(1), 2011. Use ("interact with an existing computational artifact"), Modify ("modifying and iteratively refining someone else's project to become their own"), Create ("develop ideas for new computational projects of their own design"); developed by the NSF ITEST working group for youth (K-12) programs. Described as "shown to support and deepen students' computational thinking experience" | [K12CS summary](https://k12cs.org/computational-thinking/)
- A separate prediction-first result at college level: "Prediction versus production for teaching computer programming," Learning and Instruction, June 2024: 121 college students with no coding experience, randomly assigned to predict (given code, predict output before being taught) or tell-and-practice (taught, then write); "Participants in the predict condition showed greater learning and better non-cognitive outcomes," including more positive emotional responses to R and its error messages | [index record (access blocked at fetch time)](https://ubmr.hds.hebis.de/EdsRecord/ehh%252C176332717)

### Inferences
- PRIMM's evidence is a single school-age quasi-experiment, so its value for an adult book is the structure, not the effect: the Predict step (write what this prints before you run it) and the Investigate step (trace, annotate, explain) are paper exercises that already match the BRACElet findings; Modify maps onto completion/extension exercises; Make onto write-from-spec.
- Use-Modify-Create is a design heuristic without controlled evidence in the documents read; its contribution to a book is the ordering "change a given listing before writing a new one," which the completion-strategy data (question 2) support independently.
- The 2024 prediction study is the only adult, randomized evidence that asking "what does this print" *before* the explanation beats explain-then-practice; a chapter can place a prediction prompt above a listing and the explanation after it.

### Gaps
- Effect size, exact test, and attrition for the PRIMM 2019 study were not in the abstract or seminar text.
- Authors of the Learning and Instruction 2024 prediction paper could not be retrieved (two index services blocked); cite by title and journal until confirmed.
- No controlled evaluation of Use-Modify-Create was found.

## Key question 5: Reading, tracing, and explain-in-plain-English before writing

### Takeaway
The BRACElet line (Lister 2004; Lister 2006; Lopez 2008; Venables 2009) found that end-of-CS1 students who cannot trace at roughly 50% accuracy almost never write code well, that tracing plus explaining predicts 46-66% of writing variance, and that relational "explain in plain English" answers mark readiness to write; the authors advocate "a mix of reading and writing tasks," never a proportion.
A 600-student replication (Fowler et al. 2022) found the hierarchy did not emerge as the top model and concluded that correlational snapshots cannot settle teaching order; a data-structures study (Harrington and Cheng 2018) found the hierarchy reversed for CS2 students, which Pelchen, Mathieson, Lister (2020) attribute to question difficulty.
All of these formats are paper.

### Cited Findings
- Lister et al., "A Multi-National Study of Reading and Tracing Skills in Novice Programmers," ITiCSE 2004 working group, SIGCSE Bulletin 36(4), pp. 119-150: 12 institutions, 7 countries, 556 students with all 12 multiple-choice tracing items, mostly end of first semester. Quartile means 89/70/50/24% correct; Cronbach's α 0.75. Conclusion: "many students lack knowledge and skills that are a precursor to problem-solving. These missing elements relate more to the ability of students to read code than to write it" | [Lister et al. 2004](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~j-hamer/ITiCSE04-wg.pdf)
- The "doodle" finding (56 students): careful trace marks gave 75-78% correct answers, no marks 50%: "if a student carefully traces through the code (S, T, or O), thus documenting changes in variables, the likelihood of getting the correct answer is high. In contrast, not doodling (B) only leads to the correct answer 50% of the time." Inference: "novices also rely heavily on their short-term working memory when attempting to trace code" | [Lister et al. 2004](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~j-hamer/ITiCSE04-wg.pdf)
- The study never measured writing: "This study assumed that the knowledge and skills ... are precursors to problem solving. The next logical research step is to examine that assumption." Teaching suggestion: "perhaps an early emphasis on program comprehension and tracing, with the aim of automating basic skills, might then free the minds of students to concentrate on problem-solving" | [Lister et al. 2004](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~j-hamer/ITiCSE04-wg.pdf)
- Lister, Simon, Thompson, Whalley, Prasad, "Not Seeing the Forest for the Trees: Novice Programmers and the SOLO Taxonomy," ITiCSE 2006, pp. 118-122: on "In plain English, explain what the following segment of Java code does" (an is-sorted loop), one third of 108 end-of-semester students answered relationally, half multistructurally, 7 of 8 educators relationally; "Approximately one half of students in the top two quartiles manifested a relational response ... but multistructural responses dominated in the lower two quartiles." Claim: "students who cannot read a short piece of code and describe it in relational terms are not intellectually well equipped to write similar code." Hedge: "We are not advocating that students must first be taught to read code ... before they ever write a line of code, but we do advocate a mix of reading and writing tasks" | [Lister et al. 2006](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/courses/compsci747s2c/lectures/p118-lister.pdf)
- Lopez, Whalley, Robbins, Lister, "Relationships between reading, tracing and writing skills in introductory programming," ICER 2008, pp. 101-112: 38 consenting end-of-semester Java students at one NZ institution. Tracing of iterative code vs writing r=0.63 (p<.0001); explain vs writing r=0.56 (p=.0002); "In combination, Tracing2 and Explain account for 46% of the variance in Writing." Hierarchy: "Knowledge of programming constructs forms the bottom of the hierarchy, with 'explain in English', Parson's puzzles, and the tracing of iterative code forming one or more intermediate levels." Non-iterative tracing did not predict iterative tracing; writing averaged 37% even with the String API supplied. "The direction of the arrows should not be interpreted as evidence of causation" | [Lopez et al. 2008](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/10806/1/2008001530.pdf)
- The traffic-light rule quoted there from Philpott, Robbins, Whalley 2007: "The green light for relational thinking would seem to be a complete mastery of the code tracing task. A better than 50% performance on the tracing task could be viewed as an orange light. However if tracing ability is at a lower level than 50% then … the light is definitely red when it comes to relational thinking" | [Lopez et al. 2008](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/10806/1/2008001530.pdf)
- Venables, Tan, Lister, "A Closer Look at Tracing, Explaining and Code Writing Skills in the Novice Programmer," ICER 2009, pp. 117-128: 32 end-of-semester Java students, one institution. Tracing vs writing R²=0.50; explain vs writing R²=0.49; combined model R²=0.66 (Writing = 1.24·Tracing + 1.68·Explain − 3.12). Threshold: "students who score poorly on the tracing questions rarely score well on the code writing tasks, but there is no clear relationship with code writing for students who scored well on tracing questions. This suggests a causal relationship, where a minimal level of skill at tracing is necessary for code writing, but that minimal skill at tracing is not sufficient." Of students who traced above 50% and explained both items correctly, 83% wrote well; below 50% tracing, zero did. "None of the students who scored the maximum number of points on explanation questions did poorly on the code writing questions" | [Venables et al. 2009](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/11384/1/2009002706OK.pdf)
- Their ordering claim and its limit: "First, the novice acquires the ability to trace code. As the capacity to trace becomes reliable, the ability to explain code develops. When students are reasonably capable of both tracing and explaining, the ability to systematically write code emerges." But "we do not support the idea of a strict hierarchy ... We believe that all three skills reinforce each other and develop in parallel." Also: complex tracing correlated *worse* with writing than simple tracing (R²=0.27 vs 0.51); "part of the skill in code writing is verifying code without doing complex tracing" | [Venables et al. 2009](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/11384/1/2009002706OK.pdf)
- Replication at scale: Fowler, Smith, Hassan, Poulsen, West, Zilles, "Reevaluating the relationship between explaining, tracing, and writing skills in CS1 in a replication study," Computer Science Education 2022: 600+ students in a non-majors Python course, structural equation modeling across four exams; the Lopez hierarchy "did not emerge as a top candidate model, though comparable alternatives did"; "analyzing student performance on these skills at a moment in time is not sufficient to determine teaching order." A search summary of the same paper adds that tracing as a prerequisite to writing replicated, while the explain/write ordering flipped depending on the threshold used (mean vs median) | [Fowler et al. 2022 (NSF PAR)](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10340072)
- Beyond CS1: Harrington and Cheng, "Tracing vs. Writing Code: Beyond the Learning Hierarchy," SIGCSE 2018, pp. 423-428, DOI 10.1145/3159450.3159530, reported for data-structures students that "the hierarchy reversed ... tracing and explaining were skills that followed writing." Pelchen, Mathieson, Lister (ACE 2020) found the CS1 hierarchy did hold at the end of a data-structures course and suggest the earlier reversal "may occur due to a mismatch in the relative difficulty of tracing, explaining and writing questions" | [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Tracing+vs.+Writing+Code+Beyond+the+Learning+Hierarchy+Harrington+Cheng&rows=2); [Pelchen et al. 2020](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/138544)
- EiPE question craft (Fowler, Chen, Zilles, "How should we 'Explain in plain English'? Voices from the Community," ICER 2021, DOI 10.1145/3446871.3469738; 11 CS1 instructors interviewed): the code should "have a purpose" and be "doing something real"; use introductory patterns ("find the biggest thing," "sum up all the elements," checking a property of a collection); behavior "should not be guessable from one line"; size has a sweet spot (too small and relational and multistructural answers are indistinguishable, too large and it cannot be abstracted); "EipE questions are not supposed to be about trickery"; descriptive names make it easier, so either scaffold from good names to stripped ones or present stripped names and discuss naming afterward; train students by showing a multistructural answer beside a relational one; use EiPE "more useful in a formative sense" | [Fowler et al. 2021](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/how_to_eipe_icer_2021.pdf)
- SOLO levels operationalized for explain answers (Clear, Whalley, Lister, Carbone, Sheard, Hu, Simon, Thompson, NACCQ 2008): Relational "Provides a summary of what the code does in terms of the code's purpose. (The 'forest')"; Multistructural "A line by line description is provided of all the code. (The individual 'trees')"; Unistructural "Provides a description for one portion of the code"; Prestructural "Substantially lacks knowledge of programming constructs or is unrelated to the question." Inter-rater agreement was moderate (Kendall's W 0.50 to 0.77 with four judges); hardest to rate were the nested-loop and binary-search items | [Clear et al. 2008](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/12626/1/2008001537.pdf)
- EiPE predicts proficiency (Murphy, Fitzgerald, Lister, McCauley, ICER 2012): "a strong correlation between ability to correctly answer 'explain in plain English' (EiPE) questions and ability to write code"; EiPE "aren't widely used in practice because of difficulty of grading and workload issues" | [UTS record](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/22969); [ICPEC 2020 review](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/01oasics/oasics-vol081-icpec2020/OASIcs.ICPEC.2020.24/OASIcs.ICPEC.2020.24.pdf)
- Comprehension-first tracing instruction (Nelson, Xie, Ko, "Comprehension First," ICER 2017): 37 self-selected University of Washington students entering CS1, PLTutor (step an instruction-level notional machine through fixed examples, never writing code) vs Codecademy. SCS1 gains 3.89 vs 2.42 of 27 (d=.40, not significant, p<.41); within-condition gain significant only for PLTutor (p<.0044); gains predicted midterm only for PLTutor (adj R²=.65); "PLT had no midterm failures (vs. 2 in CC)." Authors: "Our empirically strongest result is that PLTutor normalized midterm outcomes." Related-work note: Anderson et al. 1989 found tracing exercises interleaved with writing gave no benefit for writing | [Nelson et al. 2017](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Nelson2017PLTutor.pdf)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports reading and tracing exercises *preceding* writing on each new construct, at the level of "a mix," and gives one calibrated threshold (about 50% on simple tracing) below which writing exercises are wasted. No paper proposes a ratio; Lopez's "20 explanation tasks and 20 short pieces of code" is a wish, not a finding.
- For readers who already program, the CS2 data are split: Harrington and Cheng's reversal and Fowler's non-replication both say the strict order is a novice phenomenon, while Pelchen keeps the hierarchy when question difficulty is matched. The safe reading for this book: tracing exercises earn their place where the runtime behavior is new to the reader (descriptors, generators, `match`, async scheduling), and explain-in-plain-English exercises earn theirs on any listing whose purpose is not visible from one line.
- Venables's finding that complex tracing predicts writing *worse* than simple tracing argues against long trace tables as book exercises; short traces of the one surprising step, followed by an explain prompt, match the data better.
- EiPE and tracing are the formats with the best paper fit of all: a listing, a prompt, and a model relational answer in the solutions folder. The Fowler 2021 guidance on question size, purpose, and naming is directly usable as an authoring checklist.

### Gaps
- No study gives a proportion of reading to writing exercises, and none tests the ordering experimentally (Fowler et al. 2022 state this as the needed study).
- Whalley et al. ACE 2006 (the Bloom and SOLO question-set paper) was not retrieved; the file at its URL was the 2008 reliability study.
- The Nelson 2017 result is small (n=37) and the condition comparison is not significant; it supports tracing-first as promising, not proven.

## Key question 6: Debugging, refute, critique, and refactoring exercises

### Takeaway
Explicit debugging instruction raises self-efficacy and performance (Michaeli and Romeike 2019, K-12 RCT; Zhang et al. 2025, 44 undergraduates, context-specific steps gave 80% localization after one session vs 20-44%), and refute questions (give a counter-example that breaks this code) are a defined but lightly evaluated format.
Refactoring and code-critique studies on second-year software-engineering students show they fix local redundancies readily (92-95%) but overlook structural issues (god methods, loop choice, nested-if duplication) and often "fix" them wrongly.
All of these are paper formats; critique and refactoring need no runtime.

### Cited Findings
- Michaeli and Romeike, "Improving Debugging Skills in the Classroom: The Effects of Teaching a Systematic Debugging Process," WiPSCE 2019, pp. 57-63: pre-post control-group design in K-12; "a significant increase in both self-efficacy expectations and debugging performance in the experimental group in contrast to the control group." Sample size not in the abstract | [FAU record](https://cris.fau.de/publications/228376370); [PDF](https://computingeducation.de/pub/2019_Michaeli-Romeike_WIPSCE19.pdf)
- Zhang, Roy, Arnaoudova, "Context-Specific Instruction: A Longitudinal Study on Debugging Skill Acquisition and Retention for Novice Programmers," arXiv 2509.22420, 2025: 44 undergraduates (mean 4.0 years programming, 4.6 months C#), four weekly sessions plus a retention session after three weeks, four arms. Context-specific instruction reached 80% bug-localization correctness at session 1 vs 20-44% for control, abstract guidelines (Ko et al. 2019), and concrete context-agnostic steps (LaToza et al. 2020); retained at 80% vs 38% control (p=0.014); time 13-15 min vs 22-31. Abstract guidelines gave "limited, not significant improvements." Limits: ~10 per group, localization only, 91% male | [Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22420v1)
- Refute questions: "a Refute question asks students for a counter-example which proves that a given code fragment is an incorrect solution for a given task"; it tests "abilities to comprehend the task and the code well enough to recognize a mismatch." Three design concerns: "(1) Is the task comprehensible? (2) Is the incorrect code a plausible solution for the task? (3) Is the complexity of finding a counter-example acceptable?" Introduced by Viraj Kumar as "Refute: An Alternative to 'Explain in Plain English' Questions," ICER 2021 lightning talk and poster; follow-up "A Bug's New Life: Creating Refute Questions from Filtered CS1 Student Code Snapshots" (DOI 10.1145/3576882.3617916, a Koli Calling 2023 DOI prefix) mines buggy student submissions for plausible wrong code | [ICER 2021 profile](https://icer2021.acm.org/profile/virajkumar); [search summary of the follow-up](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3576882.3617916)
- Refactoring in a tutor (Keuning, Heeren, Jeuring, "Student Refactoring Behaviour in a Programming Tutor," Koli Calling 2020, DOI 10.1145/3428029.3428043): 133 mostly second-year software-engineering students, six Java exercises, each a functionally correct method with quality issues. "On average, 92% of the quality issues that were present at the start were dealt with." "Students had no problem with simple edits, such as using compound operators and removing self assignments, but did have trouble with complex control flow (nested if statements and breaking out of a loop) and composed expressions." No control group or pre/post test. Misconception seen: replacing `count = count` with `return count` | [Keuning et al. 2020](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~jeuri101/homepage/Publications/StudentRefactoringBehaviour.pdf)
- Refactoring think-aloud (Oliveira, Keuning, Jeuring, Koli Calling 2024, DOI 10.1145/3699538.3699550; 12 second-year-and-up Utrecht students, C#, 1-6 years experience): of 203 issue opportunities, 60% fixed, 31% not identified. Redundant boolean expressions fixed 95%, redundant if-else 91%; suboptimal loop choice overlooked 76% (`for` to `foreach` fixed in 2 of 30); god method not identified in 12 of 20; complex nested ifs fixed by 2 of 11, with four students merging ifs with `&&` and dropping the else. "Students were able to remove code quality issues in most cases. However, they often overlooked particular code quality issues, such as the presence of a method with multiple responsibilities or the use of a less suitable loop structure." "the Unnecessary Code reasoning is also often used to motivate incorrect refactoring steps." Experienced students "reason more often about code quality attributes rather than pointing at a problem" | [Oliveira et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20875)
- Prior code-quality findings cited there: Keuning, Heeren, Jeuring (ITiCSE 2017) "modularization issues are rarely fixed"; Tigina et al. (ICSE-SEET 2023) students fix simple issues but ignore hard-to-understand ones; Wiese et al. (ICPC 2019) 66% of novices preferred an if returning true/false over returning the boolean directly; Kirk et al. 2020: 71% of 141 intro courses lack code quality in their learning outcomes | [Oliveira et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20875)
- Debugging proposed as a SOLO probe (Lister et al. 2006): "The above code is meant to count the number of equal numbers ... The bug is due to an error in one line or in two lines. Nominate the buggy line(s), explain what is wrong, and provide a corrected version" | [Lister et al. 2006](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/courses/compsci747s2c/lectures/p118-lister.pdf)
- Fix-code versus Parsons in the review: "fix and write code activities were more beneficial for more advanced students" (Fabic et al.) | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)

### Inferences
- The refactoring data are the closest thing to intermediate-learner evidence in this whole set, and they point at what a critique exercise must do: readers of this book will spot a redundant `== True` unaided, so an exercise on that is wasted; the value is in structural issues they do not see (a class doing two jobs, the wrong loop or data-structure choice, duplicated branches) and in checking the *reason* they give, since "unnecessary code" reasoning produced wrong fixes.
- Refute questions fit a pattern book well on paper: "this implementation of *Observer* is wrong; give an input sequence that shows it," with the counter-example in the solutions folder. Kumar's three design concerns are the authoring checklist; the second (the wrong code must be plausible) is the hard one and his later work mines real student bugs for it.
- Debugging instruction works when the steps are tied to the specific code, not stated as general advice; an exercise that says "find the bug" should carry a short, concrete procedure for that listing (which test would isolate it, which value to inspect) rather than a general debugging strategy.

### Gaps
- No controlled study measures learning from refute questions; the ICER 2021 item is a lightning talk.
- No study measures learning gains from refactoring or critique exercises (both Keuning studies are descriptive and say so).
- Sample size and effect size for Michaeli and Romeike 2019 were not in the abstract.

## Key question 7: Design-level question types (which pattern applies, what breaks if you remove X, implement two ways and compare, predict the output)

### Takeaway
The one controlled result on comparative exercises in programming (Patitsas, Craig, Easterbrook, ICER 2013, 241 CS2 students) found side-by-side comparison of two algorithms beat sequential presentation on procedural knowledge (p=.02) and flexibility (p=.047) with no difference on conceptual knowledge, and the effect held at follow-up.
Pattern-selection difficulty is documented (students confuse patterns of overlapping intent) but only in small, uncontrolled studies; "what breaks if you remove X" has no CER evidence beyond a 2023 position paper on counterfactual questions.
All four types are paper; prediction works best with a runtime to check.

### Cited Findings
- Patitsas, Craig, Easterbrook, "Comparing and Contrasting Different Algorithms Leads to Increased Student Learning," ICER 2013, pp. 145-152: CS2 Python course, University of Toronto, n=241 (83 completed all four parts), double-blind, hierarchical linear models. Workbook on hash tables: control saw linear probing then quadratic probing sequentially with "why would you use this method?"; experimental saw both side by side with "compare and contrast the two methods." Procedural: "students in the experimental section demonstrated significantly more procedural knowledge on the two posttests (p = 0.02) ... 0.61 points (6%) better." Flexibility: p=0.047 (posttest 53.9% vs 47.1%, follow-up 57.5% vs 49.4%). Conceptual: p=0.860, "The two groups performed equally on conceptual knowledge." "We found that the effect held in our second posttest." Recommendation: "demonstrate multiple solution approaches while teaching programming – showing solutions in parallel to students in CS2 (and presumably above)" | [Patitsas et al. 2013](https://cs.mcgill.ca/~patitsas/publications/compare_contrast.pdf)
- The authors note the result "runs against what cognitive load theory would predict, yet it worked," and that Rittle-Johnson and Star say students need "familiarity and fluency with a limited range of strategies before comparison," which may not hold early in CS1; students with no prior conceptual knowledge gained more from comparison at follow-up | [Patitsas et al. 2013](https://cs.mcgill.ca/~patitsas/publications/compare_contrast.pdf)
- Contrasting cases in general: "small examples that each differ concerning one deep feature of a concept while keeping other features constant," which "helps students detect flaws in their solution attempts" | [Instructional Science 2020 record](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11251-020-09504-7)
- Weinman's finding that open-ended writing lets students miss the pattern, and the consequence for comparison: "If students are given the freedom to solve two related exercises in entirely different ways, they miss an opportunity to compare and contrast two applications of the same pattern" | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Pattern selection: San Martin, Manzano, Vieira de Camargo, "Do Students Learn Better Together? Teaching Design Patterns and the OSI Model with the Aronson Method," arXiv 2508.16770, 2025: third-year Java course, n=20, jigsaw over creational/structural/behavioral sessions with quiz items of the form "In a system where multiple incompatible interfaces must work together, which pattern is most appropriate? Facade / Singleton / Adapter / Builder." Difficulties: "Overlapping intent caused confusion (e.g., Adapter vs. Facade)"; "Difficulty selecting the appropriate pattern from similar options"; Strategy vs Decorator; Singleton vs Factory Method. "abstract intent, rather than implementation syntax, was the main obstacle." The 2025 cohort beat three earlier cohorts on the final exam (Wilcoxon p=0.0187, 0.0006, 0.0095), but the collaborative index did not correlate with final grade (ρ=0.195, p=0.41); no pre/post test | [San Martin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16770)
- Cited there: Ferrandis Homsi (2021): "many students struggle to identify when and how to apply a specific pattern, resulting in a gap between theoretical knowledge and practical implementation"; students reported "high cognitive load involved in memorizing patterns, especially when trying to differentiate between those with overlapping purposes or similar structural elements" | [San Martin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16770)
- A search-level claim (source quality low, an eScholarship abstract): "students often struggle with pattern selection and problem identification, and a more structured, intent-focused approach to teaching design patterns is necessary" | [eScholarship record](https://www.escholarship.org/uc/item/3xk123wk)
- Design-level exercises for upper-division students (Weinman chapter 6, n=12, Rails software-engineering course, qualitative): Subgoal Decomposition (drag subgoal blocks into goals and into MVC locations) and Data Flow (attach data in/out and connector snippets to ordered subgoals) for adding third-party authentication. Unaided decomposition covered 35% of subgoals; 6 of 12 would have started coding without understanding all subgoals. Self-reports: confidence 3.29 to 4.31, completeness 3.48 to 4.30, estimated hours 4.10 to 2.68. 11 of 12 misplaced an unpracticed concept (middleware). All 12 wanted the exercises as a *pre-assignment* to code writing; only 2 wanted them to replace it. Guess-and-check was enabled by first-error feedback. "These exercises focus on topics that are difficult and inefficient to learn with traditional code writing exercises" | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Design goals Weinman used to judge any exercise format: provide opportunity to practice; scaffold vocabulary; scaffold process; guide students to one solution; present examples efficiently. Code writing rates Low on vocabulary, process, and one-solution | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Prediction exercises: "Predict program output questions show a short code segment (5-10 lines) and ask students to predict the output, identify the value of a variable after execution, or determine how many times a loop runs"; "the act of making a prediction - right or wrong - focuses attention when observing the actual outcome" | [search summary; primary is the Learning and Instruction 2024 study above](https://ubmr.hds.hebis.de/EdsRecord/ehh%252C176332717)
- Counterfactual ("what if") questions: Lekshmi Narayanan, Hendrawan, V, "Enhancing Programming eTextbooks with ChatGPT Generated Counterfactual-Thinking-Inspired Questions," arXiv 2306.00551, 2023: proposes adding counterfactual questions to textbook examples because "example programs ... don't offer sufficient interactivity"; it is a proposal with no reported study, sample, or results | [arXiv 2306.00551](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00551)
- Linn and Clancy's exercise list for templates, including "spotting opportunities and 'nonopportunities' for reuse" and "comparing related patterns" | [Xie et al. 2019](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2019IntroCSTheoryOfInstruction.pdf)

### Inferences
- "Implement two ways and compare" has the best evidence of the four design-level types and it is from CS2, the closest population to this book's readers; the mechanism is side-by-side presentation plus an explicit compare-and-contrast prompt, and the gain is in procedure and flexibility (choosing among methods), not in conceptual recall. For a pattern book: print the two implementations side by side (or have the reader write the second from the first) and ask for the trade-off, not "which is better."
- "Which pattern applies here" is the question students demonstrably get wrong when two patterns share intent, so a book's multiple-choice version should put the confusable pair in the options (Adapter vs Facade, Strategy vs Decorator) and the solution should explain the discriminating feature.
- "What breaks if you remove X" has no evidence either way; its closest cousins with evidence are the refute question (give input that breaks it) and the tracing-after-a-change task. Writing it as a prediction ("remove the `__slots__` line; what does the next listing print?") converts it into a format with support.
- Weinman's upper-division participants wanted design-level exercises *before* coding and not instead of it, which fits a book layout of design question, then implementation exercise.

### Gaps
- No controlled study of pattern-selection exercises, "what breaks" questions, or design critique on learning outcomes.
- The Patitsas result has not been replicated in a design-patterns context.

## Key question 8: Notional machines and exercises that build a runtime model

### Takeaway
A notional machine is "a pedagogic device to assist the understanding of some aspect of programs or programming" (Fincher et al. 2020), and the field's own working group found "limited literature looking at the educational effectiveness of NMs" and no study testing the idea; the evidence that exists is for comprehension-first tracing instruction on an explicit machine (Nelson 2017, small) and for interactive diagram-construction exercises that expose misconceptions (Informa, qualitative).
Diagram-drawing and trace tables are paper; stepping through a visualizer needs a tool.

### Cited Findings
- Fincher, Jeuring, Miller, Donaldson, du Boulay, Hauswirth, Hellas, Hermans, Lewis, Mühling, Pearce, Petersen, "Notional Machines in Computing Education: The Education of Attention," ITiCSE-WGR 2020, pp. 21-50, DOI 10.1145/3437800.3439202. Definition: "A notional machine (NM) is a pedagogic device to assist the understanding of some aspect of programs or programming." Characteristics: purpose ("it should simplify an actual concept or skill as an aid to understanding"), function ("to uncover something about programming ... that is not obviously apparent in the artefact the student is using"), focus, representation ("may simply be verbal, such as saying 'a variable is like a box'"). Framing: NMs "put a spotlight on those things that are important to look at ... which, if un-noticed or misunderstood, would cause the learner to go hopelessly wrong" | [Fincher et al. 2020 (Aalto PDF)](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/76828957/SCI_Hellas_Notional_Machines_in_Computing_Education_The_Education_of_Attention.pdf)
- du Boulay's origin, first-hand: du Boulay, O'Shea, Monk 1981: "A notional machine is the idealised model of the computer implied by the constructs of the programming language"; the 1976 Logo manual's "worker" with ears (parameters), mouth (output), hands (actions); "They did not set out to evaluate how far the pedagogy based on NMs was effective as compared to some other pedagogy" | [Fincher et al. 2020](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/76828957/SCI_Hellas_Notional_Machines_in_Computing_Education_The_Education_of_Attention.pdf)
- Literature review in the report: 226 papers; "Most (72%) cited NMs either as related work, theoretical basis for the work, or motivation. Very few (27%) directly engaged with the concept"; "Relatively few papers performed an evaluation on an NM or its impact on students"; "None focused their evaluation on the idea of NMs themselves, to test the theory"; "There is limited literature looking at the educational effectiveness of NMs" | [Fincher et al. 2020](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/76828957/SCI_Hellas_Notional_Machines_in_Computing_Education_The_Education_of_Attention.pdf)
- Sorva, "Notional machines and introductory programming education," ACM TOCE 13(2), 2013, article 8 (334 citations per Semantic Scholar): defines an NM as "an abstraction of the computer in the role of executor of programs of a particular kind," notes several NMs at different abstraction levels can describe one execution, and "argued for greater awareness and adoption of NMs in research and in practice." Cited as often as du Boulay 1986 in the corpus | [Fincher et al. 2020](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/76828957/SCI_Hellas_Notional_Machines_in_Computing_Education_The_Education_of_Attention.pdf); [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/2483710.2483713?fields=title,authors,venue,year,abstract,citationCount)
- Sorva, Karavirta, Malmi, "A review of generic program visualization systems for introductory programming education," TOCE 2013: "The results of evaluations largely support the use of program visualization in introductory programming education, but research to date is insufficient for drawing more nuanced conclusions with respect to learner engagement"; most systems were "short-lived research prototypes that support the user-controlled viewing of program animations" | [Turku research record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/4015378?lang=fi_FI)
- Catalogue of NMs captured from teachers (43), including: variables as boxes, parking spaces, pigeon holes, clothes pins ("Variables contain *one* value"); typed-boxes memory; stack-and-heap diagram; Python Computer V1-V4 (each "a superset of the previous one, i.e. nothing is removed or replaced"); variable trace table; expression as tree; control flow as graph; hand-drawn runtime stack ("Each function call/frame has its own set of stored values"); call stack as stack of papers; paper list/ListNode (fold the paper over an arrow to mean null); recursion role play; method-call dance ("return address"); Python Tutor, which "shows the state of global variables, the call stack frames with the state of the local variables, and the heap" with "Each variable ... represented as a box" and references as arrows | [Fincher et al. 2020](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/76828957/SCI_Hellas_Notional_Machines_in_Computing_Education_The_Education_of_Attention.pdf)
- Exercises in the report that build the model: Informa clicker tasks where students "construct or complete a stack-and-heap diagram, a control-flow graph, or an expression tree during lecture," which "allowed the instructor to immediately detect and correct possible misconceptions" (stack-and-heap diagrams diagnose "object allocation, references and aliasing, method calls, parameter passing, and polymorphism"); overlaying a flowchart on one's own code "serves as a diagnostic tool to uncover their emerging mental model"; Greg Wilson's "How do you tell?" question "reliably distinguishes whether students have a sequential execution or constant evaluation mental model" (no data given) | [Fincher et al. 2020](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/76828957/SCI_Hellas_Notional_Machines_in_Computing_Education_The_Education_of_Attention.pdf)
- Cautions: on Python Tutor, students "tend to use it a little bit mechanically so they tend to just step through it and not really think about what's actually happening on the screen"; machine-generated forms "can be mechanically 'clicked through' without resulting in a deeper understanding," whereas hand-drawing "slows the teacher and makes students follow along"; analogies carry "the ever-present possibility that incidental aspects ... may lead the student astray" | [Fincher et al. 2020](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/76828957/SCI_Hellas_Notional_Machines_in_Computing_Education_The_Education_of_Attention.pdf)
- Advanced NMs come from inside computing, not analogy: Hauswirth on lambdas, "my NM [now] is just a subset of the programming language"; beginner NMs "may add more 'extra'" | [Fincher et al. 2020](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/76828957/SCI_Hellas_Notional_Machines_in_Computing_Education_The_Education_of_Attention.pdf)
- Nelson, Xie, Ko 2017's machine: five instruction forms (push, pop-compute-push, set namespace, pop-and-jump, clear stack), a stack, a namespace, a call frame; lessons per construct run "prior knowledge, contrast, goal, syntax pattern, then 'step through' and mental-execution prompts," with a counter-example run against each misconception; they claim "the first comprehension-first pedagogy that teaches and assesses tracing skills for a Turing-complete portion of a programming language, without learners writing or editing code" | [Nelson et al. 2017](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Nelson2017PLTutor.pdf)
- Xie et al.'s S1 exercises for the semantics skill: fixed-code questions, annotated control-flow diagrams, crossing out lines that do not execute, and "memory tables" with variable values written per line (Xie, Nelson, Ko 2018) | [Xie et al. 2019](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2019IntroCSTheoryOfInstruction.pdf)

### Inferences
- The field has no controlled evidence that any particular notional machine improves learning, so the usable guidance is structural: pick one machine per runtime idea (references as arrows, frames as a stack, the namespace as a table), keep it a true simplification by omission rather than an analogy that adds things (the report's Aristotelian vs Galilean distinction), and make exercises *construct* the diagram rather than view it, since viewing is the mode the report and Sorva both flag as weak.
- For this book's readers the relevant machines are the ones the report says advanced learners use: subsets of the language itself (the descriptor protocol as a lookup procedure, the generator as a suspended frame, the event loop as a queue of ready tasks). An exercise then reads "draw the frames at the moment the generator yields" or "write the namespace table after this `__set_name__` runs," answerable on paper and checkable with a printed diagram in the solutions folder.
- Python Tutor-style stepping suits the website only, and even there the report's caveat is that it is clicked through; a prediction prompt before stepping (PRIMM's Predict) is the documented fix.

### Gaps
- Sorva 2013's abstract and its visualization-engagement conclusions were not retrievable (publisher blocked); the engagement taxonomy (Naps et al. 2002) is cited in the working-group report only for adoption figures.
- No study compares one notional machine against another on learning (the report calls for exactly this).

## Key question 9: Bloom, SOLO, and cognitive-complexity frameworks for programming tasks

### Takeaway
SOLO is the taxonomy with CER evidence behind it: relational vs multistructural explanations separate students who are ready to write from those who are not, and raters can apply it with "moderate levels of consistency."
Bloom's levels are hard to apply consistently to programming tasks and Thompson et al. (2008) supply an interpretation with exemplars; Duran, Sorva, Leite's CCCP (ICER 2018) extends Soloway's plan analysis into two metrics, plan depth and maximal plan interactivity, to rank programs by cognitive demand, but it is unvalidated.
All three are authoring tools, not exercise formats.

### Cited Findings
- SOLO levels for code reading (Lister et al. 2006): prestructural (misconception), unistructural ("educated guess" on part), multistructural (all parts, unrelated: "fails to see the forest for the trees"), relational ("the student sees the forest"), extended abstract (beyond the problem, e.g. "it will only work for sorted arrays"). "The SOLO taxonomy is not a model of cognitive development. We merely advocate a mix of assessment exercises" and testing "at all levels of the SOLO taxonomy" | [Lister et al. 2006](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/courses/compsci747s2c/lectures/p118-lister.pdf)
- Reliability (Clear et al. 2008): Kendall's W with three raters 0.96 (array sum), 0.55 (nested-loop square), 0.61 (binary search); augmented codes "Relational Error" and "Multistructural Omission/Error" were needed; conclusion "SOLO ratings can be applied with moderate levels of consistency." Disagreement came from category boundaries, students' English, and whether a student guessed the purpose | [Clear et al. 2008](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/12626/1/2008001537.pdf)
- Whalley, Lister, Thompson, Clear, Robbins, Kumar, Prasad, "An Australasian Study of Reading and Comprehension Skills in Novice Programmers, using the Bloom and SOLO Taxonomies," ACE 2006 (Australian Computer Science Communications 52, pp. 243-252): a multi-institutional question set built within both taxonomies; "the Bloom and SOLO taxonomies are the most widely adopted taxonomies to date in computer science education. However, applying Bloom's taxonomy to programming problems proves to be a challenging process" | [AUT record](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/handle/10292/15405); [UTS copy of the 2008 follow-up](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/12626/1/2008001537.pdf)
- Thompson, Luxton-Reilly, Whalley, Hu, Robbins, "Bloom's Taxonomy for CS Assessment," ACE 2008, CRPIT 78, pp. 155-162: "Bloom's Taxonomy is difficult to apply consistently to assessment tasks in introductory programming courses"; the paper gives "a consistent interpretation with concrete exemplars" per category | [DigitalNZ record](https://digitalnz.org/records/30158531)
- Xie et al. map their four skills onto SOLO: tracing is "comparable to SOLO multistructural," template comprehension to "SOLO relational"; they define "code explanation ... as recognizing templates and their uses" | [Xie et al. 2019](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2019IntroCSTheoryOfInstruction.pdf)
- Duran, Sorva, Leite, "Towards an Analysis of Program Complexity From a Cognitive Perspective," ICER 2018, DOI 10.1145/3230977.3230986: "there is a dearth of established methodologies for assessing the complexity of a program from a learning point of view"; the framework draws on Cognitive Load Theory and the Model of Hierarchical Complexity "in order to extend Soloway's plan-based analysis of programs and apply it at a fine level of granularity"; CCCP "generates metrics for two aspects of a program: plan depth and maximal plan interactivity. Plan depth reflects the overall complexity of the cognitive schemas that are required for reasoning about the program, and maximal plan interactivity reflects the complexity of interactions between schemas that arise from program composition"; applied "to illustrate why one program or construct is more complex than another, to identify dependencies between constructs that a novice programmer needs to learn and to contrast the complexity of different strategies for program composition"; the authors "discuss the upcoming work to validate and refine the CCCP" | [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1145/3230977.3230986?fields=title,authors,venue,year,abstract,citationCount)
- How CLT is used in CER (Duran, Zavgorodniaia, Sorva, "Cognitive Load Theory in Computing Education Research: A Review," ACM TOCE 2022, DOI 10.1145/3483843): "the most common reason to cite CLT is to mention it briefly as a design influence; authors predominantly cite old versions of the theory; hypotheses phrased in terms of cognitive load components are rare; and only a small selection of cognitive load measures have been applied, sparsely" | [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F3483843)
- Weinman's design-goal rubric for exercise formats (practice, vocabulary, process, one solution, efficiency) is a working alternative to Bloom for judging a format | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)

### Inferences
- SOLO is the usable one for a solutions folder: a model answer to an explain exercise should be the relational statement first ("checks whether the array is sorted"), with the multistructural trace available as a second layer for readers who could not get there; this matches the Fowler 2021 interviewees' practice of showing both side by side as training.
- CCCP's two metrics give a vocabulary for calibrating exercises in a pattern chapter: plan depth rises when a pattern nests a second mechanism (a descriptor inside a metaclass), interactivity rises when two plans share state (an observer that also mutates the subject). It is unvalidated, so it is a checklist for the author, not a claim to print.
- Bloom's "create" level is where this book's write-from-spec exercises sit; the taxonomy literature's warning is that classifying tasks by Bloom level is unreliable, so a chapter should not label its exercises by level.

### Gaps
- Thompson et al. 2008's per-level exemplars and the Whalley 2006 question set were not retrieved in full text.
- No validation study of CCCP against student difficulty data was found.

## Key question 10: Difficulty calibration and number of exercises per concept

### Takeaway
CER has no finding that sets a number of exercises per concept.
The closest evidence: many small programs (5+ per week) beat one large one on stress and exam coding performance in CS1 (Vahid and Allen); spacing three problems over three quizzes retained better than three on one quiz; and the subgoal and Parsons literatures each use three worked examples with practice per concept and two to five exposure exercises per pattern, by convention rather than by test.
The 50% simple-tracing threshold (Venables 2009) is the only calibration point with data.

### Cited Findings
- Many small programs (Allen, Vahid, et al., ASEE 2018/2019): MSPs assign "multiple programming assignments (such as 5 or more) each week instead of one large programming assignment"; "MSPs can improve the student experience by reducing stress and increasing student satisfaction, and have been shown to improve student grade performance in CS1, especially on the coding portion of exams"; students "complete more MSPs than required, and use MSPs more to study for exams" | [ASEE PEER record](https://peer.asee.org/many-small-programs-in-cs1-usage-analysis-from-multiple-universities)
- Spacing versus massing (NSF-funded engineering-course study; discipline of the course not confirmed in the snippet): "students worked problems on various learning objectives in either massed fashion (3 problems on a single quiz) or spaced fashion (3 problems across 3 quizzes), with spacing increasing retention of learning objectives on an end-of-semester test" | [NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10352318)
- Interleaving, mixed: "On tests of short-term retention, interleaved practice increased test scores by 0.29 standard deviations, while in contrast, there was no evidence that interleaving improves average performance on a cumulative assessment measuring retention of material over the academic year" (a Nigerian mathematics program, not programming) | [NBER w31853](https://nber.org/papers/w31853)
- A CS1 retrieval-practice intervention (spaced, interleaved low-stakes quizzes with reflection): "a statistically significant increase in all grades evaluated" (single-site, with visualization and reflection confounds) | [Mountain Scholar record](https://mountainscholar.org/items/55f9d5e8-8575-45e5-962b-bb563f71d9b4)
- Spaced practice in Ericson's own tool: "each hour of use was associated with more than a 1% increase in final exam grade" (YeckehZaare, Resnick, Ericson 2019) | [Ericson and Hou 2024 slides](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2024/01/January-2024-seminar-slides.pdf)
- Dose used in the subgoal studies: three worked examples, each followed by one practice problem, then four novel problems and one Parsons | [Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)
- Dose used in the faded-Parsons pattern study: five exposure exercises per pattern (study 1) or two (study 2), then a writing-only acquisition phase; the authors list "only two exercises in Study 2" and "consecutive same-pattern exercises that may boost acquisition" as limitations | [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)
- Time per item: a Parsons or write-code problem averages about five minutes in prior work; Haynes-Magyar's per-problem load ratings differed across problems (Friedman χ²(10)=35.48, p<.001), with one problem bimodal (55.6% rated it 7-8 of 9), which the author reads as a mismatch between the learner's approach and the scaffold rather than intrinsic difficulty | [Haynes-Magyar 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22407)
- The tracing threshold as a calibration point: 50% on simple tracing is "a necessary condition ... but it is not a sufficient condition"; above it, writing correlates only weakly with tracing (R²=0.03 to 0.23) | [Venables et al. 2009](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/11384/1/2009002706OK.pdf)
- Expertise reversal as the calibration risk for scaffolded formats: "at what point can Parsons problems become harmful (e.g. due to the expertise reversal effect)" and "work should also be done to determine how to best fade that scaffolding as expertise develops" | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Xie et al.'s open question: "How much practice each skill needs; precursory skills may need less" | [Xie et al. 2019](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2019IntroCSTheoryOfInstruction.pdf)
- EiPE size rule (Fowler 2021): too small and levels are indistinguishable, too large and the code cannot be abstracted; the behavior "should not be guessable from one line" | [Fowler et al. 2021](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/how_to_eipe_icer_2021.pdf)

### Inferences
- The literature supports several short exercises over one long one, and spreading a concept's exercises across later chapters over clustering them, but it gives no count. The conventions in the best-controlled studies (three examples plus practice; two to five exposures per pattern; about five minutes each) are the only anchors.
- Calibration by format rather than by count has data behind it: trace and explain before write for anything new at runtime; completion before generation for a new idiom; comparison once two implementations exist. Difficulty then comes from the chapter's own content (CCCP's plan depth), not from exercise count.

### Gaps
- No CER study varies the number of exercises per concept and measures learning.
- No study of exercise sequencing or dosage for self-study readers of a book; every study is a course with deadlines or a timed lab.

## Sequencing frameworks that order these formats

### Takeaway
Three frameworks order the formats: Xie et al.'s four-skill theory (trace, write syntax, read templates, write with templates: "Reading before writing, semantics ... before templates"), PRIMM/Use-Modify-Create (predict and run, investigate, modify, make), and the worked-example-to-problem fade (example, completion, problem, with comparison once two solutions exist).
The direct evidence for any fixed order is small (Xie: n=9; PRIMM: one school quasi-experiment) and the correlational hierarchy behind them did not replicate cleanly at scale.

### Cited Findings
- Xie, Loksa, Nelson, Davidson, Dong, Kwik, Tan, Hwa, Li, Ko, "A theory of instruction for introductory programming skills," Computer Science Education 29(2-3), 2019, pp. 205-253. Four skills: S1 "the ability to accurately trace code and predict the effect of syntax on program behavior"; S2 "translating of unambiguous natural language descriptions of language constructs into syntax that will compile and execute as expected"; S3 "identifying reusable abstractions of programming knowledge (which we will refer to as templates) and mapping them to an objective"; S4 "start with a problem description that contains ambiguity, identify a template that they could use to solve the problem, and implement each component of the template in code." Claims: C1 "Tracing code is a different and precursory skill to writing syntactically correct code"; C2 "Understanding the features of a programming language is different than solving a problem with code"; C3 "Comprehending code templates is a different skill than using templates to write code." "Our theory recommends reading before writing (as substantiated by C1, C3) and semantics before templates (as substantiated by C2)." Reasoning for not teaching templates first: it "can result in a problem-specific understanding of what constructs can do" | [Xie et al. 2019](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2019IntroCSTheoryOfInstruction.pdf)
- Their exercise formats per skill: S1 trace tables, control-flow annotation, crossing out unexecuted lines; S2 translate a sentence to code, bad-code/fixed-code rule tables; S3 decide whether code correctly implements a named template and which part fails, explain in plain English; S4 write a plan in plain English, then code, then comment each line | [Xie et al. 2019](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2019IntroCSTheoryOfInstruction.pdf)
- Their evidence: one exploratory study, n=9 rank novices (5 experimental, 4 control), 3-hour paper packet. "we found no significant difference between the performance of the control and experimental group participants"; experimental participants "made fewer errors on the post-test, especially in the later skills"; "the experimental group demonstrated higher level understanding at least twice as often as the control group did" (SOLO coding of 186 responses). The authors: "the sample size is not large enough to make quantitative claims"; "the most salient part of this theory is not necessarily the four specific skills we defined, but rather the emphasis on being rigorous and explicit in how skills are defined and ordered" | [Xie et al. 2019](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2019IntroCSTheoryOfInstruction.pdf)
- Their own caution on transfer: S1 and S2 "likely transfer poorly" across languages, S3 and S4 better (Linn and Dalbey's chain) | [Xie et al. 2019](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Xie2019IntroCSTheoryOfInstruction.pdf)
- Zavala and Mendoza 2017 ("Precursor Skills to Writing Code"), as summarized in the Parsons review: results "suggesting that code comprehension, code manipulation, and code writing are phases that students should sequentially master" | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)
- Fowler et al. 2022 "concluded that optimal order of instruction should be studied directly rather than via any skills hierarchies" | [working group report](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf); [Fowler et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10340072)
- The worked-example fade: example study, then faded steps, then problem solving (Renkl and Atkinson), with the order reversing to problem-then-example for high-prior-knowledge learners | [Expertise reversal effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)
- PRIMM's order and UMC's ownership transfer, as above | [Sentance 2021](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2022/08/Teaching_Programming_with_PRIMM-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- The three frameworks agree on one ordering for a new construct: predict/trace it, explain it, complete or modify a given program with it, then write a new one; comparison of two implementations comes last, once two exist. Where they disagree is dosage and whether the order should be strict, and the strongest evidence (Fowler 2022; Venables 2009's "develop in parallel") says not strict.
- For intermediate readers the templates-before-semantics warning in Xie cuts the other way: the reader already has semantics for most constructs, so a chapter can open with the template (the pattern) and reserve the trace-and-predict step for the one construct whose runtime behavior is new.

### Gaps
- No experimental test of instruction order exists at any level (Fowler et al. 2022 call for one).
- The Cambridge Handbook of Computing Education Research (Fincher and Robins 2019) and Ko's open book were not retrieved; the handbook's pedagogy chapters may synthesize these same sources but I could not confirm their content.
