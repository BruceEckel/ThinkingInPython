# Cognitive-science principles for designing exercises and solutions in a programming textbook

Scope note.
These notes cover the learning-science evidence on how practice exercises and their solutions should be structured,
read for a textbook author (print, EPUB, website) with end-of-chapter exercises and a separate solutions file,
no live instructor, no adaptive system.
Each finding carries a domain tag:
[verbal] for word lists, paired associates, and prose passages;
[math/science] for worked problems in mathematics, physics, circuits, and the like;
[programming] for code-writing or code-reading tasks.
Transfer from [verbal] to code exercises is not automatic, and several sections say where the evidence thins out.
Most "cited findings" below come from search-result abstracts and secondary summaries of the primary paper, with the primary linked where it was found.
Where a number could not be confirmed against the primary paper, it is in Gaps rather than Cited Findings.

## Key question 1: Worked examples, the expertise-reversal effect, and faded/completion sequencing

### Takeaway
For a novice, studying a fully worked solution beats solving the problem (medium effect, about g = 0.48 in mathematics across 55 studies),
and the advantage reverses once the learner has domain experience, at which point problem solving wins.
The best-supported sequencing is example first, then examples with steps removed, then full problems;
example-then-problem pairs beat problem-then-example pairs, and fading beats pairs on near transfer.

### Cited Findings
- [math/science] A 2023 meta-analysis of the worked-example effect on mathematics performance (Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste, Chawla; Educational Psychology Review) screened 8,033 abstracts down to 43 articles (41 experimental, 2 quasi-experimental), 55 studies, and 181 effect sizes, and found an average effect of g = 0.48, about 18 percentile points, for learners from elementary school through adulthood studying worked examples against standard practice such as solving practice problems. [Kent State OAKS record](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [University of Delaware summary](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/in-the-news-christina-barbieri/)
- [math/science] In the same meta-analysis, correct examples alone produced larger effects than incorrect examples alone or mixed correct-plus-incorrect sets; faded solution steps was the design feature with the strongest effect in favor of worked examples, followed by self-explanation training; and adding self-explanation prompts to worked examples was a negative moderator relative to worked examples without prompts. [University of Delaware summary](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/in-the-news-christina-barbieri/); [poster PDF (binary; not parsed)](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf)
- [math/science] A separate meta-analysis of learning from erroneous examples found a statistically significant but weak effect, g = 0.136. [TU Dresden record](https://fis.tu-dresden.de/portal/de/publications/a-framework-for-learning-from-erroneous-examples-and-metaanalysis-of-empirical-research(113f115d-59d4-43c3-b1c8-52c3c97b0b40).html)
- [math/science, trade training] Kalyuga, Chandler, Tuovinen, and Sweller (2001, Journal of Educational Psychology 93(3), 579-588, "When problem solving is superior to studying worked examples") gave mechanical trade apprentices either worked examples or problems to solve. Inexperienced trainees did best with worked examples, with lower rated mental load; with more experience in the domain, worked examples became redundant and problem solving proved superior. [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/1405548/When_problem_solving_is); [UOW repository](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/139/)
- [cross-domain review] Kalyuga's 2007 review (Educational Psychology Review, "Expertise reversal effect and its implications for learner-tailored instruction") defines the effect as instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners losing their effectiveness, and even producing negative consequences, with more experienced learners; it covers the empirical findings since the mid-1990s, the cognitive-load interpretation, the relation to aptitude-treatment interactions, and attempts at adaptive implementations. [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-007-9054-3); [PDF copy](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)
- [multimedia] Reviews of multimedia learning report the same pattern: formats effective for low-knowledge learners are often ineffective or harmful for high-knowledge learners. [UNSW record](https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/entities/publication/c26cbc05-47ae-4c02-be87-97ab0e0dca70)
- [math/science] Renkl and Atkinson ("Structuring the transition from example study to problem solving in cognitive skill acquisition: a cognitive load perspective") argue that example-problem pairs are static, propose fading (complete example, then increasingly incomplete examples, then a full problem), and report that fading beat example-problem pairs, at least on near transfer. Fading out steps also triggers self-explanation of the given steps. [ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/structuring-the-transition-from-example-study-to-problem-solving--2); [PsychArchives](https://www.psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.11780/196); [Wikipedia summary of the worked-example literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worked-example_effect)
- [math/science, circuits] Van Gog, Kester, and Paas (2011, Contemporary Educational Psychology 36(3), 212-218) compared worked examples only, example-problem pairs, problem-example pairs, and problem solving for novices troubleshooting faulty electrical circuits. Example-problem pairs took less learning effort and produced better test performance than problem-example pairs; alternating examples with problems was not necessary (examples alone did as well), but if alternating, example first. [Open University record](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/effects-of-worked-examples-example-problem-pairs-and-problem-exam/); [Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/26205)
- [programming] Parsons problems (the correct code supplied as scrambled blocks the learner orders and indents) sit between a worked example and a from-scratch problem. Ericson's studies report that Parsons problems saved 15 to 17 minutes (43.6% to 65.2% of total time) on 30-minute lab assignments, with students performing just as well on later assignments as those who wrote the code. [Georgia Tech record](https://hg.gatech.edu/node/603172)
- [programming] Students who used Parsons problems as scaffolding for code writing reached higher practice performance and efficiency than students who wrote code without help, with the same posttest scores; the authors frame Parsons problems as a balance between the low cognitive load of worked examples and the higher engagement of problem solving. [NSF PAR, Parsons scaffolding and self-efficacy](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10510497-understanding-effects-using-parsons-problems-scaffold-code-writing-students-varying-cs-self-efficacy-levels); [NSF PAR, worked examples vs Parsons](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10510493)
- [cross-domain] MIT Open Learning's research-based teaching summary treats worked and faded examples as an established finding and recommends the fade. [MIT Open Learning](https://openlearning.mit.edu/mit-faculty/research-based-learning-findings/worked-and-faded-examples)

### Inferences
- A chapter's listings are the worked examples. The exercise set should begin where the listings leave off: the first exercise or two can be completion problems (a listing with a function body or a step removed), and later exercises full problems. Across the book, the proportion of full problems can rise with the reader's growing expertise (chapter 2 heavy on completion, chapter 40 nearly all full problems).
- The expertise-reversal finding means a solutions file that opens with the full solution for every exercise is right for the reader who is a novice on that topic and wrong for the reader who is not. A reveal-in-steps layout (hint, then shape, then full solution) lets each reader stop at the level that matches their expertise. That is a book-implementable approximation of adaptive instruction.
- The negative moderator for self-explanation prompts on worked examples (Barbieri) and the positive meta-analytic effect of self-explanation in general (Bisra, question 5) conflict. A plausible reading, consistent with cognitive-load theory, is that prompts added to an already demanding worked example overload a novice; prompts that replace a removed step (fading) do not. Prefer "fill the missing step" over "explain this step" in a book.
- Erroneous examples are weak on average. If a chapter shows a wrong version of code, show the correct version alongside it and label which is which; a solutions file should not rely on "spot the bug" as its main form.
- Parsons-style exercises are hard to deliver in print but easy on a website. On the printed page the equivalent is a listing with its lines given in the wrong order, or a shape block with bodies removed, which the book's existing "The shape" step already resembles.

### Gaps
- Barbieri et al.'s per-moderator effect sizes (how much stronger faded steps were; the size of the self-explanation penalty; near vs far transfer; immediate vs delayed test) were not retrieved; the poster PDF did not parse and the journal page was not fetched.
- No meta-analysis of the worked-example effect in programming was found. The programming evidence is single studies (Parsons problems, subgoal labels) and transfers from mathematics and circuit troubleshooting.
- Coppens et al. (2019, Instructional Science) replicated the example-problem vs problem-example comparison; its result was not retrieved. [Utrecht repository](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/handle/1874/384159/Coppens2019_Article_EffectsOfProblemExampleAndExam.pdf?sequence=1)
- Wittwer and Renkl (2010, Educational Psychology Review 22(4), 393-409, "How effective are instructional explanations in example-based learning? A meta-analytic review") covered 21 studies and 28 comparisons and found minimal overall benefit from adding instructional explanations to worked examples, with more benefit for conceptual than procedural knowledge and no significant effect of explanation type or self-explanation prompts. The primary page was not retrieved, so this is reported from a search summary and belongs in Gaps until confirmed. Renkl's related argument that instruction should move from instructional explanations toward self-explanation prompts is at [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Alexander-Renkl/publication/252065774_Learning_from_worked-out_examples_The_transition_from_instructional_explanations_to_self-explanation_prompts/links/0c96053bbcc1ac1622000000/Learning-from-worked-out-examples-The-transition-from-instructional-explanations-to-self-explanation-prompts.pdf).

## Key question 2: Retrieval practice and the testing effect

### Takeaway
Retrieving from memory beats re-reading by a medium effect (g about 0.50 to 0.61 across three large meta-analyses, over 200 classroom studies in the newest),
the advantage appears only on delayed tests,
and it depends on the learner succeeding at retrieval or getting corrective feedback.
Whether it survives complex, high-element-interactivity material such as code is contested.

### Cited Findings
- [verbal, lab] Rowland (2014, Psychological Bulletin) meta-analyzed 159 effect sizes from 61 studies and found a testing effect of g = 0.50 against restudy. Feedback (mostly presenting the correct answer) increased the effect; there was no testing effect in laboratory experiments without corrective feedback when initial retrievability was at or below 50%; short-answer (recall) formats produced stronger effects than multiple choice. Rowland excluded applied classroom research. [Frontiers summary of both meta-analyses](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full); [Rowland PDF](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- [mixed, including classroom] Adesope, Trevisan, and Sundararajan (2017, Review of Educational Research) meta-analyzed 272 effect sizes from 118 articles and 15,427 participants and found practice tests beat other conditions by g = 0.61, with g = 0.67 in classroom settings. Unlike Rowland, multiple-choice produced stronger effects than short answer, and matching the practice format to the final-test format increased the effect. [Frontiers summary](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)
- [classroom, many subjects] Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu, and Shanks (2021, Psychological Bulletin 147(4), 399-435) integrated 48,478 students from 222 independent classroom studies and found quizzing raises achievement by g = 0.499. Moderators included the control condition's strategy, test-format consistency, material match, corrective feedback, number of repetitions, where and when the test was given, treatment duration, and design. They support three mechanisms: additional exposure, transfer-appropriate processing, and motivation. [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/); [PDF copy](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- [verbal, prose passages] Roediger and Karpicke (2006, Psychological Science) had students study prose passages then either take one or three free-recall tests without feedback or restudy the same number of times. After 5 minutes, restudy won; after 2 days and 1 week, prior testing produced substantially greater retention, even though repeated study had increased students' confidence that they would remember. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/); [PDF copy](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)
- [classroom, psychology lectures] A 2018 within-subjects study of 92 undergraduates across seven lecture sessions found a testing effect for short-answer questions on retrievable material (β = 0.44, p = 0.033, one-tailed), no effect for multiple-choice, and the short-answer effect only for high-retrievability items (46-81% initial difficulty), not medium or low. [Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)
- [verbal, general knowledge] Butler, Karpicke, and Roediger (2007) had participants read passages, take a six-alternative multiple-choice test, and receive feedback either immediately or after a delay, as either the correct answer or answer-until-correct. On a delayed cued-recall test, delayed feedback beat immediate feedback; feedback type made no difference. The authors attribute the delayed advantage to spaced re-presentation. [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-type-and-timing-of-feedback-on-learning-from-multip/)
- [contested boundary condition] Van Gog and Sweller (2015, Educational Psychology Review, "Not new, but nearly forgotten: the testing effect decreases or even disappears as the complexity of learning materials increases") argue that the number of interacting information elements in a task limits the testing effect, citing century-old evidence. Karpicke and Aue's reply in the same issue ("The testing effect is alive and well with complex materials") says element interactivity was never defined measurably and was applied inconsistently in the review. [Springer, van Gog and Sweller](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3); [Erasmus record](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/not-new-but-nearly-forgotten-the-testing-effect-decreases-or-even/); [Didau's summary of the exchange](https://learningspy.co.uk/featured/the-testing-effect-is-dead-long-live-the-testing-effect)
- [cross-domain review] Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan, and Willingham (2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest) evaluated ten techniques and gave practice testing and distributed practice the highest utility ratings, with effectiveness shown across ages and kinds of tests; summarization, highlighting/underlining, rereading, keyword mnemonics, and imagery for text got low ratings. [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [Kent State release](https://www.kent.edu/node/295736)
- [programming, thin] Retrieval practice has been applied in embedded-systems and data-science courses (warm-up quizzes, polling, "brain dumps"), with reports of improved retention, but these are course reports rather than controlled comparisons of code-writing retrieval against restudy. [ASEE, embedded systems course](https://peer.asee.org/embedding-better-learning-retrieval-practice-in-an-embedded-systems-course); [arXiv, LLM-generated retrieval questions in data-science courses](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.05629)

### Inferences
- An end-of-chapter exercise is a retrieval event only if the reader attempts it before opening the solution. The solutions file should be physically and navigationally separate (it is), and the book can say so in one sentence at the top of each exercise set: attempt first, then read.
- Retrieval must succeed, or be followed by corrective feedback, for the effect to appear (Rowland's 50% boundary). For a reader working alone, the solution is the feedback. That argues for every exercise having a solution, and against exercises whose solution is "left to the reader."
- Rowland's recall-beats-recognition result and Adesope's format-match result together favor exercises that ask the reader to produce code, not pick among options, when the real-world task is producing code. Multiple-choice "which output does this print" questions fit only if output prediction is the skill being trained.
- The van Gog and Sweller caveat bears directly on code. A full program is a high-element-interactivity task. Where the material is complex, the safer design is a retrieval exercise scoped to one element (write the one method, name the dunder that fires) rather than "write the whole thing from memory," at least until the chapter's later exercises.
- Delayed tests show the effect and immediate ones hide it (Roediger and Karpicke). A reader who checks the solution immediately and feels they "knew it" is seeing the 5-minute condition.

### Gaps
- No controlled study comparing retrieval practice to restudy on code-writing tasks was found. The programming reports are course-level implementations.
- The disagreement between Rowland and Adesope on multiple-choice vs short-answer is unresolved in the sources found; Rowland excluded applied studies, Adesope included them, which may explain it.

## Key question 3: Spacing and interleaving

### Takeaway
Spacing study of the same material across sessions improves retention, with the best gap growing with how long the material must be remembered.
Interleaving problem types (rather than blocking them) helps learners discriminate which method a problem calls for, with a moderate average effect (g = 0.42 across 59 studies) and large heterogeneity;
the strongest classroom evidence is a mathematics RCT with d = 0.83.

### Cited Findings
- [verbal] Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, and Rohrer (2006, Psychological Bulletin 132(3), 354-380) reviewed 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments from 184 articles. Spaced beat massed presentation, and the inter-study interval that maximized retention increased as the retention interval increased. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/); [PDF copy](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf)
- [cross-domain] Brunmair and Richter (2019, Psychological Bulletin, "Similarity matters: a meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators") analyzed 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples, and found a moderate interleaving effect of g = 0.42 with substantial heterogeneity; interleaving worked best for paintings and other visual materials, and the advantage over blocking appears in some situations only, depending on the similarity of the categories being discriminated. [Würzburg PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- [math/science, classroom RCT] Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig, and Cheung (2020, Journal of Educational Psychology) ran a preregistered cluster-randomized trial with 787 grade-7 students in 54 classes, 15 teachers, 5 schools in one Florida district. A higher dose of interleaved practice raised scores on an unannounced test from 38% (blocked) to 61% (interleaved), d = 0.83, with a positive effect for every teacher. Limitations: one country, age group, and subject; every teacher reported mixed worksheets took longer; interleaving was tested with feedback and after students had some grounding in each method. [WWC study record](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770); [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2021/10/28-1); [Structural Learning briefing](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/mix-it-up); [IES replication award](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/systematic-replication-study-interleaved-mathematics-practice)
- [math/science] An elementary-school mathematics study found interleaved learning improved flexible and adaptive use of subtraction strategies. [Frontiers in Psychology 2019 corrigendum record](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6813467/)
- [cross-domain review] Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated distributed practice high utility and interleaved practice moderate utility. [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- [cross-domain] Bjork and Bjork list interleaving separate topics, spacing sessions on one topic, varying conditions of practice, and testing as the canonical desirable difficulties. [Bjork and Bjork 2011 PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- A book cannot schedule the reader's sessions, but it can space content: an exercise in chapter N that requires a technique from chapter N-3, written so the reader must recognize that the earlier technique applies. The Brunmair-Richter "similarity matters" result says interleaving pays most when the alternatives are confusable, so interleave the things readers mix up (for this book: the pattern chapters, where *State* vs *Strategy* vs *Command* are structurally similar), not arbitrary pairs.
- Blocked practice, where every exercise under a heading uses that heading's technique, removes the discrimination step: the reader never has to decide which tool fits. One or two exercises per chapter that do not announce their technique restores it.
- Rohrer's "took longer" and "after some grounding" caveats translate to: interleave after the chapter's own blocked exercises, not instead of them, and accept that the interleaved exercise feels harder.
- Cepeda's result implies that a cumulative exercise late in the book (one that revisits a chapter-5 technique in chapter 30) is worth more for long-term retention than a second exercise on the same technique in chapter 5.

### Gaps
- No interleaving study in programming was found. The Rohrer RCT is mathematics; the meta-analytic heterogeneity means the g = 0.42 may not hold for code.
- Cepeda's optimal-gap ratios (the specific ISI-to-retention-interval percentages) were not extracted from the sources found.

## Key question 4: Desirable difficulties and productive failure

### Takeaway
Difficulties that make the learner generate, retrieve, or discriminate help; difficulties that overload working memory do not.
Problem-solving before instruction (productive failure) has a supportive meta-analysis across 53 studies in math and science,
and a direct experimental contradiction (Ashman, Kalyuga, Sweller 2020) when the material has high element interactivity, which is the normal case for code.

### Cited Findings
- [cross-domain] Bjork and Bjork (2011, "Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way") define desirable difficulties as conditions that trigger the encoding and retrieval processes that support learning, comprehension, and remembering: varying study conditions, interleaving, spacing, and using tests rather than presentations as learning events. [Bjork lab PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf); [Bjork and Bjork 2020 JARMAC update](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2021/01/RABjorkELBjorkJARMAC2020ForPostingSingleSpaced.pdf)
- [math/science, medicine] Sinha and Kapur (2021, Review of Educational Research) meta-analyzed 53 studies with 166 comparisons on whether instruction should precede problem solving or follow it, across school and university students in mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology, and medicine. Press coverage reports problem-solving-first as "two to three times more effective." They name four mechanisms: activation of prior knowledge, awareness of the gap between what the learner knows and does not, affect (receptiveness), and assembly (the instructor's explanation that resolves the problem and shows why the learner's attempts fell short). [World Economic Forum summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/students-who-productively-fail-learn-more/); [Education Today summary](https://www.educationtoday.com.au/news-detail/Have-we-been-getting-5403); [Futurity summary](https://futurity.org/?p=2629782)
- [math/science, contradicting] Ashman, Kalyuga, and Sweller (2020, Educational Psychology Review, "Problem-solving or explicit instruction: which should go first when element interactivity is high?") randomized learners to problem-solving-then-lecture or lecture-then-problem-solving. The lecture-first group scored significantly higher on both an application test and a transfer test, with mean scores almost 50% higher. They identify element interactivity (how many concepts a task's elements connect to and depend on) as the variable deciding whether a problem-first advantage appears. [NYU IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2019/journal-article-abstracts/s10648-019-09500-5/); [Chen, Kalyuga, Sweller, "When instructional guidance is needed"](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/When_instructional_guidance_is_needed/12052683/1)
- [math/science] Chen, Kalyuga, and Sweller, "The worked example effect, the generation effect and element interactivity" (Journal of Educational Psychology), is the paper that frames the generation effect and the worked-example effect as two ends of one variable: generation helps when element interactivity is low, worked examples help when it is high. [Loughborough repository](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/The_worked_example_effect_the_generation_effect_and_element_interactivity/12053721)
- [math/science, trade] Kalyuga et al. 2001 (question 1) is the same boundary from the other side: struggle (problem solving) beats examples once the learner has experience.
- [cross-domain] Schwartz and Bransford (1998, Cognition and Instruction, "A time for telling") found that a lecture is useful when it follows meaningful and relevant experience with the material; the experience has to be relevant to what the lecture then explains. [Wright State PDF](https://www.wright.edu/sites/www.wright.edu/files/uploads/2017/Feb/event/Schwartz_Bransford_1998_TimeForTelling.pdf)
- [cross-domain] Bransford and Schwartz (1999) proposed "preparation for future learning" (PFL): assess whether an experience prepares learners to learn from later resources, not only whether they can apply it at once. [NSF PAR reference](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10214147)
- [classroom, single study] A single-study report compared success-driven, failure-driven, and productive-failure groups and found the failure-driven group had an initial dip during problem solving but the highest reasoning quality and the highest transfer posttest score. [Columbia EPIC summary](https://epic.tc.columbia.edu/research/academic-alert/blog-posts/2022/is-failure-necessary-for-problem-solving)

### Inferences
- The two literatures agree on more than the press suggests. Both say struggle followed by a canonical solution is better than struggle alone, and both say the solution has to be explained against the learner's own attempt. The disagreement is about order for complex material, and the Sweller camp's experimental result for high-interactivity material (explicit first) is the one that matches code.
- For a textbook, the chapter is the instruction and the exercises follow it, so the book already sits on the explicit-first side. Productive failure can still be used inside an exercise set: pose a problem that the chapter's technique solves badly or not at all, then have the solution show why, which delivers Kapur's "awareness" and "assembly" mechanisms without reordering the book.
- A "Preparation for future learning" exercise is one whose solution the next chapter supersedes. The exercise can say so, which also justifies the reader's effort.
- Difficulty is desirable only if the learner can eventually succeed. A hint step before the solution keeps the difficulty from becoming the undesirable kind.

### Gaps
- Sinha and Kapur's overall Hedges' g and its moderator breakdown (fidelity of implementation, age, conceptual vs procedural outcomes) were not retrieved; the SAGE page returned 403 and the press summaries report only the study count and "two to three times" framing. Treat the press figures as the authors' framing, not a measured effect size.
- No productive-failure study in programming was found.

## Key question 5: Self-explanation, elaborative interrogation, and subgoal labels

### Takeaway
Prompting learners to explain material to themselves has a medium effect (g = 0.55 across 64 reports, about 6,000 participants) and beats being given an explanation;
but adding self-explanation prompts to worked examples in mathematics was a negative moderator, so the form of the prompt matters.
Subgoal labels on worked examples improve novel problem solving in programming in lab studies, with mixed classroom replication and a persistence benefit for weaker students.

### Cited Findings
- [cross-domain] Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi, and Winne (2018, Educational Psychology Review, "Inducing self-explanation: a meta-analysis") found 69 effect sizes from 64 reports (about 6,000 participants) and a random-effects mean of g = 0.55. Self-explanation beat instructor explanation, held across materials (problem solving, worked examples), learning types (conceptual, procedural), and topics (math, science, social science), and the benefit was not explained by extra time on task. [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies); [TUM Clearinghouse summary PDF](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/CHU-KR-25_ENG_Bisra_2018_Selbsterklaerungen.pdf); [PDF copy](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
- [math/science, conflicting] Barbieri et al. (2023) found self-explanation prompts added to worked examples reduced the worked-example advantage relative to examples without prompts, while self-explanation *training* (teaching the skill beforehand) was the second-strongest positive design feature after fading. [University of Delaware summary](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/in-the-news-christina-barbieri/)
- [math/science] Renkl's position is that fading solution steps triggers the learner's own explanations of the given steps, and that instruction should move from supplying explanations toward prompting self-explanation. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Alexander-Renkl/publication/252065774_Learning_from_worked-out_examples_The_transition_from_instructional_explanations_to_self-explanation_prompts/links/0c96053bbcc1ac1622000000/Learning-from-worked-out-examples-The-transition-from-instructional-explanations-to-self-explanation-prompts.pdf)
- [cross-domain review] Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated self-explanation and elaborative interrogation ("why would this be true?") as moderate utility, below practice testing and spacing. [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- [programming, lab] Margulieux and Catrambone found that learners given subgoal labels in both the instructional text and the worked example solved novel problems better than those without; labels in the text helped learners articulate the procedure, labels in the example helped them apply it. [GSU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/items/80aa2cc7-cce3-4d9f-8b7c-c6e21bd3cb58); [GSU faculty publications](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/ltd_facpub/4)
- [programming, lab] Across their subgoal-learning studies in programming, constructive activities (learners generating labels) produced better problem solving than active or passive ones, and the intervention changed how learners approached problems. [GSU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/items/0238d58e-dc98-4924-8f07-3871e0094393); [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10099373)
- [programming, replication] Morrison, Margulieux, and Guzdial (2015, ICER, best paper) tested subgoal labels for writing `while` loops in a textual language with three conditions (no labels, labels given, labels generated). Performance gains did not replicate as expected; results were mixed and depended on other features of the materials. [GSU ScholarWorks PDF](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f40a79f2-edaf-49fb-b0cb-05451bd21bac/content); [ACM DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/2787622.2787733)
- [programming, classroom] Decker, Margulieux, and Morrison ran subgoal-labeled instruction across a semester-long Java CS1 course. Students who learned with subgoals produced higher-rated (SOLO taxonomy) explanations on three of four quizzes and were three times as likely to discuss data type on an assignment-and-expressions question; performance on early applications improved; variance was lower and course persistence higher, suggesting the benefit concentrates in students who would otherwise fail or drop out. [NSF PAR, SOLO study](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171478); [Buffalo record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/using-the-solo-taxonomy-to-understand-subgoal-labels-effect-in-cs/); [Raspberry Pi summary](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf)

### Inferences
- The safe form of self-explanation in a book is structural, not a question appended to a solution. A solution that is divided into labeled subgoals ("parse the input", "build the lookup", "emit the report") gives the reader the labels Margulieux found helpful, and a shape step that removes the bodies under those labels asks the reader to supply what the label names.
- "Explain why this step is needed" prompts after a full solution match the condition that hurt in Barbieri's meta-analysis. If the book wants explanation, ask it before the solution (as part of the exercise) or make the explanation the thing the reader must produce to complete a faded step.
- Subgoal labels are cheap to add to a solutions file: a one-line comment or a prose line before each function or block. The CS1 evidence says the gain goes mostly to weaker readers, which matches a book that has both audiences.
- Elaborative interrogation ("why would a frozen dataclass block this?") fits conceptual exercises better than code-writing ones, per Dunlosky's moderate rating and Wittwer and Renkl's conceptual-over-procedural finding.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests self-explanation prompts on programming solutions specifically. The Bisra meta-analysis spans domains but the summaries do not break out programming.
- The Morrison et al. 2015 paper's specific numbers and which "other features" mattered were not retrieved.

## Key question 6: Feedback timing, hint granularity, and hint abuse

### Takeaway
Feedback improves learning by a medium effect (d = 0.48 across 435 studies) but its content matters more than its presence;
delayed feedback after a test beats immediate feedback on long-delay retention in several studies, with the literature split overall;
in tutoring systems, learners click through hint ladders to the final answer on a large share of actions, and that behavior predicts poor learning except when the learner treats the final hint as a worked example and studies it.

### Cited Findings
- [cross-domain] Wisniewski, Zierer, and Hattie (2020, Frontiers in Psychology, "The power of feedback revisited") meta-analyzed 435 studies (k = 994, N > 61,000) and found d = 0.48 for feedback on learning, with heterogeneity large enough that feedback cannot be treated as one treatment; the information content conveyed substantially moderated the effect. [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/pdf); [Augsburg repository](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/71009/fpsyg-10-03087.pdf)
- [verbal] Kulik and Kulik (1988) found an advantage for delayed over immediate feedback in studies using multiple-choice tests with 5-7 day retention intervals. A review of 39 feedback-timing studies on long-term retention found 16 favoring delayed, 12 favoring immediate, and 11 null. [Smith and Kimball 2010 PDF, which reports both](https://www.ou.edu/memorylab/pdfs/SmithKimball_2010_LearningFromFeedback_ms.pdf)
- [verbal] Butler, Karpicke, and Roediger (2007): delayed feedback after a multiple-choice test beat immediate feedback on delayed cued recall; answer-until-correct and plain correct-answer feedback were equivalent. [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-type-and-timing-of-feedback-on-learning-from-multip/)
- [verbal] Feedback increases retention of low-confidence correct responses, correcting a metacognitive error (Butler, Karpicke, Roediger). [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/correcting-a-metacognitive-error-feedback-increases-retention-of-/)
- [math/science, ITS] Cognitive Tutors give on-demand hints in levels, from a general principle to a final "bottom-out" hint that states the next action. Aleven et al. (2006) found students in a Geometry Cognitive Tutor unit abused hints (requesting them without need, or clicking rapidly through to the bottom-out hint) on 36% of their actions. [LearnLab wiki, help abuse (returned 404 on direct fetch; reported from search summary)](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Help_abuse); [Aleven et al. 2006 Help Tutor article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs11257-007-9045-6?LI=true)
- [math/science, ITS] Baker, Corbett, and Koedinger (2004) coined "gaming the system": succeeding in an educational environment by exploiting its properties rather than learning, operationalized as rapid repeated hint requests and systematic guess-and-check. Gaming correlates with reduced performance and poor learning. [CHI 2004 paper](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/materials/conferences/CHI2004/1p383.pdf); [Baker retrospective](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/PSCS-gaming-v6.pdf); [JEDM intervention study](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/768/197)
- [math/science, ITS] Shih, Koedinger, and Scheines (2008, EDM) argue that drilling to the bottom-out hint is not always abuse: some students are seeking a worked example. A response-time model (how long the student pauses on the final hint) separates productive from unproductive bottom-out use, predicts learning, and captures self-explanation-like behavior. [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf)
- [math/science, ITS review] Aleven, Roll, McLaren, and Koedinger (2016, International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education 26(1), "Help helps, but only so much") conclude: "Although we do not view on-demand, principle-based help during tutored problem solving as being as important as we once did, we still view it as helpful under certain circumstances, and recommend that it be included." [LearnTechLib record](https://learntechlib.org/p/176008/email); [Help Tutor project page](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/TheHelpTutor_Roll)
- [math/science, ITS] Interface changes that promote unsolicited hint use have been studied as a counter to help avoidance (the opposite failure: never asking). [arXiv, Avoiding help avoidance](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.13371)

### Inferences
- A separate solutions file is a bottom-out hint the reader can open at will, so the ITS findings apply. The 36% figure is the rate when the ladder costs one click per rung. The design lever a book has is the cost and the sequence: a hint rung before the solution rung, and a visible "attempt first" instruction, raise the price of clicking through.
- Shih's result is the reassuring one: a reader who opens the full solution and reads it slowly is studying a worked example, which for a novice is the right thing (question 1). The harmful pattern is reading the solution fast, feeling done, and moving on. The book cannot measure pause time, but it can structure the solution so that reading it fast is unsatisfying: subgoal labels with the reasoning under each, not a bare listing.
- The delayed-feedback findings are from verbal recall with a test in between. For code, the nearest analogue is: solve, then do the next exercise, then check both. A book can suggest that pacing in the exercise-set preamble, but cannot enforce it.
- Wisniewski's "content matters" result argues for solutions that explain the choice points (why this structure, what the alternative was) over solutions that are the finished code alone. That is also the "assembly" step from the productive-failure literature.
- What needs a live system and a book cannot do: measure help-seeking, delay a hint, or adapt rung depth to the reader. The step ladder with fixed rungs is the static approximation.

### Gaps
- No study of hint granularity (how many rungs, how specific each) with effect sizes was found in the sources retrieved; the Aleven 2016 review likely has it but was not fetched.
- The LearnLab help-abuse wiki page returned 404 on direct fetch; the 36% figure comes from the search engine's cached summary and from Aleven et al. 2006, whose Springer page was not fetched.

## Key question 7: Generation effect and preparation for future learning

### Takeaway
Generating an answer rather than reading it has a reliable effect of d = 0.40 across 86 studies in verbal memory,
and in problem-solving domains it is the low-element-interactivity end of the same continuum whose high end is the worked-example effect.
Preparation-for-future-learning reframes an exercise's purpose: an attempt that fails can still make the next chapter's explanation land, if the explanation follows.

### Cited Findings
- [verbal] Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott, and McDaniel (2007, Memory & Cognition 35(2), 201-210) meta-analyzed 445 effect sizes over 86 studies and found a generation effect of d = 0.40 for generated over read material. Moderators among 11 tested: larger in older than younger adults, larger within-subjects than between, largest for calculation-based generation rules, and larger under incidental than intentional learning. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/)
- [math/science] Chen, Kalyuga, and Sweller: generation effect at low element interactivity, worked-example effect at high. [Loughborough repository](https://repository.lboro.ac.uk/articles/The_worked_example_effect_the_generation_effect_and_element_interactivity/12053721)
- [cross-domain] Schwartz and Bransford (1998): telling works after relevant experience. [Wright State PDF](https://www.wright.edu/sites/www.wright.edu/files/uploads/2017/Feb/event/Schwartz_Bransford_1998_TimeForTelling.pdf)
- [cross-domain] Bransford and Schwartz (1999) PFL framework. [NSF PAR reference](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10214147)

### Inferences
- The "calculation" moderator in Bertsch is the closest the verbal literature gets to code: generating by computing a result beat generating by other rules. "Predict what this prints before running it" is a generation exercise of that kind and is cheap to include in a book.
- Generation is for small units. An exercise that asks the reader to generate one line, one expression, or one output is on the d = 0.40 side; asking them to generate a whole design from nothing is on the worked-example side, where showing beats generating for a novice.
- A PFL exercise says, in effect, "try this; the next chapter shows why it is hard." The book should then make good on it in the next chapter and name the exercise there, or the preparation is wasted.

### Gaps
- No generation-effect study on code was found. The link from "calculation generation" to output prediction is an inference.

## Key question 8: Metacognition, judgments of learning, and the illusion of competence from reading a solution

### Takeaway
Learners judge their learning while the answer is in front of them and fail to discount it, so reading a solution produces confidence the later test does not justify (foresight bias);
re-reading raises confidence while testing raises retention;
the debiasing that transfers to new material is teaching the learner the theory of why fluency misleads, not merely making them feel the difficulty once.

### Cited Findings
- [verbal, paired associates] Koriat and Bjork (2005, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, "Illusions of competence in monitoring one's knowledge during study") showed that judgments of learning are inflated whenever information present at study and absent-but-solicited at test highlights aspects of the cue that are less apparent when the cue is presented alone. They named it foresight bias. [PDF copy](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2005-koriat.pdf)
- [verbal] Koriat and Bjork (2006, JEP:LMC, "Illusions of competence during study can be remedied by manipulations that enhance learners' sensitivity to retrieval conditions at test") tested two remedies: enhancing sensitivity to ease-of-retrieval cues, and inducing theory-based judgments. Both fixed JOLs and study-time allocation, but only theory-based debiasing transferred to new items. [Bjork lab PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Koriat_Bjork_2006_JEP-LMC.pdf); [Haifa record](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/illusions-of-competence-during-study-can-be-remedied-by-manipulat/); [Koriat, Ma'ayan, Sheffer, Bjork 2006 PDF](https://newiipdm.haifa.ac.il/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/2006-Koriat-Maayan-Sheffer-Bjork.pdf)
- [verbal, prose] Roediger and Karpicke (2006): repeated study increased students' confidence in later recall while testing produced the better delayed recall. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)
- [cross-domain] Bjork and Bjork (2011) frame the same point from the learner's side: conditions that feel productive (fluent re-reading, massed practice) are often the ones that do not last, so optimizing learning often means acting against intuition. [Bjork lab PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- A reader who reads a solution and thinks "yes, that's what I would have done" is in the Koriat-Bjork condition: the answer is present, the judgment is inflated. The presentation that reduces it is one that withholds the answer while the judgment is made: a hint step first, a shape step (signatures with bodies removed) second, so the reader commits to an attempt before the full listing appears. The book's existing ladder is this design.
- The transferable remedy is theory-based: one short passage, early in the book, explaining that recognizing a solution is not the same as producing one, and that the feeling of knowing after reading is unreliable. A single such passage does more, per the 2006 result, than repeated "cover the solution" instructions.
- A solution written as a bare listing invites recognition. A solution written with the decision points named invites the reader to compare each decision with their own, which is a judgment they can make from their attempt rather than from the presence of the answer.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measured judgments of learning after reading a code solution specifically. The paired-associate and prose findings are the basis.

## Key question 9: Meta-analyses and reviews, with their numbers

### Takeaway
The large syntheses agree on a ranking: practice testing and spacing are the best-supported techniques (Dunlosky high utility);
worked examples, self-explanation, interleaving, generation, and feedback each carry a medium effect in the g = 0.40 to 0.61 range with wide heterogeneity;
instructional explanations added to examples and erroneous examples carry small effects.

### Inferences
Design recommendations for a book with end-of-chapter exercises and a separate solutions file, each tied to the finding above that supports it:

1. Keep the solutions separate and say "attempt first" once per exercise set. (Testing effect; foresight bias.)
2. Give every exercise a solution. Retrieval without feedback below 50% success shows no effect, and the solution is the only feedback a solo reader gets. (Rowland.)
3. Order each chapter's exercises from completion problems (a listing with a step removed) toward full problems, and shift the mix toward full problems as the book progresses. (Worked-example effect; expertise reversal; fading beats pairs.)
4. Where an exercise alternates an example with a problem, put the example first. (Van Gog, Kester, Paas 2011.)
5. Reveal solutions in steps: hint, then shape (signatures, bodies removed), then full solution. The ladder serves the novice (worked example) and the experienced reader (problem solving) from one file and raises the cost of clicking through. (Expertise reversal; hint abuse; foresight bias.)
6. Label the subgoals inside each solution, in prose or a one-line comment per block, and give the reasoning under each label. (Margulieux and Catrambone; Decker et al.; Wisniewski's content moderator.)
7. Do not append "explain why" prompts after a full solution. If explanation is wanted, make it the missing step the reader fills in. (Barbieri's negative moderator vs Bisra's positive effect.)
8. Prefer "produce the code" and "predict the output" over multiple choice, matching the exercise format to the real task. (Rowland recall > recognition; Adesope format match; Bertsch calculation generation.)
9. Scope retrieval exercises to one element when the material is complex; save whole-program recall for later exercises or later chapters. (Van Gog and Sweller's contested boundary; Chen, Kalyuga, Sweller's continuum.)
10. Include one or two exercises per chapter that do not announce their technique and that need a tool from an earlier chapter, placed after the chapter's own blocked exercises. Interleave the confusable things (the structurally similar patterns), not arbitrary pairs. (Brunmair and Richter; Rohrer et al.)
11. Use productive failure inside an exercise set, not instead of the chapter: pose a problem the chapter's technique handles badly, then show in the solution why and what the next chapter does about it. Name the exercise again in that next chapter. (Sinha and Kapur's mechanisms; Ashman et al.'s explicit-first result; Schwartz and Bransford.)
12. Write solutions that explain decisions, not just finished code; a solution read fast should feel incomplete. (Shih et al.'s productive bottom-out use; Wisniewski.)
13. Put one short passage early in the book on why recognizing a solution is not knowing it. The theory-based remedy is the one that transfers. (Koriat and Bjork 2006.)
14. Suggest, in the exercise preamble, doing two exercises before checking either. The book cannot delay feedback but can describe the schedule. (Butler et al. 2007.)
15. Show erroneous code beside its correction, not alone. (Erroneous-examples g = 0.136; Barbieri correct > incorrect.)

What needs a live instructor or adaptive system, and a book cannot do: measure help-seeking and pause times, adapt hint depth to the reader, schedule spaced review sessions, or enforce feedback delay. The step ladder, cross-chapter exercises, and preamble instructions are the static approximations.

### Gaps
- Several primary pages were not fetched (SAGE 403, PDFs that did not parse, one 404), so numbers come from abstracts, repository records, and secondary summaries; the table flags where a number was not retrieved.
- Programming-domain evidence exists for worked examples and Parsons problems, subgoal labels, and course-level retrieval practice; it does not exist, in the sources found, for interleaving, spacing, productive failure, generation, feedback timing, or judgments of learning. Every recommendation above that rests on [verbal] evidence is a transfer.
- Wittwer and Renkl 2010 and Aleven et al. 2003 (Review of Educational Research, help seeking and help design) were not fetched; both likely hold the hint-granularity and instructional-explanation detail this brief asked for.
