## 1. Programmed instruction and its descendants: what the evaluations showed, how it failed, what survived

### Takeaway
Programmed instruction (PI) was never shown to be worse than conventional teaching and was often modestly better, but its measured advantage was small, shrank over time, and did not depend on its signature features (tiny frames, overt responses, linear vs branching).
What survived into modern practice is the feedback-corrective loop (attempt, check, correct, re-test), mastery criteria, and self-pacing, not the frame format;
the frame format's failure modes were triviality, forced linearity, and shallow responding.

### Cited Findings
- Schramm's 1964 annotated bibliography for the U.S. Office of Education reviewed 165 studies of programmed learning; of 36 that compared it with conventional instruction, 17 found PI more effective, 18 found no difference, and one found conventional instruction better. — [KnowledgeJump, "Instructional Design: Programmed Learning"](https://www.knowledgejump.com/hrd/media/programmed_learning.html)
- Studies that added branching ("multitrack") paths to a linear program found no significant difference in efficiency between the linear program and the multitrack version; branching looked reasonable for individual differences but the attempted methods showed no advantage. — [IIT, "An Evaluation of Multiple Tracks in a Linear Program"](https://contrails.library.iit.edu/item/160280)
- Crowder's intrinsic (branching) programming was "not founded on any particular theory of learning, but only on practicality": learners who answered a multiple-choice frame wrongly were routed to a remedial sequence, and those who answered correctly skipped ahead. — [EdTech Books, "Programmed Instruction"](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/14180)
- The PI movement "produced, paradoxically, both research in program design and many programs marketed to schools without adequate evaluation, a situation that contributed to the decline of programmed instruction in the next decade." — [LIDT Foundations, "Programmed Instruction"](https://pressbooks.pub/lidtfoundations/chapter/programmed-instruction)
- PI's popularity "waned due to concerns over student boredom and the lack of conclusive evidence supporting its superiority over traditional teaching methods." — [EBSCO Research Starters, "Programmed Instruction"](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/programmed-instruction)
- Pressey (1962) cited numerous studies showing PI produced no better results than simply reading the declarative answers; Pressey (1963) argued PI threw away the structure of prose (sections, indices, random-access review); Skinner (1984) blamed poor program writing. Failure modes catalogued: forced linearity, atomization of questions away from context, shallow responding, and "guess the teacher's password" frames. — [Andy Matuschak, "Programmed instruction" (working notes, citing Benjamin 1988; Rutherford 2003; Pressey 1962, 1963; Skinner 1984)](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zJnwHfze71uA7T6pUwfe36f)
- The "blackout ratio" (Kemp & Holland 1966, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, "Blackout ratio and overt responses in programmed instruction: resolution of disparate results") is Holland's measure of frame triviality; the fetched article cites it but does not reproduce the definition. In the same tradition, Tudor & Bostow (1991, *JABA*) found with a 315-frame program that students who covertly answered frame blanks or had to type the answers "performed significantly better" than passive readers. — [Tudor & Bostow 1991, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1279579)
- Keller Plan (PSI) is PI's most-evaluated descendant: Kulik, Kulik & Cohen (1979, *American Psychologist* 34, 307-318) found PSI sections superior on the final exam in 57 of 61 studies; mean PSI exam score 73.6% vs 65.9% conventional (7.7 points); a delayed test several months later showed about 14 percentage points' advantage; essay exams showed larger differences than objective tests; students rated PSI classes "more enjoyable, more demanding, and higher in overall quality". — [Athabasca University, "PSI: The Data"](https://psych.athabascau.ca/open/keller/data.php)
- A second synthesis of the same 61 PSI studies reports an effect size of 0.49 SD, a 19-percentile-point advantage (search summary; verify against the paper). — [Florida Journal of Educational Research, "Meta-Analysis and the Planning of Future Studies"](https://journals.flvc.org/fjer/article/view/133748)
- Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990) found completion rates lower in PSI than in conventional classes, but the achievement advantage "is not an illusion created by the withdrawal of the weaker students". Reboy & Semb (1991) documented PSI teaching "complex subject matter" with transfer. Taveggia (1976): PSI superior in all 28 comparisons examined. — [Athabasca University, "PSI: The Data"](https://psych.athabascau.ca/open/keller/data.php)
- Bangert, Kulik & Kulik's 1983 synthesis of 51 studies of individualized teaching systems in secondary schools found only a small effect on achievement (search summary; verify against the paper, *Review of Educational Research* 53(2)). — [Kulik, Kulik & Cohen 1980 (JSTOR copy, cited in the same search)](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Kulik_Kulik_Cohen_1980.pdf)
- For calibration of the same group's other syntheses: Kulik, Kulik & Cohen (1980, *RER* 50(4), 525-544) found computer-based college teaching raised exam scores by about one quarter of a standard deviation across 54 studies (mean d ≈ .25, a 3.0-percentage-point gain), with effects larger (d = .51) when different teachers taught the two sections and smaller (d = .13) when the same teacher taught both, and no reliable effect on withdrawal. — [Kulik, Kulik & Cohen 1980, PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/575/Kulik_Kulik_Cohen_1980.pdf)
- Modern revival with data: Quantum Country (Matuschak & Nielsen 2019) embeds retrieval prompts in an essay and schedules review; readers who recall an answer for 5+ days keep median accuracy at 95% afterward (89/95/98% at the 25th/50th/75th percentiles); across 53 readers who reviewed for a full year, median 463 reviews with 10 lapses. An informal 2019 experiment that withheld review from some users found a 4-point accuracy drop versus a 7-point rise for controls over two weeks. — [Matuschak, "Quantum Country users rarely forget after demonstrating five-day retention"](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z2Rv4RAjXUrwa3fMYn9Q1iR); [Matuschak, "2019 Quantum Country efficacy experiment"](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/z2cKiAiZ8tAWiz4ZUoTaaBv)

### Inferences
- The PI evidence supports the loop (respond, get confirmation, correct) and not the frame.
  A book whose exercises end in a visible answer already has PI's active ingredient;
  the part PI got wrong (frames too small to require thought) is the analogue of a hint that gives the answer away.
- Branching never beat linear in controlled studies, so a static site should not expect gains from elaborate remedial paths;
  a single well-chosen remedial pointer (the "where to look" hint) is as much branching as the evidence supports.
- Pressey's finding that PI often did no better than reading the answers is the warning for any staged reveal:
  a step that can be completed by reading is a frame with a high blackout ratio.
- The PSI results say the mastery requirement and the retest matter more than the medium, and that a demanding format loses some learners (lower completion), which a self-study book cannot see.

### Gaps
- The programmed-instruction effect sizes from Kulik, Cohen & Ebeling (1980, *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis* 2(6), 51-64) and Kulik, Schwalb & Kulik (1982, *Journal of Educational Research* 75(3), 133-138) could not be read: the PDF the search returned under that name was the computer-based-teaching meta-analysis, the ERIC summary (ED263880) is image-only, and Semantic Scholar rate-limited. Both papers exist; their mean effect sizes and the reported decline across study years are unverified here.
- Holland's blackout-ratio definition and his evidence that high-blackout programs teach less were not retrieved (Kemp & Holland 1966 is cited but not fetched).
- No evaluation of Crowder's TutorText books or of modern "A Programmed Introduction to..." textbooks was found.

## 2. Hint design in tutoring systems and programming autograders

### Takeaway
Hint type (abstract vs bottom-out) by itself does not change learning in controlled comparisons;
what matters is whether the learner processes the hint (reflection time, explanation) and whether the hint arrives on request rather than unasked.
Rapid click-through to the answer is common (a third of help requests in one Cognitive Tutor dataset) and predicts less learning, while a data-driven next-step hint with an explanation plus a self-explanation prompt is the only programming-hint configuration that has shown transfer.

### Cited Findings
- Cognitive Tutor hint progressions start with a high-level hint "to encourage students to be constructive" and end in a bottom-out hint that "essentially provides the answer"; the Cognitive Geometry tutor (Aleven, McLaren, Roll & Koedinger 2006) is the canonical example. **[adaptive]** — [Sale & Muldner, "Learning with an algebra computer tutor: What type of hint is best?" (Cognitive Science Society)](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/28865/galley/18736/download)
- Sale & Muldner, algebra ITS, N = 50, random assignment to high-level-only vs bottom-out-only hints: no effect of hint type on posttest (F(1,44) = .1, p = .75, very small η²); a modest but significant number-of-hints × condition interaction (overall model F(4,42) = 11.3, p < .001, R² = .52) such that more hints correlated with learning only in the high-level condition; after entering a step, students waited longer before asking for a hint in the high-level condition (M = 17.6 s) than in the bottom-out condition (M = 6.3 s), p = .029. **[adaptive, but the null is informative for any format]** — [Sale & Muldner](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/28865/galley/18736/download)
- Shih, Koedinger & Scheines (EDM 2008): bottom-out hints can act as worked examples; a response-time indicator of time spent thinking and reflecting around a bottom-out hint (about 6 seconds in practice) correlated r ≈ .53 with posttest and r ≈ .48 with adjusted learning gain in the control condition (n = 18) and r = .41 with gain in the experimental condition, while plain time-on-task did not (r = .23). **[adaptive measurement; static implication]** — [Shih, Koedinger & Scheines 2008, PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf)
- Aleven & Koedinger (2000, "Limitations of Student Control: Do Students Know When They Need Help?", ITS 2000): 72% of student actions were unproductive help-seeking, including hint abuse, and students often avoided help when it would have helped; 30% of actions were consecutive fast help requests ("clicking through hints") without time to read them (search summary of secondary citations; verify). — [Aleven et al., "Toward Tutoring Help Seeking" (KiltHub)](https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/journal_contribution/Toward_Tutoring_Help_Seeking_Applying_Cognitive_Modeling_to_Meta-Cognitive_Skills/6470579); [arXiv 2604.23486](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.23486)
- Aleven, Stahl, Schworm, Fischer & Wallace (2003, *RER* 73(3), 277-320) review: learners "are not using the help facilities of ILEs effectively"; effective help use depends on metacognitive skill, system design, and their interaction. — [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00346543073003277)
- Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Wagner (CHI 2004): "gaming the system" (systematic guessing and help abuse, "quickly and repeatedly asks for help until the tutor gives the correct answer") was more strongly associated with reduced learning than other off-task behavior; gaming frequency correlated with posttest as strongly as prior knowledge; frequent gamers learned 38% less than non-gamers controlling for pretest. **[adaptive]** — [Baker et al. 2004, CHI PDF](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/materials/conferences/CHI2004/1p383.pdf)
- Zhang, Borchers, Baker et al. (2026, think-aloud study in a chemistry ITS): gaming is "a different pattern of cognitive engagement" with less planning and reactive rather than proactive strategies, framed as "self-regulated non-learning". — [arXiv 2601.04487](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.04487)
- Roll, Aleven, McLaren & Koedinger (2011, *Learning and Instruction* 21(2), 267-280), the Help Tutor inside the Geometry Cognitive Tutor: metacognitive feedback on help-seeking made students use on-demand help more deliberately, and the change persisted after the feedback stopped, "but not to achieve better learning outcomes". **[adaptive]** — [LearnLab wiki, "The Help Tutor"](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/TheHelpTutor_Roll); [Education Week 2011](https://edweek.org/ew/articles/2011/04/20/28help-2.h30.html)
- Razzaq & Heffernan (2010, ITS, "Hints: Is It Better to Give or Wait to Be Asked?", ASSISTments): students learned reliably more with hints on demand than with proactive hints shown automatically after errors; the effect was concentrated in students who request many hints, with no significant difference for low requesters (search summary; verify). **[static-compatible: on-demand = collapsible]** — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-13388-6_39)
- Razzaq & Heffernan (2006, ITS): breaking a problem into scaffolding sub-questions beat hints-on-demand carrying the same information on a middle-school math posttest; differences were not always significant overall but scaffolding helped significantly (p < .01) on the item that was hardest at pretest. **[adaptive, but the scaffold is static content]** — [ITS 2006 chapter via Unpaywall](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2F11774303_63)
- Rivers & Koedinger's ITAP generates data-driven next-step hints for Python by canonicalizing student code, finding the nearest correct state, and reifying edits back into the student's code; it can hint 100% of submissions and chain hints into a worked example; in a randomized trial, having hints available cut practice time by 13.7% with the same learning as the control (reported in a hint-generation survey). **[adaptive]** — [arXiv 2404.04728, "Navigating the Landscape of Hint Generation Research"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.04728); [Rivers thesis, KiltHub](https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/thesis/Automated_Data-Driven_Hint_Generation_for_Learning_Programming/6714911)
- Price, Zhi, Dong, Lytle & Barnes (AIED 2018): data-driven hint quality stops improving after 15-20 student training solutions and can decline with more; student data beats a single expert solution, but "a comprehensive set of expert solutions generally performs best". **[adaptive]** — [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10065912)
- Marwan, Williams & Price (ICER 2019): next-step code hints with textual explanations significantly improved immediate programming performance; performance on a later post-test task without hints improved only when the hints were combined with self-explanation prompts. **[static-applicable: explanation + prompt are content]** — [iSnap publication page](https://isnap.csc.ncsu.edu/home/public/publication/marwan-2019-icer)
- Xiao, Hou & Stamper (CHI 2024 LBW, think-aloud with 12 novices, four hint levels from natural-language guidance to concrete code): "high-level natural language hints alone can be helpless or even misleading, especially when addressing next-step or syntax-related help requests"; "adding lower-level hints, like code examples with in-line comments, can better support students". — [arXiv 2404.02213](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02213)
- In a four-level "chain-of-hints" study (LLM-generated hints for science questions), 32.5% of questions were resolved with one hint, 21.5% needed two, 13.9% three, and 32.1% all four. — [arXiv 2510.21087](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21087)
- In conversational tutoring, "students need more than just a minimally informative hint to infer the next steps when solving challenging problems". — [University of Memphis, "A study on two hint-level policies in conversational ITS"](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/2424)
- Koedinger & Aleven (2007, *Educational Psychology Review* 19(3)) name the assistance dilemma: the balance between giving and withholding information "exerts a major influence on students' learning", with worked example vs problem as its central case. — [BibSonomy record](https://bibsonomy.org/bibtex/d25b6e2de0446afc0c5ded0916d752dd)

### Inferences
- Hint level does not buy learning by itself; the only levers with evidence are (a) the learner's processing of the hint and (b) on-demand delivery.
  A collapsible reveal is on-demand by construction, which matches the Razzaq 2010 direction.
- The "click-through" failure has no detector in a static site.
  The nearest static substitute is to make the terminal step require an act (a self-explanation prompt or a comparison task), as Marwan 2019 did, since that is the configuration that produced transfer.
- Xiao 2024 and the Memphis finding argue that a hint ladder whose first rung is only prose ("where to look") will be found insufficient by many learners for next-step and syntax trouble; the second rung should carry something concrete (code), which a skeleton with bodies elided does.
- The chain-of-hints distribution (about a third resolve at rung one, a third need every rung) suggests three to four rungs is the natural range; no study was found that varied rung count with learning as the outcome.
- Price 2018's "expert solutions generally perform best" supports a hand-written canonical solution over any crowd-derived one for a book.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was found that manipulated the number of hint levels (2 vs 3 vs 5) and measured learning; the step-count question is unanswered by the literature read here.
- "Revisiting the Hint Button: Consistent Negative Associations Between Unproductive Hint Use and Learning Outcomes in ITS" (ACM, 2025/2026) and Aleven, Roll, McLaren & Koedinger, "Help Helps, But Only So Much" (IJAIED 2016) were both blocked; their abstracts would sharpen the hint-abuse numbers.
- Price, Dong, Zhi, Paaßen et al., "A Comparison of the Quality of Data-Driven Programming Hint Generation Algorithms" (IJAIED 2019) and "Hint Generation Under Uncertainty: The Effect of Hint Quality on Help-Seeking Behavior" (AIED 2017) were blocked; the second is the one that would say whether poor hints reduce willingness to ask.
- Stamper, Eagle, Barnes & Croy (IJAIED 2013, Hint Factory in the Deep Thought logic tutor) was not read.

## 3. Solution presentation: annotated reasoning, multiple solutions, erroneous examples, attempt-first vs solution-first

### Takeaway
A worked solution helps novices (g ≈ 0.48 in mathematics), but adding instructional explanations to it has a small and unreliable effect and can hurt learners who would otherwise self-explain;
comparing two solutions side by side helps flexibility when the learner already knows one of them;
an erroneous example beside a correct one has a weak average effect (g = .14) that depends on prompting the learner to explain the error;
and attempting the problem before seeing the canonical solution (productive failure) has a large meta-analytic effect when the follow-up instruction contrasts the learner's attempt with the canonical solution.

### Cited Findings
- Wittwer & Renkl (2008, *Educational Psychologist* 43, 49-64): instructional explanations "often have no positive effects on learning outcomes"; two reasons: they are not tailored to a specific misunderstanding and they are not integrated into a learning activity; they work when "combined with indications of the limitations in learners' understanding that they are intended to revise". — [The eLearning Coach summary](https://theelearningcoach.com/elearning_design/instructional-explanations/); [Göttingen project page](https://www.uni-goettingen.de/en/research/116760.html)
- In example-based learning, "instructional explanations provided in addition to self-explanation prompts can impair learning results". — [Göttingen project page](https://www.uni-goettingen.de/en/research/116760.html)
- Wittwer & Renkl (2010, *Educational Psychology Review* 22(4), 393-409), meta-analysis of 21 studies / 28 comparisons: minimal overall benefit of instructional explanations in example-based learning, with larger effects for conceptual than procedural knowledge; a secondary source reports the effects were not significantly different from zero on near or far transfer. — [arXiv 2405.16480 (secondary)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.16480)
- Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018, *Educational Psychology Review*), 69 effect sizes, 5,917 participants: self-explanation prompts g = .55 (95% CI .45-.65). When the control received no extra explanation, g = .67; when the control received a scripted instructional explanation matching the prompts, g = .35, significantly smaller (z = 2.70, p = .007). Prompts eliciting conceptual explanations beat metacognitive prompts (z = 3.28, p = .001); multiple-choice inducements and metacognitive self-explanations had no detectable effect; effects were similar across outcome types including transfer. **[static]** — [Bisra et al. 2018, PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
- Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023, *Educational Psychology Review*), 55 experiments: worked examples improve mathematics performance by g = 0.48 (about 18 percentile points), but pairing them with self-explanation prompts "significantly moderated the effect of examples yielding a negative effect" relative to examples without prompts; the authors suggest the students' self-explanations were poor or carried misconceptions. — [Kent State summary](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [Learning and the Brain summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=7218)
- Kalyuga's expertise reversal effect: examples beat problem solving for novices, the advantage disappears with expertise, and "when a learner is able to self-explain, instructional explanations as provided in worked-out examples impose extraneous load instead of germane load". — [Chartered College of Teaching](https://my.chartered.college/?p=5912); [CS Ed Substack summary](https://csed.substack.com/p/the-expertise-reversal-effect-2003)
- Rittle-Johnson & Star (2007), 70 seventh-graders solving equations: comparing two methods side by side (vs the same examples sequentially) produced greater procedural flexibility and procedural knowledge, no difference in conceptual knowledge; frequency of explicit comparisons predicted procedural gain. Star & Rittle-Johnson (2009, 157 fifth/sixth graders, estimation): comparison supported flexibility; conceptual gains held over a delay only for students above average at pretest. — [Rittle-Johnson, Star & Durkin 2017 chapter, PDF](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)
- Rittle-Johnson, Star & Durkin (2009, 236 seventh/eighth graders with little equation experience): students who did not attempt algebraic methods at pretest learned more from sequential study than from comparing methods on procedural, conceptual, and flexibility measures; they "seemed overwhelmed", completed less, and failed more often on non-standard methods. Students who did attempt algebra at pretest learned more from comparison. General math achievement did not moderate; prior knowledge of one method did. — [same chapter](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)
- Rittle-Johnson, Star & Durkin (2012, 198 eighth graders): with fewer problem types, fewer examples, and 30 more minutes, comparison supported flexibility for all students including on a one-month retention test, with no interaction with prior knowledge. In the same study a **delayed-comparison** condition (study one method on day one, compare on day two) "learned the least", below both always-compare and always-sequential. — [same chapter](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)
- Durkin & Rittle-Johnson (2012): comparing a correct with an incorrect method "supports gains in procedural knowledge, retention of conceptual knowledge, and a reduction in misconceptions". Design used in the classroom materials: two worked examples side by side, thought bubbles with a student's description of each method in common language, and three prompt types (understand each example, compare them, make connections) culminating in the learning goal. Across five experiments with college students, comparing two algebra word problems and their solutions did not support learning or transfer. **[static]** — [same chapter](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)
- Rittle-Johnson & Star's three summary findings: "two examples are better than one", "two examples presented together are better than two examples presented separately", "instructional support augments the benefits of comparison". — [Harvard DASH record](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-9609-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)
- Gick & Holyoak: students who compared two analogous stories transferred the solution principle to a new scenario more often than those who studied one; prompting the comparison increased transfer beyond reading the same analogs unprompted. — [Harvard DASH bitstream](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-9058-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)
- Große & Renkl (2007, *Learning and Instruction* 17, 612-634), finding and fixing errors in worked examples: a learning benefit of erroneous examples "but only for learners with strong prior knowledge and for far transfer". — [arXiv 2307.06315 (secondary)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.06315)
- Adams, McLaren, Durkin, Mayer, Rittle-Johnson & Isotani (2014) and McLaren et al. (2012, "To err is human, to explain and correct is divine"): interactive erroneous examples in web-based middle-school math produced benefits on delayed tests, characterized as a desirable difficulty, with more confusion and frustration during learning. — [Leuphana record](https://fis.leuphana.de/de/publications/erroneous-examples-as-desirable-difficulty/); [EdITLib, "More confusion and frustration, better learning"](https://mail.editlib.org/p//209945)
- Alemdag, Eichelmann & Narciss (2025, *Review of Educational Research*), 42 papers / 177 effect sizes comparing erroneous examples with correct examples or problem solving: "a statistically significant but weak effect of erroneous examples on learning (g = .136)"; the design of error-explanation activities significantly moderated it, with self-explanation prompts or instructional explanations about the error beating no error explanation. — [Leibniz-IPN record](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/a-framework-for-learning-from-erroneous-examples-and-meta-analysis-of-empirical-research)
- Sinha & Kapur (2021, *Review of Educational Research*), 53 studies / 166 comparisons, secondary and undergraduate STEM and medicine: problem solving before instruction beats instruction before problem solving; the effect "is about two times that of what you would gain with an outstanding teacher over the course of a year", and about three times with high fidelity to productive-failure design principles. — [World Economic Forum summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/students-who-productively-fail-learn-more/); [Times Higher Education Campus](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/campus/using-productive-failure-activate-deeper-learning)
- Loibl, Roll & Rummel's framework names four mechanisms of problem-solving-first: activation of prior knowledge, awareness of knowledge gaps, affect, and assembly. — [UNH teaching hub summary](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-productive-failure-kapor.pdf)
- Loibl & Rummel (2014, *Learning and Instruction* 34, 74-85, "Knowing what you don't know makes failure productive"), 240 tenth graders learning standard deviation: among productive-failure students, those whose follow-up instruction compared and contrasted typical student solutions with the canonical solution scored higher on conceptual knowledge immediately and after one week than those whose instruction did not; gap awareness is "one of the main active ingredients". **[the contrast is static content]** — [Psychology in Action summary](https://www.psychologyinaction.org/?p=1233); [Instructional Science 2020 record](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11251-020-09525-2)

### Inferences
- "Why this step" annotations are not free: Wittwer & Renkl and Bisra together say a provided explanation is worth roughly half of what a prompted self-explanation is worth (g .35 vs .67 against the same baseline), and Barbieri's negative moderator says prompts attached to examples can backfire when learners explain badly.
  The configuration with the best record is a short explanation targeted at a specific misconception, plus a prompt whose answer the solution then lets the reader check.
- A solution that shows two methods side by side helps readers who already own one method and overwhelms readers who own neither; a book that cannot know which reader it has should put the second method after the first, labelled, and keep the comparison prompt specific (efficiency, when each applies).
- "Which is correct?" pairs (a wrong solution next to the right one) are the comparison form with the best record on misconceptions, but the meta-analytic average is small and the benefit needs a prompt to explain the error.
- Loibl & Rummel's result is the best evidence for the answer side specifically: the gain from attempting first appears when the solution text contrasts typical wrong attempts with the canonical one.
  A solution that opens "If you tried X, it fails because..." is that instruction in static form.
- The delayed-comparison null (2012) cautions against assuming that staging always helps; a ladder whose rungs separate related material in time can lose to either extreme.

### Gaps
- Sinha & Kapur's actual Hedges g values (overall and by fidelity) were not read; the SAGE page returned 403. Only the "two to three times a good teacher" paraphrase is available.
- Wittwer & Renkl 2010's overall d was not read from the paper.
- Große & Renkl 2007 and Durkin & Rittle-Johnson 2012 effect sizes were not retrieved.
- No study was found on annotated vs bare *code* solutions specifically; the explanation evidence is from mathematics and science worked examples.

## 4. When the learner sees the solution: delayed vs immediate access, hidden answers, copying

### Takeaway
An attempt before the answer improves learning even when the attempt fails (the pretesting effect), and for applied settings immediate feedback after the attempt beats delayed feedback;
unrestricted answer access shifts behavior toward copying, which in two large field datasets predicts lower exam scores and roughly triple the failure rate.
Hiding the answer behind a fold has no direct study found; the evidence is for attempt-first ordering and for formats that make copying harder.

### Cited Findings
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009, *JEP: LMC*): an unsuccessful retrieval attempt before seeing the answer improved later memory relative to studying the answer, with materials that guaranteed the attempt would fail (fictional questions, weak associates). **[static]** — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-pluses-of-getting-it-wrong/); [Richland, Kornell & Kao, PDF](https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf)
- Kulik & Kulik (1988) meta-analysis of 53 feedback-timing studies: the delay-retention advantage appeared mainly in laboratory list-learning and was usually absent in applied classroom conditions, where immediate feedback was more effective; a later tally of 39 timing studies found 16 favoring delayed, 12 immediate, 11 null. — [Smith & Kimball 2010, PDF](https://www.ou.edu/memorylab/pdfs/SmithKimball_2010_LearningFromFeedback_ms.pdf); [Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1026174/full)
- Palazzo, Lee, Warnakulasooriya & Pritchard (2010, *Physical Review ST: Physics Education Research*), MIT introductory physics with an online homework system: copying increased toward deadlines and on later problems; copying of analytic problems correlated with a 2σ decline over the semester in relative exam score on similar problems but not on conceptual questions; repetitive copiers (>30% of problems) had about three times the chance of failing; an anonymous survey underreported copying by about a third; students copied nearly twice as much written homework as online homework; format changes cut copying by more than a factor of four (from about 11% to under 3% of electronic problems). — [Publication page](https://youngjin-lee.rbind.io/publication/per_2010/); [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2010/homework-copying-0318)
- Repetitive copiers "had enough knowledge, at least at the beginning of the semester... They didn't start their homework long enough ahead of time"; time pressure is the proximate cause. — [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2010/homework-copying-0318)
- Karimi (2011, ASEE Annual Conference, "Does Student Access to Solution Manual Pose a Challenge?"), thermodynamics at UT San Antonio: the share of D, F, and W grades rises when students have access to the solution manual, and students who pass "have a harder time in follow up courses"; the paper describes alternative homework assignment and grading policies. **[static, observational]** — [ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/17793)
- Razzaq & Heffernan (2010): hints on demand beat proactive hints (see §2). — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-13388-6_39)

### Inferences
- The attempt must come first; a fold that costs one click is a weak but real barrier, and the pretesting literature says even a failed attempt before the click is worth having.
- Copying rises with time pressure and with ease of copying; a self-study reader has no deadline, so the main lever a book has is the ease of the copy (how much of a solution one click reveals) and the usefulness of the intermediate rungs.
- Once the reader has attempted, immediate access is better than enforced delay for applied material, so a book should not add artificial delays past the first attempt.

### Gaps
- No study was found on collapsible or separately-filed solutions in textbooks or online books as an independent variable.
- The "Kim et al." solution-manual study named in the assignment was not located; the search found Karimi 2011 instead, which may be the intended reference or a different one.
- No Mazur-authored copying study was located; the MIT dataset is Pritchard's group.
- Feedback-timing evidence is from recall and homework, not from code solutions.

## 5. Metacognitive prompts attached to solutions

### Takeaway
Prompts that make the reader explain the solution's content (why a step holds, how two methods differ) carry a medium effect (g ≈ .55) and the effect holds on transfer measures;
prompts that ask the reader to reflect on their own process (metacognitive prompts) have no detectable average effect, and poorly scaffolded prompts attached to worked examples can reduce performance.

### Cited Findings
- Bisra et al. (2018): conceptual self-explanation prompts outperform metacognitive ones (z = 3.28, p = .001); metacognitive self-explanations and multiple-choice inducements had no significant effect; "justify", "conceptualize", and "explain" prompts all did; effects held across outcome types including transfer and problem solving. — [Bisra et al. 2018, PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
- A mathematics-specific meta-analysis of prompted self-explanation found small-to-moderate immediate gains in procedural knowledge, conceptual knowledge, and procedural transfer, but "evidence that self-explanation reliably promotes learning within a classroom context or retention of knowledge over a delay is much more limited". — [ZDM 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11858-017-0834-z)
- Barbieri et al. (2023): self-explanation prompts paired with worked examples were a negative moderator (see §3). — [Kent State summary](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance)
- Marwan, Williams & Price (2019): a self-explanation prompt attached to a code hint with explanation was the condition that transferred to a later task (see §2). — [iSnap publication page](https://isnap.csc.ncsu.edu/home/public/publication/marwan-2019-icer)
- Rittle-Johnson, Star & Durkin's classroom materials pair each side-by-side example with three prompt types: understand ("How did Morgan solve the equation?"), compare, and make connections, ending in the pair's learning goal; comparative explanations during the intervention predicted learning. — [2017 chapter, PDF](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)
- Alemdag et al. (2025): for erroneous examples, prompts to explain the error were the moderator that separated effective from ineffective designs. — [Leibniz-IPN record](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/a-framework-for-learning-from-erroneous-examples-and-meta-analysis-of-empirical-research)
- Roll et al. (2011): feedback on help-seeking changed behavior durably without changing learning (see §2). — [LearnLab wiki](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/TheHelpTutor_Roll)

### Inferences
- "Compare your solution to this one and list the differences" is a comparison prompt and sits on the strong side of the evidence; "reflect on how you approached this" is a metacognitive prompt and sits on the null side.
- "Explain why the alternative fails" is the prompt form the erroneous-example meta-analysis identifies as necessary for that format to work.
- A prompt should name its content ("why does the slot declaration matter here?") rather than ask for a general explanation; Bisra's coding found content-specific and general prompts both effective but conceptual content the differentiator.
- The Barbieri reversal warns that a prompt without a way to check the answer can entrench a misconception; in a static book the solution text that follows the prompt is the check, so the prompt should be answerable from the solution.

### Gaps
- No study was found that attached a "list the differences from your own solution" prompt to a programming solution and measured transfer.
- Which prompt format (question vs imperative vs fill-in) is best remains undecided; Bisra found an omnibus p = .048 with no significant pairwise differences.

## 6. Self-assessment and rubric-style solutions ("your answer should...")

### Takeaway
Rubrics and criteria-based self-assessment have a moderate effect on performance (g ≈ 0.45) and small effects on self-regulation;
no study was found that compared a criteria-style answer ("a correct answer must have A, B, C") with a single canonical listing, so the case for criteria rests on the rubric literature and on the comparison literature's finding that learners need instructional support to compare well.

### Cited Findings
- Panadero and colleagues' meta-analytic review of rubrics: after correcting for publication bias, g = 0.45 on academic performance, g = 0.23 on self-regulated learning, g = 0.18 on self-efficacy; most moderators (number of criteria, number of performance levels, use of self or peer assessment, educational level) were not significant. **[static]** — [Deusto repository record](https://deustoteka.deusto.es/items/59022074-df96-4c6a-8c52-3aa513c0ae3e)
- A later meta-analysis of self-assessment and peer assessment found positive effects on self-regulated learning strategies and affective outcomes, Hedges' g from 0.205 to 0.683. — [Educational Psychology Review 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09718-4)
- Rittle-Johnson & Star: "instructional support augments the benefits of comparison"; without prompts and side-by-side layout, students "often did not explicitly compare the methods" (Lynch & Star 2014b). — [Harvard DASH record](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-9609-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b); [2017 chapter](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)
- Across five college experiments, comparing two problems and their solutions did not support learning or transfer; the comparison that works is between methods for the same problem. — [2017 chapter](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)

### Inferences
- A criteria list next to the canonical listing gives the reader the comparison scaffold the Rittle-Johnson work says untutored learners lack, and the rubric literature says criteria improve performance at a moderate size.
- The null moderators in the rubric meta-analysis (criteria count, level count) suggest a short list of three or four must-haves does as well as an elaborate rubric.
- Criteria should describe the method's properties ("the base declares empty `__slots__`"), since comparing methods is the form with evidence and comparing problems is not.

### Gaps
- No experiment comparing a rubric-style answer against a single model solution was found.
- The rubric meta-analyses are mostly on writing and project tasks, not code.

## 7. Mastery learning, Bloom's two sigma, and the Keller Plan: what the exercise-solution loop contributes

### Takeaway
Mastery learning's feedback-corrective loop (formative test, corrective work, parallel re-test to an 80% criterion) produces about 0.5 to 0.6 SD on course-aligned tests in meta-analyses, about 1 SD in Bloom's dissertations, and about zero on standardized tests in Slavin's best-evidence synthesis;
the two-sigma tutoring result has not replicated at that size.
The part of the loop a book can supply is the parallel re-test: a second exercise on the same objective after the solution.

### Cited Findings
- Bloom (1984, *Educational Researcher*): in Anania's and Burke's dissertations, mastery learning (formative tests, feedback-corrective procedures, parallel formative tests) put the average student about 1σ above the conventional class and one-to-one tutoring about 2σ; "about 90% of the tutored students" reached the level of the top 20% of the control. Bloom's table of alterable variables (adapted from Walberg 1984): tutorial instruction 2.00, reinforcement 1.20, feedback-corrective 1.00, cues and explanations 1.00, student classroom participation 1.00, improved reading/study skills 1.00, cooperative learning 0.80, graded homework 0.80, classroom morale 0.60, assigned homework 0.30. — [Bloom 1984, PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1984-bloom.pdf)
- Bloom's enhanced-prerequisites study: a 3-4 hour corrective process in week one brought most students to the 80% mastery standard on a parallel test; those classes were about .7σ ahead on the first formative test of the advanced course; combining enhanced prerequisites with the feedback-corrective loop over 10-12 weeks gave about 1.6σ on the summative exam. — [Bloom 1984, PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1984-bloom.pdf)
- Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), 108 controlled evaluations: mastery learning raised exam scores by 0.52 SD on average; effects stronger for weaker students; positive attitudes; may increase time on task. — [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis)
- Guskey & Pigott (1988), 46 studies of group-based mastery learning: effect size 0.6, with large heterogeneity (studies "do not share a common effect size"). — [EdPsycInteractive](https://edpsycinteractive.org/files/mastlear.html)
- Slavin (1987, *Review of Educational Research* 57(2), 175-213, "Mastery Learning Reconsidered"): best-evidence synthesis of group-based mastery learning over at least 4 weeks in elementary and secondary schools found "essentially no evidence" on standardized measures (median ES = +.04 across seven equal-time studies), moderate positive effects on experimenter-made measures (mean +.64 on experimenter-made math tests), retention effects with a median "essentially zero", and "little evidence that effects maintained over time"; he frames a coverage-vs-mastery dilemma and rejects the "strong claim" of superiority at equal time. — [Slavin 1987, PDF](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf)
- The 2σ tutoring effect "has never been replicated at that scale"; a 2020 meta-analysis of 96 tutoring studies found none producing two sigma; later work reproduces a high mastery-learning effect but below Bloom's. — [Wikipedia, "Bloom's 2 sigma problem"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom%27s_2_sigma_problem); [Nintil, "Bloom's sigma"](https://nintil.com/bloom-sigma/)
- Keller Plan results (57 of 61 studies favor PSI; 7.7-point exam advantage; 14-point delayed advantage; lower completion rates): see §1. — [Athabasca University, "PSI: The Data"](https://psych.athabascau.ca/open/keller/data.php)

### Inferences
- The mastery effect lives in the re-test after correction, not in the first attempt; a solution that ends the episode gives the reader the feedback half of the loop and skips the corrective half.
  A second exercise on the same objective, placed after the solution, is the static form of the parallel formative test.
- Slavin's standardized-test null means the loop improves what it tests; for a book, that is acceptable if the exercises test what the chapter claims to teach, and it is the reason to write exercises against named chapter objectives.
- "Cues and explanations" at 1.00 in Bloom's table is a correlational estimate ("averaged or estimated from correlational data"), not an experiment; it should not be read as evidence that explanations in solutions are worth a full sigma.

### Gaps
- The Keller Plan studies did not isolate which component (mastery criterion, self-pacing, proctors) carries the effect; the Athabasca summary says so explicitly.
- The 2020 tutoring meta-analysis cited by Wikipedia was not read directly.

## 8. Reveal in steps: fading, completion problems, hint ladders, and Polya's structure

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced stepwise design is backward fading: a complete example, then an example with the last step blank, then more blanks, then the bare problem, which beat example-problem pairs on near transfer with fewer errors along the way.
A hint-then-skeleton-then-solution ladder maps onto that sequence in reverse order for a single problem (the skeleton is a completion problem), but no study was found that evaluates a ladder as such, and the one study of delayed staging in the comparison literature found it worst.
Polya's four phases have thin experimental support: one seven-student study, and field data showing "look back" is the phase learners skip.

### Cited Findings
- Renkl, Atkinson, Maier & Staley (2002, *Journal of Experimental Education*): a fading procedure (complete example, then one solution step omitted, then more blanks, until only the problem remains) "clearly fostered near transfer performance" in a field experiment against example-problem pairs; the effect was mediated by fewer errors under fading. **[static]** — [LearnLab wiki, "Fading"](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fading); [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2854h4v0/qt2854h4v0.pdf)
- Renkl & Atkinson's model: worked examples are most effective "during initial stages of cognitive skill acquisition, while in later stages, solving problems is superior"; fading ("the completion strategy") sits between the two and was developed by Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill and by van Merriënboer's completion-problem work. — [Cambridge, *Cognitive Load Theory*, "Learning from worked-out examples and problem solving"](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cognitive-load-theory/learning-from-workedout-examples-and-problem-solving/DBA85CE81E9A7B1B090547D12E2F2A04)
- Kalyuga's expertise reversal: the example advantage disappears with expertise and explanations become extraneous load for learners who can self-explain. — [Chartered College of Teaching](https://my.chartered.college/?p=5912)
- Rittle-Johnson, Star & Durkin (2012): the delayed-comparison condition learned the least (see §3). — [2017 chapter](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)
- Sale & Muldner: hint type did not change learning; students waited longer before requesting a hint under high-level hints (17.6 s vs 6.3 s) (see §2). — [Sale & Muldner](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/28865/galley/18736/download)
- Xiao, Hou & Stamper: high-level natural-language hints alone can mislead on next-step and syntax requests; concrete code with inline comments helps (see §2). — [arXiv 2404.02213](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02213)
- Shih et al.: a bottom-out hint functions as a worked example when the learner spends time thinking and reflecting on it (see §2). — [Shih, Koedinger & Scheines 2008](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf)
- Schoenfeld (1979, *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education* 10(3), "Explicit Heuristic Training as a Variable in Problem-Solving Performance"): seven upper-division Berkeley science and math majors, four randomly assigned to explicit instruction in five Polya-style heuristics; all four trained students outscored all three controls on completely solved posttest problems (p = 1/35 < .05); mean net gain more than 2 problems for the trained group vs 0 for controls. — [Schoenfeld 1979, PDF](https://www.ime.usp.br/~alpereir/cursosdosemestre/resprob/schoenfeld_heuristics_79.pdf)
- Schoenfeld's later conclusion: Polya's strategies "alone are weak and need to be strengthened by complementary domain-specific tactics"; students "tend to look at the problem then quickly try to solve it". — [University of Nottingham, "Problem solving: what have we learned since Polya's introspection"](https://nottingham.ac.uk/education/events/2019-20/problem-solving.aspx)
- In a field study of Polya's four steps, only 42.80% of students applied "look back" against over 93% for each of the first three steps; "look back" is performed "with less care than other stages". — [Journal of Advances in Humanities and Social Sciences 2016 (IDEAS record)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/apb/jahsss/2016p261-268.html)
- Roughly a third of learners resolve a question at the first of four hint levels and a third need all four (see §2). — [arXiv 2510.21087](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21087)

### Inferences
- The three-rung ladder is defensible as a completion problem (rung two) framed by a hint (rung one) and a worked example (rung three); the fading literature supports the skeleton rung in particular, since a partially worked example with blanks is the completion-problem format that reduced errors and improved near transfer.
- The ladder's weak rung is the first, if it is prose only: the hint-level studies say abstract prose hints are the ones learners find insufficient, and Sale & Muldner's null says a high-level hint neither helps nor hurts learning on its own.
  The first rung earns its place when it names a location in the chapter (which turns it into a retrieval cue) rather than restating the problem.
- Rittle-Johnson's delayed-comparison null is the one piece of evidence against staging per se; it is about separating two methods in time, not about hint-then-solution, so it argues for keeping all rungs of one exercise on one page rather than against the rungs.
- Polya's "look back" is the phase learners skip unprompted, which is the case for ending a solution with a look-back prompt (what else would this solve, what would break it) rather than with the listing.
- Expertise reversal implies the ladder should be flattenable: a reader who has mastered the chapter should be able to open the solution directly without passing through hints, which a collapsible design permits and a forced-sequence design would not.

### Gaps
- No study evaluates a hint / skeleton / full-solution ladder on a single exercise as a unit, in programming or elsewhere.
- No study varies the number of rungs with learning as the outcome (see §2).
- Van Merriënboer's completion-problem experiments in programming (1990s) were not retrieved; they are the closest precedent for a code skeleton with bodies elided and should be read.
- Research on Polya-structured written solutions (understand / plan / carry out / look back as the solution's format) was not found; Schoenfeld's study is about instruction in heuristics, not about solution presentation.
