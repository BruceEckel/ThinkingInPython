# Fade the Code, Explain the Choices

The research supports the three-step ladder, but unevenly: the middle rung (the code skeleton with bodies elided) rests on the best-replicated finding in the whole literature, the hint rung has a null result on its own and earns its place as a retrieval cue pointing into the chapter, and the full-solution rung is where the evidence says the largest unrealized gain sits.
Worked examples beat problem solving for novices by about **g = 0.48**, the advantage reverses once a learner has domain experience, and fading solution steps (which a skeleton does) is the strongest single design feature in the mathematics meta-analysis and the one programming study that compares completion against generation ([Kent State record](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [University of Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/strategies-for-programming-instruction-in-high-school-program-com/)).
What a solution says matters more than that it exists: a provided explanation is worth about half of a prompted self-explanation against the same baseline, prompts appended after a worked example can hurt, and the configurations with evidence are subgoal labels above step groups, a contrast between a typical wrong attempt and the canonical one, and two implementations set side by side with a trade-off prompt ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf); [Patitsas et al. 2013](https://cs.mcgill.ca/~patitsas/publications/compare_contrast.pdf)).
For exercises aimed at experienced programmers, six formats carry evidence: completion with the idea-bearing lines blanked, predicting output before the explanation, explaining a listing in plain English with a short trace of the one surprising step, comparing two implementations, critiquing a solution for structural (not local) faults, and cumulative exercises that interleave confusable patterns from earlier chapters.
Four devices have evidence against them for this audience: reorder-the-lines Parsons puzzles, distractor lines, "explain why" prompts appended after a full solution, and difficulty labels by Bloom level.
Most of what well-regarded books and platforms do (Knuth's ratings, Bentley's hints-then-outlines, Rustlings' idiomatic comparison, Advent of Code's part two, Exercism's approaches) is author practice with no controlled test; that makes it untested, not wrong, and several of these devices are the static form of a mechanism that does have evidence.
Two caveats bound every claim here: nearly all controlled programming evidence is on CS1 novices, and no study evaluates a hint-skeleton-solution ladder as a unit, so the ladder is a well-grounded hypothesis rather than a measured treatment.

## Expertise reversal makes the shape rung the ladder's strongest step

The cognitive-load literature gives one frame that organizes everything else.
A 2023 meta-analysis of 55 mathematics studies and 181 effect sizes found that studying worked examples beats solving practice problems by **g = 0.48**, about 18 percentile points, and that faded solution steps was the design feature with the strongest effect in favor of examples ([Kent State record](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [University of Delaware summary](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/in-the-news-christina-barbieri/)).
The same literature says the advantage is conditional on the learner.
Kalyuga, Chandler, Tuovinen, and Sweller gave trade apprentices either worked examples or problems; the inexperienced did best with examples and lower rated mental load, and with more experience "worked examples became redundant and problem solving proved superior" ([UOW repository](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/139/)).
Kalyuga's 2007 review names this the expertise reversal effect: techniques that help inexperienced learners lose their effect, and can turn negative, with experienced ones ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-007-9054-3)).
The order of example and problem reverses with it: low-prior-knowledge learners do best with example-then-problem pairs, high-prior-knowledge learners with problem-then-example pairs ([Expertise reversal effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)).

Your reader is on the experienced side of that line for Python and on the inexperienced side for each chapter's new idea.
A single file that opens with the full solution for every exercise is right for the second reader and wrong for the first.
A collapsible ladder lets each reader stop at the rung that matches their expertise, which is the static approximation of the adaptive instruction the reversal literature recommends.
The ordering is also right: the reader attempts first and sees the example second, which is the problem-example pairing that favors high prior knowledge.
One consequence follows that the ladder honors but is worth stating: the rungs must stay independently openable.
A design that forces a reader through the hint and the shape before the solution would impose the novice sequence on the expert, which is the harmful case.

The middle rung is a completion problem, and completion problems have the best programming-specific evidence in this set.
Renkl, Atkinson, Maier, and Staley's field experiment faded steps out of worked examples (a complete example, then one step blank, then more, then the bare problem) and found fading "clearly fostered near transfer performance" against example-problem pairs, with the effect mediated by fewer errors along the way ([eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt2854h4v0/qt2854h4v0.pdf)).
Van Merriënboer's 1990 study ran the same idea in a programming course: the group taught by completing partial programs was "superior to the generation group in measures concerning the construction of programs," dropped out less, and used language templates better, with no difference on interpreting programs ([University of Twente record](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/strategies-for-programming-instruction-in-high-school-program-com/)).
His stated mechanism is the one a skeleton exploits: "students cannot complete a program without carefully studying it so that 'mindful abstraction' is explicitly provoked" ([Twente record, computer-based study](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/strategies-for-computer-based-programming-instruction-program-com/)).

Weinman's faded-Parsons work adds the finding that bears most directly on a book about patterns.
Across two CS1 studies with 237 consenting students, open-ended code-writing exercises exposed students to the intended pattern **39.2%** of the time against **97.2%** for the faded version, and pattern acquisition ran 55.3% against 20.7% in the first study; the author's conclusion is that "open-ended Code Writing exercises often do not expose students to the intended patterns even when a well-documented instructor solution is provided after the fact" ([Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)).
An exercise meant to teach *Observer* can be solved without *Observer*, and a reader who does that learns nothing about the pattern from the attempt.
The shape rung constrains the solution's structure to the chapter's pattern before the full code appears, which is how a static format gets the exposure Weinman measured.
It also avoids a cost Haynes-Magyar found with experienced programmers new to Python: when a scaffold's solution "diverges from a learner's mental model, the scaffolding may increase rather than decrease cognitive load," to the point that a 13-year veteran removed a correct block because it did not match his strategy ([Haynes-Magyar 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22407)).
A reader who sees the intended structure at rung two is less likely to meet a full solution at rung three that contradicts the approach to which they committed.

Weinman's blanking rules say what to elide.
In the faded exercises, 0% to 75% of the code was blanked (32% on average), required function arguments and top-level control-flow tokens stayed intact in every exercise, and blanking every variable name turned the scaffold into something harder than writing from scratch (completion fell to zero in a small study), so the rule is to blank the lines that carry the idea and leave the identifiers ([Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)).
The book's generated shape (signatures and class structure kept, bodies elided) matches that rule in the common case, where the idea lives in the bodies.
It under-serves two cases.
A one-function solution reduces to a bare signature, which restates the exercise and adds nothing the hint did not.
A solution whose idea is the class structure (a base with empty `__slots__`, a registry decorator stacked under `@record`, a *Composite* whose shape is the point) gives that idea away at rung two, which is Pressey's old complaint about programmed instruction: a frame the learner can complete by reading teaches no more than reading the answer ([Matuschak, "Programmed instruction"](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zJnwHfze71uA7T6pUwfe36f)).
Those exercises would gain from a hand-chosen elision (blank the structural lines, keep a body) rather than the mechanical one.

The evidence also says the fade should be fast for this audience.
The one direct upper-division test of the novice-proven format found nothing: 84 Berkeley students past CS2 solved 59-74% more reorder puzzles per session than code-writing peers, with no change in learning gains over four weeks, and "could deduce which statements belonged to which functions" from variable names, like "edge pieces in a jigsaw puzzle" ([Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)).
The ITiCSE 2022 working group report on Parsons problems names expertise reversal as the open risk and quotes Fabic et al.: Parsons problems "were beneficial for novice students, but fix and write code activities were more beneficial for more advanced students" ([Parsons Problems and Beyond](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)).
One completion exercise per new idiom, then write-from-spec, is the fade this implies; a chapter-long ladder of scaffolded exercises would be the novice design.

## The hint rung works as a retrieval cue, not as a restatement

Hint level, on its own, does not change learning.
Sale and Muldner randomized 50 algebra-tutor students to high-level hints alone or bottom-out hints alone and found no effect of hint type on the posttest (F(1,44) = 0.1, p = .75); the one difference was behavioral, with students waiting longer before asking under high-level hints (17.6 s against 6.3 s) ([Sale & Muldner](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/cognitivesciencesociety/article/28865/galley/18736/download)).
Aleven, Roll, McLaren, and Koedinger's 2016 review of on-demand help reaches a tempered verdict: "we do not view on-demand, principle-based help during tutored problem solving as being as important as we once did," though they still recommend it ([LearnTechLib record](https://learntechlib.org/p/176008/email)).
What does predict learning is processing.
Shih, Koedinger, and Scheines found that time spent thinking around a bottom-out hint correlated r ≈ .53 with the posttest while raw time on task did not (r = .23), and framed the bottom-out hint as a worked example when the learner reads it slowly ([Shih, Koedinger & Scheines 2008](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/scheines/Shih_Koedinger_Scheines_2008_EDM.pdf)).
The failure mode is the fast click-through.
Baker, Corbett, Koedinger, and Wagner's "gaming the system" (rapid repeated help requests, systematic guessing) correlated with posttest scores as strongly as prior knowledge did, and frequent gamers learned **38% less** than non-gamers controlling for pretest ([Baker et al. 2004](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/materials/conferences/CHI2004/1p383.pdf)).

A static site cannot detect a click-through, so its levers are the content of each rung and the cost of reaching the next.
Two findings say what the first rung should and should not be.
Xiao, Hou, and Stamper's think-aloud with four hint levels found that "high-level natural language hints alone can be helpless or even misleading, especially when addressing next-step or syntax-related help requests," and that "code examples with in-line comments can better support students" ([arXiv 2404.02213](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02213)).
A prose hint that paraphrases the problem is the kind that misleads; the concrete code the study asked for comes from rung two, so rung one need not supply it.
Rung one, and no other rung, can point back into the chapter.
Retrieval practice beats restudy by a medium effect across three meta-analyses (g = 0.50 in Rowland's 61 lab studies, g = 0.61 in Adesope's 118, g = 0.499 across Yang's 222 classroom studies), and Rowland found no testing effect without corrective feedback when initial retrievability was at or below 50% ([Frontiers summary](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full); [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)).
A "where to look" that names the section and the listing where the mechanism appears turns a stalled attempt into a retrieval with a cue, which is the condition under which the effect appears; a hint that gives the first step of the answer turns it into a worked example early.
Bentley's print precedent uses the same form: his hints are "further questions providing insightful ways of approaching a solution," followed in a separate section by outlines of solutions ([Linux Journal review](https://www.linuxjournal.com/node/3846)).

How many rungs is unanswered by any controlled study, but one distribution suggests the range.
In a four-level chain-of-hints study, 32.5% of questions resolved at the first hint, 21.5% at the second, 13.9% at the third, and 32.1% needed all four ([arXiv 2510.21087](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21087)).
About a third of learners need the bottom rung whatever the ladder, and about a third stop at the first, which argues for three or four rungs and against five; no study varies rung count with learning as the outcome.
One result cautions against staging that separates related material: Rittle-Johnson, Star, and Durkin's delayed-comparison condition (one method on day one, the comparison on day two) "learned the least," below both always-compare and always-sequential ([Rittle-Johnson, Star & Durkin 2017](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)).
That is about splitting two methods across days, not about hint-then-solution, and it argues for keeping every rung of one exercise on one page, which the `<details>` ladder does.
Programmed instruction's history adds that elaborate branching is not worth building: studies that added remedial "multitrack" paths to linear programs found no difference in efficiency ([IIT record](https://contrails.library.iit.edu/item/160280)), and Schramm's 1964 review of 36 comparisons found 17 favoring programmed instruction, 18 no difference, and one favoring conventional teaching ([KnowledgeJump](https://www.knowledgejump.com/hrd/media/programmed_learning.html)).
What survived from that movement is the loop (respond, check, correct), not the frame; Tudor and Bostow found students who answered frame blanks, covertly or by typing, "performed significantly better" than passive readers ([Tudor & Bostow 1991](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1279579)).
One remedial pointer per exercise is as much branching as the evidence supports.

The attempt before the first click is the part the ladder cannot enforce and the part with the most evidence.
Roediger and Karpicke's prose-passage study shows why a reader who checks a solution at once misjudges the result: after five minutes restudy won, after two days and a week prior testing produced substantially greater retention, and repeated study had raised students' confidence that they would remember ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)).
Kornell, Hays, and Bjork found that an unsuccessful retrieval attempt before seeing the answer improved later memory relative to studying the answer, with materials built so the attempt would fail ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-pluses-of-getting-it-wrong/)).
Field data say what unrestricted answer access does: in MIT's online physics homework, copying rose toward deadlines, repetitive copiers (over 30% of problems) had about three times the chance of failing, and format changes cut copying by more than a factor of four ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2010/homework-copying-0318)); in a thermodynamics course the share of D, F, and W grades rose when students had the solution manual ([ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/17793)).
A self-study reader has no deadline, so the main lever is how much one click reveals, which the ladder limits.
Once the attempt is made, do not add delay: Kulik and Kulik's review found the delayed-feedback advantage mainly in laboratory list learning and usually absent in applied settings, where immediate feedback did better ([Smith & Kimball 2010](https://www.ou.edu/memorylab/pdfs/SmithKimball_2010_LearningFromFeedback_ms.pdf)).

The remedy for the illusion of competence is a passage, not a repeated instruction.
Koriat and Bjork showed that judgments of learning inflate whenever the answer is present at study and absent at test ([Koriat & Bjork 2005](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2005-koriat.pdf)), and their follow-up tested two fixes: making the learner feel the difficulty, and teaching the learner the theory of why fluency misleads; both corrected judgments, but "only theory-based debiasing transferred to new items" ([Koriat & Bjork 2006](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Koriat_Bjork_2006_JEP-LMC.pdf)).
One short passage early in the book, saying that recognizing a solution is not the same as producing one and that the feeling of knowing after reading is unreliable, does more than a "cover the solution" line under every exercise set.
A single "attempt first" sentence at the top of each set still belongs there; Codewars names the cost of early unlocking ("forfeit any honor and rank progression") and the prose can do the same ([Codewars docs](https://docs.codewars.com/concepts/kata/solutions/)).

## The full solution is where the evidence says to invest

Reviewers bracket the design space from both ends: Learn You a Haskell is faulted for having no exercises ([rmathew review](https://rmathew.com/2015/lyah.html)), and The Little Schemer for solutions with no explanation, a book that "literally never explains anything directly" ([Invent with Python review](https://inventwithpython.com/blog/book-review-the-little-schemer.html)).
Between those ends, the question is what a solution should contain, and the evidence on explanations is more specific than "add some."
Wittwer and Renkl's review found that instructional explanations "often have no positive effects on learning outcomes," for two reasons: they are not tailored to a specific misunderstanding, and they are not integrated into an activity; they work when "combined with indications of the limitations in learners' understanding that they are intended to revise" ([eLearning Coach summary](https://theelearningcoach.com/elearning_design/instructional-explanations/)).
Bisra and colleagues' meta-analysis of 69 effect sizes puts a number on the gap: self-explanation prompts carry **g = 0.55** overall, g = 0.67 against a control with no extra explanation but g = 0.35 against a control given a scripted explanation matching the prompts, and conceptual prompts beat metacognitive prompts (z = 3.28), which had no detectable effect ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)).
Barbieri's meta-analysis supplies the warning on the other side: self-explanation prompts paired with worked examples were a negative moderator relative to examples without prompts, which the authors attribute to poor or misconception-laden self-explanations ([University of Delaware summary](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/in-the-news-christina-barbieri/)).
Together these rule out the easy device of a "why does this step work?" question appended after the full listing: it is the condition that hurt.
The programming study that found transfer used a different configuration.
Marwan, Williams, and Price found that next-step code hints with textual explanations improved immediate performance, and that a later task without hints improved "only when the hints were combined with self-explanation prompts" ([iSnap publication page](https://isnap.csc.ncsu.edu/home/public/publication/marwan-2019-icer)).
A prompt belongs before the text that answers it, where the reader commits and then checks; in a ladder, the shape rung is that prompt in code form, and a conceptual question in the hint ("which object owns the registration?") is the prose form.

Subgoal labels are the form of explanation with the best fit to a book of listings.
Morrison, Margulieux, and Decker's aggregated loop studies (220 CS1 students at three universities) found problem solving depended on the interaction of label format and transfer distance (ω² = .08, a medium effect), with given labels beating no labels by **d = .59** on far-transfer practice and learner-generated labels beating given ones by d = .44 on near-transfer practice; labels are "action-based phrases" that state a step's function, are context independent, and "are NOT comments," since the control group saw identical comments ([Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)).
The semester-long CS1 study (N = 307) found no difference in the exam mean but lower variance, and students in the subgoal group were "half as likely to withdraw and half as likely to fail" ([Morrison seminar slides](https://www.raspberrypi.org/app/uploads/2020/07/Using-subgoal-labels-to-reduce-cognitive-load-in-introductory-programming.pdf)).
Two details matter for this book.
The third experiment's students had learned loops before the study (94% had a prior course) and still benefited, with generated labels and near-transfer practice doing best there, so the device survives some prior knowledge ([Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)).
And learners who wrote specific labels "performed as poorly as the worst performing group" on far transfer while general-label writers "performed better than any other group," so the labels a solution models should name the function of a step ("register the responder," "dispatch on the current state"), not its content ("append to the list") ([Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483)).
Three to six such labels above the step groups of a solution, each with its reasoning under it, is cheap to add; and because the shape rung is generated from the solution, labels in the solution can carry into the shape, where a reader fills in what each label names.

The other device with direct evidence on the answer side is contrast with the reader's own attempt.
Loibl and Rummel had 240 tenth graders attempt a problem before instruction; those whose follow-up compared and contrasted typical student solutions with the canonical one scored higher on conceptual knowledge immediately and a week later, and gap awareness is "one of the main active ingredients" ([Psychology in Action summary](https://www.psychologyinaction.org/?p=1233)).
This is the productive-failure mechanism in static form: Sinha and Kapur's 53-study meta-analysis supports problem solving before instruction, but Ashman, Kalyuga, and Sweller found the reverse when element interactivity is high, with the lecture-first group scoring almost 50% higher on application and transfer ([WEF summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/students-who-productively-fail-learn-more/); [NYU abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2019/journal-article-abstracts/s10648-019-09500-5/)).
Code is high-interactivity material, and a textbook puts the chapter before the exercises in any case, so the order is settled; what remains usable is the assembly step.
A solution paragraph that opens "If you kept the state in the subject, the second observer sees the first one's update because..." delivers the contrast without reordering anything.
The erroneous-example literature says the same from another direction: across 42 papers the average effect of erroneous examples is weak (**g = .136**), and prompting the learner to explain the error is the moderator that separates effective designs from ineffective ones ([Leibniz-IPN record](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/a-framework-for-learning-from-erroneous-examples-and-meta-analysis-of-empirical-research)); Große and Renkl found the benefit "only for learners with strong prior knowledge and for far transfer" ([arXiv 2307.06315](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.06315)), which describes your reader.
A wrong version beside the right one, with a prompt to say why it fails, works for this audience; a wrong version alone is the g = .136 case.

Comparison between two implementations has the one controlled programming result on the closest population.
Patitsas, Craig, and Easterbrook ran a double-blind study in a CS2 Python course (n = 241): students who saw linear and quadratic probing side by side with a compare-and-contrast prompt showed more procedural knowledge (p = .02) and more flexibility (p = .047) than students who saw them in sequence, with no difference in conceptual knowledge, and "the effect held in our second posttest" ([Patitsas et al. 2013](https://cs.mcgill.ca/~patitsas/publications/compare_contrast.pdf)).
The mathematics line it builds on sets the condition: comparing methods helped seventh graders who could attempt one method at pretest and overwhelmed those who could attempt neither, "two examples presented together are better than two examples presented separately," and comparing two problems rather than two methods for one problem did not help in five college experiments ([Rittle-Johnson, Star & Durkin 2017](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/t2-my/my-prd/wp-content/uploads/sites/3147/2020/01/RittleJohnson_Star_Durkin_2017_ComparisonChapter.pdf)).
For a solutions file this means a second implementation placed after the first, labeled, with a prompt that names the trade-off (when each applies, what each costs) rather than asking which is better; Codewars' split between "Best Practices" and "Clever" solutions is a ready pair of labels ([Codewars blog](https://blog.codewars.com/2014/solutions-tag-voting/)).
This book has natural pairs: a class-based against a closure-based *Strategy*, a *State* machine as classes against an enum with `match`, exceptions against a `Result` type, a function with hidden effects against its Stateless Effect.
A short criteria list ("a correct solution declares the base's `__slots__`, keeps the registry out of the instance, and rejects a duplicate key") gives the comparison scaffold that untutored learners lack; rubrics carry g = 0.45 on performance after correcting for publication bias, and the number of criteria is not a significant moderator, so three or four suffice ([Deusto repository record](https://deustoteka.deusto.es/items/59022074-df96-4c6a-8c52-3aa513c0ae3e)).

Two smaller devices close the solution.
Polya's "look back" is the phase learners skip: in a field study, 42.8% applied it against over 93% for each of the first three phases ([IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/apb/jahsss/2016p261-268.html)).
Ending a solution with a one-line look-back the text then answers (what else this shape solves, what input breaks it) supplies the step readers do not take unprompted.
And mastery learning's effect lives in the retest, not the first attempt: the feedback-corrective loop (formative test, correction, parallel retest) gave about 0.52 SD across 108 evaluations, with stronger effects for weaker students ([Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/81783373/Effectiveness_of_Mastery_Learning_Programs_A_Meta_Analysis)), while Slavin's synthesis found "essentially no evidence" on standardized measures, which says the loop improves what it tests ([Slavin 1987](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf)).
A solution that ends the episode gives the reader the feedback half and skips the corrective half; a second, smaller exercise on the same objective after the first solution is the parallel retest in static form, and it is also the reason to write exercises against named chapter objectives.

## Six exercise formats carry evidence for experienced programmers

Completion comes first, and the previous section covers it; the rule is one completion exercise per new idiom, blanking the lines that carry the idea, then write-from-spec.

Prediction is the second format, and it has the one adult randomized result in this set.
A 2024 Learning and Instruction study assigned 121 college students with no coding experience to predict a program's output before being taught, or to be taught and then write; "participants in the predict condition showed greater learning and better non-cognitive outcomes," including more positive responses to error messages ([index record](https://ubmr.hds.hebis.de/EdsRecord/ehh%252C176332717)).
The generation effect behind it is d = 0.40 across 86 verbal studies, largest for calculation-based generation rules ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/)), and "predict what this prints" is generation of that kind.
PRIMM, the school framework built on the same step, found a significant posttest advantage across 13 schools and 493 pupils (no effect size reported) and specifies that prediction "should be unassessed and low stakes" ([Glasgow eprints](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/229013); [PRIMM quick read](https://static.raspberrypi.org/files/curriculum/quickreads/11-Pedagogy_Summary_PRIMM_V4_2023.pdf)).
For this book the targets are the places where runtime behavior is new to a Python programmer: descriptor lookup, a generator's suspended frame, `match` on a class pattern, task scheduling, an Effect handler's dispatch.
Prediction also rescues a format with no evidence of its own.
"What breaks if you remove X" has nothing behind it beyond a 2023 position paper ([arXiv 2306.00551](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00551)); written as "remove the `__slots__` line; what does the next listing print?" it becomes a prediction, and the `#:` markers in every listing are the answer key.
A notional-machine exercise is the same move for state: the Fincher working group found "limited literature looking at the educational effectiveness" of notional machines and no study testing the idea, but its catalogue and cautions point one way, that machines should be constructed, not viewed, since stepping tools get used "mechanically" ([Fincher et al. 2020](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/76828957/SCI_Hellas_Notional_Machines_in_Computing_Education_The_Education_of_Attention.pdf)).
"Draw the frames at the moment the generator yields" is answerable on paper with a diagram in the solutions file.

Explain-in-plain-English and short tracing are the third format, with the oldest CER evidence and a known limit.
Lister's 12-institution study of 556 novices found quartile tracing scores of 89, 70, 50, and 24% and concluded the missing skills "relate more to the ability of students to read code than to write it" ([Lister et al. 2004](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/~j-hamer/ITiCSE04-wg.pdf)).
Venables, Tan, and Lister found tracing and explaining together account for **66%** of code-writing variance, that of students tracing above 50% and explaining both items correctly 83% wrote well while below 50% tracing none did, and that complex tracing predicted writing worse than simple tracing (R² = 0.27 against 0.51) ([Venables et al. 2009](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/11384/1/2009002706OK.pdf)).
The limit is that the hierarchy is a novice phenomenon: a 600-student replication found the Lopez hierarchy "did not emerge as a top candidate model" and that a snapshot "is not sufficient to determine teaching order" ([Fowler et al. 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10340072)), and a data-structures study reported the order reversed for CS2 students ([Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Tracing+vs.+Writing+Code+Beyond+the+Learning+Hierarchy+Harrington+Cheng&rows=2)).
For your reader, then: no long trace tables, a short trace of the one surprising step, and an explain prompt on any listing whose purpose is not visible from one line.
Fowler, Chen, and Zilles' interviews give the authoring checklist: the code should "have a purpose," its behavior "should not be guessable from one line," there is a size sweet spot, and the training device is "showing a multistructural answer beside a relational one" ([Fowler et al. 2021](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/how_to_eipe_icer_2021.pdf)).
That last point sets the shape of a model answer: the relational statement first ("checks whether the sequence is sorted"), the line-by-line account second, which is how SOLO separates readers ready to write from those who are not ([Lister et al. 2006](https://www.cs.auckland.ac.nz/courses/compsci747s2c/lectures/p118-lister.pdf)).

Comparison is the fourth, covered above as a solution device; as an exercise it reads "implement it the second way and state the trade-off."
Pattern selection is its cousin and is the question students demonstrably get wrong.
A third-year design-patterns course reported that "overlapping intent caused confusion (e.g., Adapter vs. Facade)," along with *Strategy* against *Decorator* and *Singleton* against *Factory Method*, and that "abstract intent, rather than implementation syntax, was the main obstacle" ([San Martin et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16770)); the study is small (n = 20) and uncontrolled, but the confusion it names is the one an exercise can target.
The interleaving meta-analysis says why that matters: across 59 studies interleaving carries g = 0.42 with large heterogeneity, and the advantage depends on the similarity of the categories being discriminated ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)).
A "which pattern applies" exercise should put the confusable pair in the options and its solution should name the discriminating feature.

Critique, refactoring, refute, and debugging form the fifth, and here the evidence comes from second-year software-engineering students, the closest population in the set.
Oliveira, Keuning, and Jeuring's think-aloud found students fixed redundant boolean expressions 95% of the time and redundant if-else 91%, but overlooked a suboptimal loop choice 76% of the time, failed to identify a god method in 12 of 20 cases, and when they merged nested ifs with `&&` four of them dropped the else; "the Unnecessary Code reasoning is also often used to motivate incorrect refactoring steps" ([Oliveira et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.20875)).
The earlier tutor study with 133 students found 92% of quality issues fixed, with trouble concentrated in control flow and composed expressions ([Keuning et al. 2020](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~jeuri101/homepage/Publications/StudentRefactoringBehaviour.pdf)).
Readers of this book will find a redundant `== True` unaided; a critique exercise earns its place on the faults they do not see (a class doing two jobs, a *Visitor* where `match` would do, duplicated branches, a mutable default) and should ask for the reason, since the wrong fixes came from wrong reasons.
Refute questions ("give an input that proves this code wrong") are defined with three design concerns, of which the hard one is that the wrong code must be a plausible solution, but they are a lightning talk with no learning study ([ICER 2021 profile](https://icer2021.acm.org/profile/virajkumar)).
Debugging instruction works when the steps are tied to the code: context-specific steps gave 80% bug-localization correctness at the first session against 20-44% for control and abstract guidelines, retained at 80% against 38% after three weeks, in 44 undergraduates with four years' programming on average ([Zhang et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.22420v1)).
A "find the bug" exercise should carry its own short procedure (which test isolates it, which value to inspect), not a general strategy.

Cumulative, interleaved exercises are the sixth.
Rohrer's preregistered cluster trial with 787 seventh graders raised scores on an unannounced test from 38% (blocked) to **61%** (interleaved), d = 0.83, with a positive effect for every teacher; every teacher also reported mixed worksheets took longer, and interleaving was tested after students had some grounding in each method ([WWC record](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770)).
Spacing is the best-supported technique of all: across 317 experiments spaced beat massed, and the optimal gap grows with the retention interval ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)); Dunlosky's review rates practice testing and distributed practice high utility and interleaving moderate ([APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)).
A book cannot schedule sessions but can space content.
One or two exercises per chapter that do not announce their technique and need a tool from an earlier chapter, placed after the chapter's own blocked exercises, restore the discrimination step blocked practice removes; a late exercise that revisits chapter 12's `@dataclass(frozen=True)` under chapter 36's pickling is worth more for retention than a second exercise in chapter 12.
Interleave what readers confuse (the structurally similar patterns), not arbitrary pairs.
A preparation-for-future-learning exercise is the same idea pointed forward: Schwartz and Bransford found a lecture useful when it follows relevant experience with the material ([Wright State PDF](https://www.wright.edu/sites/www.wright.edu/files/uploads/2017/Feb/event/Schwartz_Bransford_1998_TimeForTelling.pdf)), so an exercise whose solution the next chapter supersedes can say so, and the next chapter should then name it.

No study sets a number of exercises per concept.
The conventions in the best-controlled studies are three worked examples each followed by practice, two to five exposure exercises per pattern, and about five minutes per item ([Curious Case of Loops](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10171483); [Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)); Exercism sizes a concept exercise at 5-10 minutes for a seasoned developer and makes it target first use ([Exercism docs](https://exercism.org/docs/building/product/concept-exercises)); and many small programs (five or more a week) reduced stress and improved coding-exam performance in CS1 ([ASEE PEER](https://peer.asee.org/many-small-programs-in-cs1-usage-analysis-from-multiple-universities)).
Several short exercises beat one long one, and format calibrates better than count: predict and explain for anything new at runtime, complete before generate for a new idiom, compare once two implementations exist.

Four formats have evidence against them for this audience.
Reorder-the-lines Parsons puzzles showed no learning gain and were guessable for upper-division students ([Weinman 2022](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2022/EECS-2022-257.html)).
Distractor lines "increased learners' cognitive load, decreased their success at completing code puzzles by 26%, and also increased their time on task by 14%" with no evidence of learning ([Harms 2016 slides](https://kharms.infosci.cornell.edu/downloads/harmsk-icer-2016-slides.pdf)), raised exam time 23-37% without improving discrimination ([Smith & Zilles 2023](https://zilles.web.engr.illinois.edu/papers/smith_parsons_sigcse23.pdf)), and Dale Parsons herself says "nobody could ever prove that they were useful" ([Parsons Problems and Beyond](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/131069230/Parsons_Problems_and_Beyond_-_Systematic_Literature_Review_and_Empirical_Study_Designs_.pdf)).
Labeling exercises by Bloom level is unreliable: "Bloom's Taxonomy is difficult to apply consistently to assessment tasks in introductory programming courses" ([DigitalNZ record](https://digitalnz.org/records/30158531)).
And "reflect on how you approached this" prompts are the metacognitive kind Bisra found null, where "compare your solution to this one and list the differences" is the conceptual kind that works.

## Platforms converge on post-solve comparison; print authors on hints before outlines

The practice of well-regarded books and platforms is almost entirely untested, and the report-worthy fact is which practices line up with a mechanism that is.
Among print authors who state a reason, the reasons converge on Knuth's: "we all learn best the things that we have discovered for ourselves," and the exercises "form a major part of this work" ([U. Oregon quotes page](https://classes.cs.uoregon.edu/10W/cis610tes/quotes5.html)).
Knuth's 0-to-50 rating scale is the one print device that tells a reader how much effort an exercise deserves before starting, maintained across printings so a solved research problem is re-rated ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_Computer_Programming)); it has no learning study, and the Bloom result above says not to label by cognitive level, but a rating by effort is a different claim and a cheap one.
Bentley's hints-as-questions followed by solution outlines is the closest print precedent for a ladder ([Linux Journal review](https://www.linuxjournal.com/node/3846)).
How to Design Programs' six-step recipe includes a "Function Template" step before "Function Definition," which is the shape rung as an authoring discipline rather than a reveal ([HtDP 2e preface](https://htdp.org/2024-11-6/Book/part_preface.html)).
Learn Python the Hard Way's "Common Student Questions" under each exercise is the print form of the contrast paragraph, and its "Study Drills" the print form of the extension ([InformIT listing](https://www.informit.com/store/learn-python-the-hard-way-a-very-simple-introduction-9780133124354)).

The platforms converged on one device after launch: show other approaches once the learner has solved it.
Rustlings 6 reveals a solution file after an exercise passes so you can "compare your solution with an idiomatic solution and maybe learn about other ways to solve a problem," and its changelog records that "while writing the solutions, all exercises have been polished" ([rustlings CHANGELOG](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rust-lang/rustlings/main/CHANGELOG.md)).
Exercism's Dig Deeper tab exists because of 500 recent solutions to one exercise "over 350 of them were unique," and it curates approaches with articles on tradeoffs ([Exercism blog](https://exercism.org/blog/dig-deeper)); its mentors are told to describe alternatives rather than hand over "the (better) solution," with a solution "optionally available" in a collapsible section ([Exercism mentoring mindset](https://exercism.org/docs/mentoring/mindset)).
Codewars sends a solver straight to the solutions page for an "Aha! moment when you realize how others have completed the same task" ([Codewars docs](https://docs.codewars.com/concepts/kata/solutions/)).
Project Euler frames its problems as "inductive chain learning" and opens a discussion thread after a correct answer ([Project Euler](https://projecteuler.net/about)).
Advent of Code's second part reuses the input with "some kind of twist or change to the first puzzle's requirements," meant to mirror real development ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2021/12/17/1042483/puzzle-challenge-coding-christmas/)).
None of these carries a controlled study, but the first three are the static-page half of the comparison result Patitsas measured, and Downey's retrospective that his second edition's exercises "were uneven" until he wrote and tested them in notebooks ([Think Python 3e preface](https://allendowney.github.io/ThinkPython/chap00.html)) agrees with Rustlings: a book whose every solution is written is a book whose exercises have been checked.
Price and colleagues' hint-generation result backs the hand-written canonical solution over any crowd-derived one: "a comprehensive set of expert solutions generally performs best" ([NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10065912)).

The one format question the platforms answer that the ladder cannot is the checker.
Four platforms gate the reveal behind a correct submission, and Ruby Koans distributes tests as the specification ("see it fail, make the test pass, then reflect") as plain files ([rubykoans.com](https://www.rubykoans.com)).
A static book cannot run a checker, but this book has two things a reader can run without one: the `#:` markers that state what a listing prints, and a type checker.
An exercise that prints the test its solution must pass, or the `ty` diagnostic the reader's first attempt will draw and must clear, is the Koans device in print; this is an inference from the platform practice, with no study behind it.

The medium splits the ladder.
On the site, the `<details>` ladder is the design; in the EPUB and PDF the Solutions folder is a link, so a print reader gets the Bentley form, hints and solutions in a separate place, and the shape rung appears on the site alone.
That is the right compromise given Rittle-Johnson's delayed-comparison null: better one page with every rung than rungs scattered across formats.

## Conclusion

The ladder's evidence is concentrated in two places: the attempt-first ordering it enforces by a click, and the completion problem at rung two.
The marginal gains lie in the text of rung three, not in more rungs or cleverer hints.
A full solution that is a bare listing invites the recognition that Koriat and Bjork showed inflates confidence, and a full solution that is a listing divided by function-naming labels, opened by a contrast with the attempt the reader likely made, followed by a labeled second implementation with its trade-off and closed by a look-back line, is the form the hint, explanation, comparison, and productive-failure literatures each point at from their own side.
Because the shape rung is generated from the solution, a labeling convention in the solution can propagate to the shape, so the device costs one authoring rule and no new tooling.

The strongest caution is about population.
The one direct test of a novice-proven format on upper-division students went null, the tracing hierarchy did not replicate cleanly at scale, and no study measures a hint-skeleton-solution ladder as a unit.
The formats whose evidence comes from CS2 or second-year students (side-by-side comparison, structural critique, completion with the idea blanked) deserve more weight for this book than those proven on CS1 novices, and the reader's own expertise shifts within a chapter, so the right fade is one completion exercise per idiom, then write-from-spec, then a comparison once two implementations exist.
Treat the design as a hypothesis with good priors, and treat a device's absence from the literature (Knuth's ratings, part-two twists, a printed `ty` diagnostic as the specification) as untested rather than disproved.
